Lecture 45

Representing local heat sources with the Dirac delta

Heat is often released in a region far smaller than the body: a fission fragment track, a laser focus, a resistance wire, a welding arc, a chemical reaction front. Modelling the region in detail is unnecessary when we only care about the temperature some distance away. We idealise it as a point, line, plane, cylindrical shell or spherical shell, and release its energy either instantaneously (all at once, in J) or continuously (at a rate, in W).

The mathematical tool is the Dirac delta, defined by what it does inside an integral:

$$\delta(x - a) = 0\ \ (x\ne a),\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x - a)\,dx = 1,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)\,\delta(x - a)\,dx = F(a)\quad(\text{sifting})$$

Matching units with the volumetric source \(\dot q\) (W/m³) gives the representations:

SourceInstantaneous (released at \(t = \tau\))Continuous
Point at \(\mathbf r'\) (Cartesian)\(\dot q = Q_{pt}\,\delta(x-x')\delta(y-y')\delta(z-z')\,\delta(t-\tau)\); \(Q_{pt}\) in J\(\dot q_{pt}(t)\,\delta(x-x')\delta(y-y')\delta(z-z')\); W
Plane at \(x = x'\)\(Q_{pl}\,\delta(x - x')\,\delta(t - \tau)\); J/m²\(\dot q_{pl}(t)\,\delta(x - x')\); W/m²
Line along \(z\) at \((x',y')\)\(Q_{ln}\,\delta(x-x')\delta(y-y')\delta(t-\tau)\); J/m\(\dot q_{ln}\,\delta(x-x')\delta(y-y')\); W/m
Cylindrical shell at \(r = r'\)\(\dfrac{Q_{cyl}}{2\pi r'}\delta(r - r')\delta(t-\tau)\); J/m\(\dfrac{\dot q_{cyl}}{2\pi r'}\delta(r - r')\); W/m
Spherical shell at \(r = r'\)\(\dfrac{Q_{sph}}{4\pi r'^2}\delta(r - r')\delta(t-\tau)\); J\(\dfrac{\dot q_{sph}}{4\pi r'^2}\delta(r - r')\); W

The \(1/2\pi r'\) and \(1/4\pi r'^2\) factors make the integral over the shell's area equal the total strength. Integrating a delta over a coordinate is where those geometric factors come from.

Lecture 46

A slab with local heat sources

A slab \(0\le x\le L\) at \(T_i\), with both faces held at \(T_i\), has a volumetric source \(\dot q(x,t)\). Eigenfunctions \(\sin\lambda_nx\), \(\lambda_n = n\pi/L\), and the transform or Green's-function method give

$$T - T_i = \frac{2\alpha}{kL}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\sin\lambda_nx\int_0^te^{-\alpha\lambda_n^2(t-\tau)}\int_0^L\dot q(x',\tau)\sin\lambda_nx'\,dx'\,d\tau$$

Insert the delta representations and the integrals collapse:

Plane source at x = a in a slab$$\begin{aligned} \text{instantaneous } Q_{pl}:\quad & T - T_i = \frac{2Q_{pl}}{\rho cL}\sum_n\sin\lambda_na\,\sin\lambda_nx\,e^{-\alpha\lambda_n^2t}\\ \text{continuous } \dot q_{pl}:\quad & T - T_i = \frac{2\dot q_{pl}}{kL}\sum_n\frac{\sin\lambda_na\,\sin\lambda_nx}{\lambda_n^2}\left(1 - e^{-\alpha\lambda_n^2t}\right) \end{aligned}$$

As \(t\to\infty\), the continuous case tends to the triangular steady profile with apex \(\dot q_{pl}\,a(L-a)/(kL)\) at the source. An instantaneous volumetric release \(Q_i\) (J/m³) through the whole slab is just a uniform initial temperature rise \(Q_i/\rho c\), which then decays.

Physical interpretation

Look at \(\sin\lambda_na\) in each term. A source at the slab centre excites only the odd modes, because the even modes have a node there. A source near a wall couples weakly to every mode, so most of its heat escapes through the nearby face. The fraction of steady-state heat leaving through each face is \((L - a)/L\) and \(a/L\), the same lever rule as a weight on a beam.

Lectures 47–48

Local sources in cylinders and spheres

For a long solid cylinder \(0\le r\le b\) with its surface at \(T_i\), the eigenfunctions are \(J_0(\beta_mr)\) with \(J_0(\beta_mb) = 0\). A continuous line source \(\dot q_{ln}\) (W/m) on the axis gives

$$T - T_i = \frac{\dot q_{ln}}{\pi kb^2}\sum_m\frac{J_0(\beta_mr)}{\beta_m^2J_1^2(\beta_mb)}\left(1 - e^{-\alpha\beta_m^2t}\right)\ \xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\ \frac{\dot q_{ln}}{2\pi k}\ln\frac{b}{r}$$

A cylindrical-shell source at \(r = r'\) replaces \(J_0(0) = 1\) with \(J_0(\beta_mr')\). For a solid sphere \(0\le r\le b\) with a point source at the centre, the eigenfunctions are \(\sin(\beta_mr)/r\) with \(\beta_m = m\pi/b\). The steady limit is \(\frac{\dot q_{pt}}{4\pi k}\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{b}\right)\). The \(\ln(1/r)\) and \(1/r\) singularities are real features of the idealisation. A real source has a finite size, and its temperature is bounded.

In the real world

A nuclear fuel pin is a line source inside cladding. An implanted medical device or a buried power cable is a line source in tissue or soil. A sphere with a point source models a spherical nuclear fuel particle (TRISO) or a reaction at the centre of a catalyst pellet. Superposing shells of different radii gives any radial source distribution, which is the Green's-function method of Module 7 in action.

Lecture 49

Point sources in an infinite region, and moving sources

In an unbounded body the Green's function of Module 7 gives the classical results directly:

SourceInstantaneousContinuous from \(t=0\)
Point\(\dfrac{Q}{\rho c(4\pi\alpha t)^{3/2}}e^{-r^2/4\alpha t}\)\(\dfrac{\dot q}{4\pi kr}\operatorname{erfc}\dfrac{r}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\ \to\ \dfrac{\dot q}{4\pi kr}\)
Line\(\dfrac{Q'}{4\pi kt}e^{-r^2/4\alpha t}\)\(\dfrac{\dot q'}{4\pi k}E_1\!\left(\dfrac{r^2}{4\alpha t}\right)\approx\dfrac{\dot q'}{4\pi k}\left[\ln\dfrac{4\alpha t}{r^2} - 0.5772\right]\)
Plane\(\dfrac{Q''}{\rho c\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}e^{-x^2/4\alpha t}\)\(\dfrac{\dot q''\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k}\,2\operatorname{ierfc}\dfrac{|x|}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\)

The continuous line source never reaches a steady state. Its temperature grows like \(\ln t\), the 2D borderline case from Module 5. That growth is turned into a measurement technique below.

Moving heat sources

A heat source moving at constant velocity \(u\) along \(x\) (a welding arc, a laser scan, a grinding wheel, a friction stir tool) eventually carries a steady temperature field along with it. In coordinates attached to the source, \(\xi = x - ut\), the heat equation becomes steady with an advection term:

$$\nabla^2T + \frac{u}{\alpha}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\xi} = 0\quad(\text{quasi-stationary state})$$

The substitution \(T - T_0 = e^{-u\xi/2\alpha}\psi\) reduces this to \(\nabla^2\psi - (u/2\alpha)^2\psi = 0\). Its point-source solution is Rosenthal's (1941) result for welding a thick plate, with surface losses neglected:

Rosenthal’s solutions$$\text{thick plate (3D):}\quad T - T_0 = \frac{\dot q}{2\pi kR}\exp\!\left[-\frac{u(\xi + R)}{2\alpha}\right];\qquad \text{thin plate (2D):}\quad T - T_0 = \frac{\dot q}{2\pi kd}\,e^{-u\xi/2\alpha}K_0\!\left(\frac{ur}{2\alpha}\right)$$

Here \(R = \sqrt{\xi^2 + y^2 + z^2}\) and \(r = \sqrt{\xi^2 + y^2}\). The factor 2π instead of 4π in the 3D case accounts for the source sitting on the surface of a half-space, where all the heat goes into the plate. A moving plane source gives \(T - T_0 = \dot q''/\rho cu\) behind the source and \((\dot q''/\rho cu)e^{-u\xi/\alpha}\) ahead of it.

Physical interpretation: the Péclet number

\(\mathrm{Pe} = uR/2\alpha\) compares how fast the source moves with how fast heat diffuses ahead of it. At low Pe the isotherms are nearly circular. At high Pe the heat cannot outrun the source: isotherms crowd in front, stretch into a long tail behind, and the melt pool becomes a teardrop. Fast welds on high-diffusivity aluminium look like slow welds on low-diffusivity steel. The pool width then grows only as \(\sqrt{\dot q/u}\), which is why welders set heat input per unit length \(\dot q/u\) rather than power alone.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import exp1, k0

# 1) Transient hot-wire: dT = q'/(4 pi k) [ln(4 alpha t / r^2) - gamma]
q1, r, k_true, alpha = 2.0, 50e-6, 0.6, 1.4e-7        # W/m, wire radius, water
t = np.logspace(-1, 1, 60)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
dT = q1/(4*np.pi*k_true)*exp1(r**2/(4*alpha*t)) + rng.normal(0, 0.003, t.size)
win = (t > 0.5) & (t < 8)                                  # linear (log-time) window
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(t[win]), dT[win], 1)[0]
print(f"fitted slope = {slope:.4f} K  ->  k = q'/(4 pi slope) = {q1/(4*np.pi*slope):.4f} W/m K (true {k_true})")

# 2) Rosenthal thick-plate melt pool for steel
q, u = 2500.0, 5e-3                                       # W (absorbed), m/s
k, a, T0, Tm = 30.0, 6e-6, 25.0, 1500.0
xi = np.linspace(-0.04, 0.01, 2001); y = np.linspace(0, 0.01, 801)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xi, y); R = np.hypot(X, Y) + 1e-9
T = T0 + q/(2*np.pi*k*R)*np.exp(-u*(X + R)/(2*a))
mask = T >= Tm
print(f"melt pool length = {np.ptp(X[mask])*1e3:.2f} mm, half-width = {Y[mask].max()*1e3:.2f} mm")
print(f"Peclet number (uL/2a) on pool length: {u*np.ptp(X[mask])/(2*a):.1f}")
// Continuous point source in an infinite medium: temperature history at several radii
const q = 1.0, k = 0.6, a = 1.4e-7;                     // 1 W in water
const t = M.linspace(1, 600, 200), radii = [2e-3, 4e-3, 8e-3];
plot(t, radii.map(r => t.map(tt => q/(4*Math.PI*k*r)*M.erfc(r/(2*Math.sqrt(a*tt))))),
     {xlabel:'t (s)', ylabel:'ΔT (K)', labels: radii.map(r => 'r = '+(r*1000)+' mm')});
radii.forEach(r => print("steady ΔT at", r*1000, "mm =", q/(4*Math.PI*k*r), "K"));
