Lecture 55

An introduction to porous media

A porous medium is a solid matrix laced with interconnected voids. Sand, sandstone, limestone, wood and lung tissue are natural examples. Ceramics, sintered metals, composites and high-porosity metal foams are man-made. When the pores are filled with a single fluid, gas or liquid, the medium is saturated; when gas and liquid coexist with the solid, it is unsaturated. Natural media rarely exceed 60% porosity. Metal foams can approach 97–98%.

Because the pores give a huge surface area per unit volume, heat transfer in porous media is a volumetric process rather than a surface one. That is exploited in transpiration cooling of rocket throats and turbine blades (coolant bled through a porous wall), microchannel heat sinks for electronics and diode lasers, packed-bed reactors, fuel cells, geothermal reservoirs, and in perfused living tissue.

Porosity and the representative elementary volume

$$\phi = \frac{V_{\text{void}}}{V_{\text{total}}},\qquad 0\le\phi\le1,\qquad \text{solidity} = 1 - \phi$$

Measure \(\phi\) in a tiny sample and you get 0 or 1, depending on whether you landed in a grain or a pore. As the sample grows, the measured value fluctuates and then settles to a stable mean. The smallest sample size beyond which it stays within a tolerance (say ±2%) is the representative elementary volume (REV). Above the REV scale we can treat the medium as a continuum with averaged properties, the same continuum hypothesis as in Module 1, applied one level up.

Volume-averaged quantities are defined over the REV. For flow, the superficial (Darcy) velocity \(\mathbf u = \phi\,\langle\mathbf v\rangle_f\) obeys Darcy's law, \(\mathbf u = -\frac{K}{\mu}\nabla p\), with permeability \(K\) in m².

Lecture 56

The heat conduction equation for porous media

Local thermal equilibrium (LTE). If solid and fluid exchange heat fast compared with the macroscopic processes, they share one temperature \(T\) at each point of the REV. This holds for small pores and slow flows: geothermal systems, insulation, most packed beds. It fails in fast transients, with large pores, or with strong internal heating (reactor cores, electronic packages), where separate solid and fluid temperatures (a two-equation, LTNE model) are needed.

Under LTE, averaging the solid and fluid energy equations over the REV gives one equation:

Porous-medium energy equation (LTE)$$(\rho c)_m\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + (\rho c_p)_f\,\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T = \nabla\cdot(k_m\nabla T) + \dot q_m,\qquad (\rho c)_m = (1-\phi)(\rho c)_s + \phi(\rho c_p)_f$$

For a 1D flow \(\dot m\) through area \(A\), this reads \(\frac{1}{\alpha_m}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\dot mc_{pf}}{Ak_m}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + \frac{\dot q}{k_m}\). With a stagnant fluid it reduces to the ordinary heat equation with averaged properties. For radial flow through a porous cylinder or sphere, the advection term becomes \(\frac{\dot mc_{pf}}{2\pi k L}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\) or \(\beta\frac{r_0}{r^2}\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\) with \(\beta = \dot mc_{pf}/4\pi kr_0\).

Effective conductivity

\(k_m\) depends on how the phases are arranged, not only on their fractions. Two arrangements bound every possibility:

$$\underbrace{k_\parallel = (1-\phi)k_s + \phi k_f}_{\text{layers parallel to heat flow (upper bound)}}\qquad\qquad \underbrace{\frac{1}{k_\perp} = \frac{1-\phi}{k_s} + \frac{\phi}{k_f}}_{\text{layers in series (lower bound)}}$$

Tighter bounds and practical models, such as Maxwell–Eucken (isolated inclusions in a continuous phase), the geometric mean \(k_s^{1-\phi}k_f^\phi\), and foam correlations of the form \(k_m\approx\frac{1}{3}(1-\phi)k_s\) for open-cell metal foams, lie between them.

Physical interpretation

When \(k_s\gg k_f\), what matters is whether the solid forms continuous paths. A metal foam at 92% porosity conducts far better than a bed of metal spheres at the same porosity, because its struts are connected while the spheres touch only at points. That is why open-cell metal foams make good heat-exchanger and slosh-baffle materials even when mostly empty space. Their effective conductivity follows the connected-strut scaling \((1-\phi)k_s/3\): a third of the parallel bound, because on average only one of three strut orientations is aligned with the heat flow.

Lecture 57

Examples: transpiration cooling and living tissue

Transpiration-cooled porous plate

A porous plate of thickness \(L\) is fed with coolant at \(\dot m\) from a reservoir at \(T_0\) through the face \(x = 0\). The face \(x = L\) is heated by convection from a hot gas at \(T_\infty\) with coefficient \(h\). Steady state, LTE, constant properties:

$$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} - \frac{\beta}{L}\frac{dT}{dx} = 0,\quad \beta = \frac{\dot mc_{pf}L}{Ak};\qquad T_0 - T(0) = -\frac{L}{\beta}\frac{dT}{dx}\Big|_0,\quad -k_s\frac{dT}{dx}\Big|_L = h\,(T(L) - T_\infty)$$

The inlet condition says the coolant arrives at \(T_0\) and is pre-heated by conduction up the plate before entering it. The solution is

Transpiration cooling$$\frac{T(x) - T_0}{T_\infty - T_0} = \frac{\mathrm{Bi}}{\mathrm{Bi} + \beta}\exp\!\left[\beta\left(\frac{x}{L} - 1\right)\right],\qquad \mathrm{Bi} = \frac{hL}{k_s};\qquad \frac{\dot m}{A} = \frac{kh\,(T_\infty - T_d)}{k_sc_{pf}\,(T_d - T_0)}$$

The last expression is the coolant flux needed to hold the hot face at a design temperature \(T_d\). The limits check out. With \(h = 0\), the plate sits at \(T_0\). With \(h\to\infty\), the hot face reaches \(T_\infty\). With infinite flow, everything is at \(T_0\). With no flow, the inlet face is effectively insulated and the plate rises to \(T_\infty\).

Physical interpretation

The coolant does two jobs. It absorbs heat volumetrically as it percolates, and it pushes the hot thermal layer back against the hot face, producing the exponential boundary layer of thickness \(L/\beta\). That is why a modest flow keeps most of the plate near coolant temperature, with only a thin hot skin at \(x = L\). The same advection–diffusion balance governs ablation cooling and the thermal boundary layer in a moving solid (Module 1).

Steady conduction in living tissue

Capillary perfusion can be modelled as blood flowing through a porous tissue matrix of thickness \(\delta\) with metabolic generation \(\dot q\). Blood enters at \(T_1\) and leaves at \(T_2\):

$$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} - \frac{\beta}{\delta}\frac{dT}{dx} + \frac{\dot q}{k} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ T(x) = T_1 + \frac{\dot q\delta}{k\beta}x + \left(T_2 - T_1 - \frac{\dot q\delta^2}{k\beta}\right)\frac{e^{\beta x/\delta} - 1}{e^\beta - 1},\quad \beta = \frac{\dot mc_{pb}\delta}{Ak}$$

Choose "living tissue" in the simulation to explore it. This advective form is a close cousin of the Pennes bio-heat equation, where perfusion acts as a distributed heat sink \(\omega_b(\rho c)_b(T_a - T)\) rather than a directed flow.

Lectures 58–59

Heat conduction in anisotropic solids

Wood, quartz and graphite crystals, laminated composites, wound transformer cores, MLI blankets and rolled metals conduct heat differently in different directions. Fourier's law then involves a conductivity tensor:

Anisotropic Fourier law and heat equation$$q''_i = -\sum_{j=1}^3k_{ij}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_j},\qquad \rho c\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \sum_i\sum_jk_{ij}\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x_i\partial x_j} + \dot q$$

By Onsager's reciprocity, \(k_{ij} = k_{ji}\), so the tensor is symmetric. Crystal symmetry restricts it further. Cubic crystals are isotropic (\(k_{11}\mathbf I\)). Orthorhombic crystals are diagonal with three different values. Monoclinic and triclinic crystals have off-diagonal terms. Several consequences surprise people:

  • The heat flux is generally not parallel to \(-\nabla T\), and not normal to the isotherms.
  • With \(T = T(x_1,x_2)\) only, there can still be a flux \(q''_3 = -k_{31}\partial_1T - k_{32}\partial_2T\) in the direction with no gradient.
  • Boundary conditions use the normal component of the tensor flux: \(-\sum_{ij}n_ik_{ij}\partial_jT = h(T - T_\infty)\) on a convective face.

Principal axes and transformation to an isotropic problem

A symmetric tensor has three real eigenvalues, the principal conductivities \(k_1, k_2, k_3\), and orthogonal eigenvectors, the principal axes. They solve \(\det(k_{ij} - \lambda\delta_{ij}) = 0\). Rotate to the principal axes and the cross terms vanish: \(k_1\partial_1^2T + k_2\partial_2^2T + k_3\partial_3^2T + \dot q = \rho c\,\partial_tT\). Now stretch each axis:

$$X_i = \xi_i\sqrt{\frac{\bar k}{k_i}},\qquad \bar k = (k_1k_2k_3)^{1/3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar k\nabla_X^2T + \dot q = \rho c\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}$$

Choosing \(\bar k\) as the geometric mean keeps volumes, and therefore \(\rho c\) and \(\dot q\), unchanged. Every isotropic solution in this course becomes an anisotropic one by this change of variables. A circle maps to an ellipse, and a point source's spherical isotherms become ellipsoids.

In the real world

Carbon-fibre laminates can have in-plane to through-thickness conductivity ratios of 10 to 100. Pyrolytic graphite heat spreaders exceed 300. MLI cryogenic blankets conduct about a thousand times better along the layers than across them, which is why seams and penetrations, not the blanket itself, dominate heat leak. Lithium-ion cells conduct heat 10 to 30 times faster along their electrode layers than across, which drives battery-pack cooling design.
