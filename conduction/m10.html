Approximate & Integral Methods

Lecture 50

Introduction to the integral method

Exact solutions are precious but fragile. A convective boundary, a variable conductivity or a moving front can make them unavailable or very hard to use. The heat-balance integral method (HBIM), introduced by Goodman in 1958, gives approximate closed-form answers that are typically within a few percent, with a page of algebra.

The idea is to satisfy the heat equation only on average over the region where things are happening, rather than at every point. In a semi-infinite body, that region is a finite thermal layer \(0\le x\le\delta(t)\). Beyond it the body is assumed undisturbed, \(T = T_i\) and \(\partial T/\partial x = 0\).

The four steps
  1. Integrate the PDE over \(0\le x\le\delta(t)\), using Leibniz's rule, to get the energy integral equation: \(\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^\delta(T - T_i)\,dx = -\alpha\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\Big|_{x=0}\).
  2. Choose a profile, usually a polynomial of degree 2 to 4 in \(x/\delta\), and fix its coefficients from the boundary conditions at \(x = 0\) and \(x = \delta\) (\(T = T_i\), \(\partial_xT = 0\), and so on).
  3. Substitute the profile to get an ODE for \(\delta(t)\). Solve it with \(\delta(0) = 0\).
  4. Put \(\delta(t)\) back into the profile to get \(T(x,t)\) and the surface flux.

The approximation lies in the choice of profile. Higher degree does not automatically mean higher accuracy, as the simulation below demonstrates.

Lecture 51

Integral method for a semi-infinite medium

A surface temperature step \(T_s\) is applied to a body at \(T_i\), with \(\theta = (T - T_i)/(T_s - T_i)\). With the quadratic profile \(\theta = (1 - x/\delta)^2\) (it satisfies \(\theta(0) = 1\), \(\theta(\delta) = 0\), \(\theta'(\delta) = 0\)):

$$\int_0^\delta\theta\,dx = \frac{\delta}{3},\quad -\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\Big|_0 = \frac{2}{\delta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{3}\frac{d\delta}{dt} = \frac{2\alpha}{\delta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta = \sqrt{12\alpha t},\qquad q''_s = \frac{k(T_s - T_i)}{\sqrt{3\alpha t}}$$

The exact flux is \(k(T_s - T_i)/\sqrt{\pi\alpha t}\). The quadratic profile is high by \(\sqrt{\pi/3} - 1 = 2.3\%\). A cubic profile \(\theta = 1 - \frac32\frac{x}{\delta} + \frac12\frac{x^3}{\delta^3}\), which adds \(\theta''(0) = 0\) from the PDE at the wall, gives \(\delta = \sqrt{8\alpha t}\) and a flux 6% low. For the family \(\theta = (1 - x/\delta)^n\), the method gives

Power-law profiles$$\delta = \sqrt{2n(n+1)\alpha t},\qquad \frac{q''_{\text{HBIM}}}{q''_{\text{exact}}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2(n+1)}},\qquad \text{exact flux when } n = \frac{2}{\pi - 2}\approx 1.75$$
Physical interpretation

The integral method conserves energy exactly. The total heat absorbed is correct for whatever profile shape you assume. What it cannot get exactly right is how that energy is distributed in depth. The real erfc profile has an infinitely long tail, but the polynomial must reach zero at \(\delta\) with zero slope. So \(\delta\) is a bit larger than the "4√(αt)" visual edge, and the flux, which depends on the slope at the wall, carries most of the error. When you need wall flux, choose the profile to match the wall behaviour; when you need penetration depth, match the far behaviour.

Lecture 52

Temperature-dependent properties

Where the method really pays off is nonlinear problems. With \(k = k(T)\) and constant \(\rho c\), apply the Kirchhoff transform \(U = \int_{T_i}^{T}k(T')dT'\). The energy integral equation becomes

$$\rho c\,\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^\delta(T - T_i)\,dx = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Big|_{x=0}$$

Assume the quadratic profile for \(U\), \(U = U_s(1 - x/\delta)^2\), and recover \(T\) from \(U\) pointwise. For \(k = k_0(1 + \beta\theta)\) with \(\theta\in[0,1]\), \(U/k_0\Delta T = \theta + \beta\theta^2/2\). The integral can be evaluated in closed form and gives \(\delta^2 = 12\alpha_0t\,(1 + \beta/2)\,f(\beta)\), with \(f\to1\) as \(\beta\to0\). The code at the end of this module carries out the calculation numerically and compares it with a finite-difference solution. For \(|\beta|\le1\) the agreement is within about 3% in wall flux.

Cryogenic angle

Below about 50 K, the conductivity of metals and of many insulators changes by an order of magnitude over the temperature range of a single cool-down. A transient cool-down calculation that uses one value of \(k\) is badly wrong. Kirchhoff plus integral method gives a quick, physically transparent estimate of how fast a cold front propagates into a support strut or a thick-walled cold mass, before committing to a full numerical model.

Lecture 53

Integral method for phase change

Return to Stefan's single-region melting problem from Module 8: liquid in \(0\lt x\lt s(t)\) with \(\theta = (T - T_f)/(T_0 - T_f)\), the face at \(\theta = 1\), and the solid at \(T_f\). Assume a quadratic profile in \(\xi = (s - x)/s\):

$$\theta = a\,\xi + b\,\xi^2,\qquad a + b = 1$$

A second condition comes from differentiating \(\theta(s(t),t) = 0\) in time and using the PDE and the Stefan condition \(\dot s = -\alpha\,\mathrm{Ste}\,\theta_x(s)\). This gives \(\theta_{xx}(s) = \mathrm{Ste}\,\theta_x^2(s)\), so \(2b = \mathrm{Ste}\,a^2\):

$$a = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 2\,\mathrm{Ste}} - 1}{\mathrm{Ste}},\qquad b = 1 - a$$

The energy integral, with the Stefan condition used for the flux at the front, is \(\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^s\theta\,dx = -\frac{\dot s}{\mathrm{Ste}} - \alpha\theta_x(0)\). With \(\int_0^s\theta\,dx = s\,(a/2 + b/3)\) and \(-\theta_x(0) = (a + 2b)/s\):

HBIM melting rate$$s(t) = 2\lambda_{\text{HBIM}}\sqrt{\alpha t},\qquad \lambda^2_{\text{HBIM}} = \frac{a + 2b}{2\,(a/2 + b/3 + 1/\mathrm{Ste})}$$

For small Ste this reduces to \(\lambda^2\to\mathrm{Ste}/2\), the quasi-steady result. At Ste = 1 it overpredicts \(s\) by about 6%. The method extends easily to finite slabs, convective faces and cylinders, where no exact solution exists.

Lecture 54

Finite regions and the method of weighted residuals

Integral method in a finite slab: two stages

For a slab of thickness \(L\), insulated at \(x = L\), the thermal layer reaches the back face at \(t_1 = L^2/12\alpha\) (quadratic profile). After that, a second stage takes over, with the profile \(\theta = 1 - (1 - \theta_L(t))\left(2\frac{x}{L} - \frac{x^2}{L^2}\right)\) and the back-face temperature \(\theta_L(t)\) as the unknown. The energy integral gives \(\frac{2L}{3}\dot\theta_L = \frac{2\alpha}{L}(1 - \theta_L)\), so

$$1 - \theta_L(t) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{3\alpha(t - t_1)}{L^2}\right],\qquad t\ge t_1 = \frac{L^2}{12\alpha}$$

The exact one-term decay rate is \((\pi/2)^2 = 2.47\) instead of 3: close enough for estimates, and obtained in three lines. Toggle "finite slab" in the first simulation to compare.

Method of weighted residuals (MWR)

The integral method is one member of a larger family. Write an approximate solution with free coefficients, \(\tilde T = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^Nc_i\phi_i\), where each trial function satisfies the boundary conditions. Substitute it into the governing equation to get a residual \(R(x; c_i)\), which would be zero for the exact solution. Then force \(N\) weighted averages of \(R\) to vanish:

$$\int_0^LR(x;c)\,w_j(x)\,dx = 0,\qquad j = 1,\dots,N$$
MethodWeights \(w_j\)Character
Collocation\(\delta(x - x_j)\)Residual exactly zero at chosen points; cheapest
Subdomain1 on the \(j\)-th sub-interval, else 0Conservation on each piece; the heat-balance integral is the case \(N = 1\)
Galerkin\(\phi_j\) (the trial functions themselves)Residual orthogonal to the trial space; the basis of the finite element method
Least squares\(\partial R/\partial c_j\)Minimises \(\int R^2dx\); always symmetric positive definite
Physical interpretation

Each method asks a different question of the residual. Collocation insists on perfection at a few points. Subdomain insists on energy balance over each patch. Galerkin insists that the error has no component the trial functions could have described. For this fin problem, all four converge rapidly. Galerkin and least squares give the best heat rate for a given N, because they control the error in an integral, energy-like sense. This is why Galerkin's method, applied to piecewise polynomials, became the finite element method used in every commercial thermal solver.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erf, erfc
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# 1) Power-law heat-balance integral vs exact surface flux
for n in (1, 1.75, 2, 3, 4):
    print(f"n = {n:4}: delta = sqrt({2*n*(n+1):.2f} alpha t),  q_HBIM/q_exact = {np.sqrt(np.pi*n/(2*(n+1))):.4f}")

# 2) Phase change: HBIM vs Stefan exact
for Ste in (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    a = (np.sqrt(1 + 2*Ste) - 1)/Ste; b = 1 - a
    lam_h = np.sqrt((a + 2*b)/(2*(a/2 + b/3 + 1/Ste)))
    lam_e = brentq(lambda l: l*np.exp(l*l)*erf(l) - Ste/np.sqrt(np.pi), 1e-9, 5)
    print(f"Ste = {Ste:3}: lambda exact {lam_e:.4f}, HBIM {lam_h:.4f}  ({(lam_h/lam_e-1)*100:+.1f} %)")

# 3) Galerkin for the fin  θ'' - m²θ = 0, θ(0)=1, θ'(1)=0 with φ_i = x^(i+1) - (i+1)x
m, N = 2.0, 3
xq = np.linspace(0, 1, 2001)
phi  = [xq**(i+1) - (i+1)*xq for i in range(1, N+1)]
d2   = [(i+1)*i*xq**(i-1) for i in range(1, N+1)]
A = np.array([[np.trapz((d2[i] - m*m*phi[i])*phi[j], xq) for i in range(N)] for j in range(N)])
rhs = np.array([np.trapz(m*m*1.0*phi[j], xq) for j in range(N)])       # residual of φ0 = 1 is -m²
c = np.linalg.solve(A, rhs)
theta = 1 + sum(ci*p for ci, p in zip(c, phi))
exact = np.cosh(m*(1 - xq))/np.cosh(m)
print("Galerkin coefficients:", np.round(c, 5), " max error:", np.abs(theta - exact).max())
print("fin heat rate  -θ'(0): Galerkin", -np.gradient(theta, xq)[0], " exact", m*np.tanh(m))
// Heat-balance integral with θ = (1 - x/δ)^n against the exact erfc profile
const n = 2, t = 1, a = 1;
const delta = Math.sqrt(2*n*(n+1)*a*t);
const x = M.linspace(0, 1.2*delta, 200);
plot(x, [x.map(v => v < delta ? Math.pow(1 - v/delta, n) : 0), x.map(v => M.erfc(v/(2*Math.sqrt(a*t))))],
     {xlabel:'x / √(αt)', ylabel:'θ', labels:['HBIM n = '+n, 'exact erfc']});
print("delta =", delta, "  flux ratio HBIM/exact =", Math.sqrt(Math.PI*n/(2*(n+1))));
