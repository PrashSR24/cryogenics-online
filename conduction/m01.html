Lecture 1

Heat transfer, thermodynamics, and the continuum

Thermodynamics tells you how much energy a system exchanges as it moves between equilibrium states. It says nothing about how fast. A cup of coffee and a block of ice will reach the same final state whether you wait a minute or a year; thermodynamics cannot distinguish the two. Heat transfer is the science of the rate, and the rate is set by a temperature difference acting through a mechanism.

There are three mechanisms. Conduction moves energy through a stationary medium by molecular collisions, lattice vibrations (phonons) and, in metals, free electrons. Radiation moves it as electromagnetic waves and needs no medium at all. Convection is conduction plus the bulk motion of a fluid that carries internal energy with it. Strictly there are only two modes, diffusion and radiation, because convection is diffusion inside a moving deformable medium. We keep the three-way split because the mathematics differ so much.

Conduction (Fourier)$$\mathbf q'' = -k\,\nabla T$$
Convection (Newton)$$q'' = h\,(T_s - T_\infty)$$
Radiation (Stefan–Boltzmann)$$q''_b = \sigma T^4,\ \ \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-4}}$$
Energy conservation$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \dot Q - \dot W$$

These split into general laws that hold for every material (conservation of mass, momentum, energy; the second law) and constitutive relations that describe how a particular material behaves (Fourier, Newton, Stefan–Boltzmann). Every conduction problem in this course is one general law (energy conservation) combined with one constitutive law (Fourier).

From molecules to a continuum

Below is a gas trapped between a hot wall and a cold wall. Each particle that touches a wall leaves with a speed drawn from that wall's temperature. Particles collide with each other and share energy. Watch the measured local temperature (bars) in each slab of the box.

Physical interpretation

Equal numbers of molecules cross the midplane in each direction, so no net mass moves. Molecules arriving from the hot side carry more kinetic energy than those arriving from the cold side, so energy moves anyway. That is conduction.

Turn the collision rate down. When molecules rarely collide (a large Knudsen number, \(\mathrm{Kn} = \lambda_{\text{mfp}}/L\)), the profile goes flat in the middle and jumps at each wall. The gas no longer has a well-defined local temperature gradient, and Fourier's law fails. The continuum hypothesis needs the mean free path to be much smaller than the length scales of interest. As a rule of thumb that means more than about \(10^{12}\) molecules per mm³.

Cryogenic angle

This is why the vacuum jacket of a dewar insulates. Pump the annulus down until the mean free path exceeds the gap width and gas conduction stops depending on gap size. It becomes proportional to pressure and collapses. Multilayer insulation then deals with the radiation that remains.

Lecture 2

Temperature fields, gradients and Fourier’s law

The temperature field \(T(\mathbf r,t)\) assigns a temperature to every point at every instant. Join the points of equal temperature and you get isothermal surfaces. Two isotherms never intersect, because a point cannot hold two temperatures. Heat cannot flow along an isotherm, because there is no temperature difference along it to drive it.

The gradient \(\nabla T = \hat{\mathbf i}\,\partial_x T + \hat{\mathbf j}\,\partial_y T + \hat{\mathbf k}\,\partial_z T\) is a vector normal to the isotherm, pointing toward increasing temperature. The proof takes one line: on an isotherm \(dT = \nabla T\cdot d\mathbf r = 0\) for every tangent \(d\mathbf r\), so \(\nabla T\) is perpendicular to every tangent.

Fourier observed that, at steady state, heat flow through a slab is proportional to area and temperature difference and inversely proportional to thickness: \(q = kA\,(T_1-T_2)/L\). Shrinking the slab to a differential layer gives the local, vector law:

Fourier’s law (isotropic)$$\mathbf q'' = -k\,\nabla T,\qquad q''_n = -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial n},\qquad q''_s = \mathbf q''\cdot\hat{\mathbf s} = -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial s}$$

The minus sign enforces the second law: heat flows down the gradient. The component of flux through any surface with unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf s}\), isothermal or not, is the projection \(\mathbf q''\cdot\hat{\mathbf s}\). In the three standard coordinate systems the gradient reads:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Cartesian: } & \mathbf q'' = -k\left(\hat{\mathbf i}\,\partial_x T + \hat{\mathbf j}\,\partial_y T + \hat{\mathbf k}\,\partial_z T\right)\\ \text{Cylindrical: } & \mathbf q'' = -k\left(\hat{\mathbf e}_r\,\partial_r T + \hat{\mathbf e}_\phi\,\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_\phi T + \hat{\mathbf e}_z\,\partial_z T\right)\\ \text{Spherical: } & \mathbf q'' = -k\left(\hat{\mathbf e}_r\,\partial_r T + \hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\,\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_\theta T + \hat{\mathbf e}_\phi\,\tfrac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\phi T\right) \end{aligned}$$
Physical interpretation

In an isotropic solid, the heat-flux arrow (orange) is always perpendicular to the isotherm, exactly antiparallel to \(\nabla T\) (blue). Switch on anisotropy. Wood, graphite, composite laminates and MLI blankets conduct far better along one axis than across it. The flux now leans toward the high-conductivity direction and crosses isotherms at an angle. Fourier's law becomes \(q''_i = -\sum_j k_{ij}\,\partial T/\partial x_j\), with a conductivity tensor. Module 11 develops this fully.

Thermal conductivity

\(k\) is a thermophysical property in W/(m·K). A medium is homogeneous if \(k\) does not vary with position and isotropic if it does not vary with direction. Porous solids such as cork and glass wool are heterogeneous, and fibrous solids such as wood are anisotropic. Order-of-magnitude values span five decades:

Material classk, W/(m·K) near 300 KDominant carrier
Gases (air, N₂)0.01 – 0.03 (He ≈ 0.15)Molecular collisions
Insulation (foams, aerogel, fibre)0.015 – 0.05Trapped gas + tortuous solid paths
Liquids (water ≈ 0.6, LN₂ ≈ 0.14)0.1 – 0.7Molecular
Non-metallic solids (glass, ceramics)1 – 40Phonons
Metal alloys (stainless steel ≈ 15)10 – 120Electrons + phonons
Pure metals (Al ≈ 237, Cu ≈ 400)50 – 430Free electrons
Diamond, graphene (in-plane)1000 – 5000Phonons
Cryogenic angle

Below about 100 K, \(k\) stops being a constant. High-purity copper peaks near 10–20 K at several thousand W/(m·K), while stainless steel falls steadily toward zero. Cryostat supports are therefore made of stainless steel or G-10, thermal straps of OFHC copper, and every conductive heat-leak calculation uses \(\int k\,dT\) rather than \(k\,\Delta T\). That integral is exactly the Kirchhoff transform of Lecture 5.

# Worked problem: T(x) = 120 - 400 x^2 (°C), 0 <= x <= 0.25 m, k = 40 W/(m K)
import numpy as np
k, L = 40.0, 0.25
T  = lambda x: 120 - 400*x**2
dT = lambda x: -800*x          # dT/dx
q  = lambda x: -k*dT(x)        # Fourier's law, W/m^2

print(f"q''(0)    = {q(0):8.1f} W/m^2   (adiabatic face: zero slope)")
print(f"q''(L)    = {q(L):8.1f} W/m^2")
# What does the curvature tell us? Steady 1D: k T'' + q_gen = 0
q_gen = -k*(-800)
print(f"implied uniform heat generation = {q_gen:.0f} W/m^3")
print(f"check energy balance: q_gen*L = {q_gen*L:.0f} W/m^2  == q''(L)")
// Same worked problem, live. Edit k or the profile and press Run.
const k = 40, L = 0.25;
const T  = x => 120 - 400*x*x;
const q  = x => -k * (-800*x);
print("q''(0) =", q(0), "W/m^2");
print("q''(L) =", q(L), "W/m^2");
print("implied q_gen =", 800*k, "W/m^3");
const xs = M.linspace(0, L, 60);
plot(xs, [xs.map(T)], {xlabel:'x (m)', ylabel:'T (°C)', labels:['T(x)']});
plot(xs, [xs.map(q)], {xlabel:'x (m)', ylabel:"q'' (W/m²)", labels:["q''(x)"]});

The curvature is what makes this example worth working. A parabolic steady profile means \(d^2T/dx^2\neq 0\), so the flux changes with position and something between the faces must be generating heat. Reading a profile backwards like this is the habit to build: slope is flux, and curvature is a source or storage.

Lecture 3

Deriving the general heat conduction equation

Take an arbitrary control volume \(V\) with surface \(A\) and outward normal \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) inside a stationary solid with volumetric generation \(\dot q(\mathbf r,t)\) in W/m³. Energy conservation reads: rate of storage equals the net inflow through the surface plus the generation.

$$\int_V \rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\,dV = -\oint_A \mathbf q''\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA + \int_V \dot q\,dV$$

The divergence theorem, \(\oint_A \mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA = \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV\), turns the surface integral into a volume integral:

$$\int_V\left[\rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf q'' - \dot q\right]dV = 0$$

This holds for every sub-volume, so the integrand itself must vanish. Substituting Fourier's law gives the general heat conduction equation:

General heat conduction equation$$\rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\left(k\,\nabla T\right) + \dot q = k\nabla^2 T + \nabla k\cdot\nabla T + \dot q$$

If \(k\) depends on temperature, \(\nabla k = (dk/dT)\nabla T\) and the term \((dk/dT)|\nabla T|^2\) makes the equation nonlinear. With constant properties it becomes linear, and four classical special cases follow:

AssumptionsEquationName
Constant \(k,\rho,c\)\(\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla^2T + \dfrac{\dot q}{k}\)Fourier–Biot
… and no generation\(\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla^2T\)Diffusion equation
Steady, constant \(k\)\(\nabla^2T + \dot q/k = 0\)Poisson
Steady, no generation\(\nabla^2T = 0\)Laplace
Physical interpretation: thermal diffusivity

\(\alpha = k/\rho c\) in m²/s is the ratio of how well a material conducts heat to how much heat it must absorb to warm up. A large \(\alpha\) means a temperature disturbance spreads quickly, because little energy is soaked up along the way. Copper (\(\alpha\approx 1.1\times10^{-4}\)) responds about 800 times faster than water (\(1.4\times10^{-7}\)). The distance a disturbance travels in time \(t\) scales as \(\sqrt{\alpha t}\). That square root runs through every transient result in Modules 4 to 10.

The Laplace equation contains no properties at all. In a steady, source-free, constant-\(k\) body, the temperature field depends only on geometry and boundary values. Copper and glass blocks with the same boundary temperatures have identical temperature fields, though very different heat flows.

The Laplacian in three coordinate systems

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla^2T &= \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2}\\ \nabla^2T &= \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial \phi^2}+\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2}\\ \nabla^2T &= \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial T}{\partial \theta}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial \phi^2} \end{aligned}$$

The \(1/r\) and \(1/r^2\) factors carry the geometry. In a cylinder or sphere, heat flowing outward spreads over a growing area, so the gradient must fall even without any loss along the way.

Moving solids

If the solid itself moves with velocity \(\mathbf V\) (extrusion, rolling, a conveyor), it carries enthalpy flux \(\rho c T\mathbf V\) in addition to conduction. The storage term becomes the substantial derivative, the rate of change seen by an observer riding with the material:

$$\rho c\,\frac{DT}{Dt} = \rho c\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \mathbf V\cdot\nabla T\right) = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + \dot q$$

This is the starting point for the moving weld source of Module 9 and for the cryogenic twin-screw extrusion of solid hydrogen pellets.

Lecture 4

Initial and boundary conditions

The heat equation is first order in time and second order in each space coordinate. A 3D transient problem therefore needs seven conditions: one initial condition and two boundary conditions per coordinate. The heat equation has infinitely many solutions; the conditions pick out the one that is physically realised. Every boundary condition is an energy balance on a surface of zero thickness, which can store nothing.

General surface energy balance$$-k\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}\bigg|_s = h\,(T_s - T_{\infty,1}) + \varepsilon\sigma\,(T_s^4 - T_{\infty,2}^4)$$
KindMathematical formPhysical realisation
1st (Dirichlet)\(T|_s = T_s(\mathbf r_s,t)\)Surface in contact with a melting solid or boiling liquid (ice bath, LN₂ pool)
2nd (Neumann)\(-k\,\partial T/\partial n|_s = q''_s\)Thin-film electric heater; laser flux; \(q''_s=0\) is perfect insulation or a symmetry plane
3rd (Robin)\(-k\,\partial T/\partial n|_s = h(T_s - T_\infty)\)Convection to a fluid
Interface\(-k_1\partial_n T_1 = -k_2\partial_n T_2 = h_c(T_1-T_2)\)Imperfect contact with contact conductance \(h_c\); \(h_c\to\infty\) gives \(T_1 = T_2\)
Sliding interface\(q''_1 + \mu p V - q''_2 = 0\)Frictional heating between brake pad and disc
Nonlinearradiation \(\propto T^4\); free convection \(h\propto \Delta T^{1/4}\); Stefan conditionFurnaces, natural cooling, melting fronts
Finiteness\(T(0,t)\) finiteRemoves \(\ln r\), \(Y_0\), \(1/r\) terms at the axis or centre

A condition is homogeneous if every term contains \(T\) or its derivative. Examples are \(T=0\), \(\partial T/\partial n = 0\), and \(k\,\partial T/\partial n + hT=0\). Separation of variables (Modules 3 and 4) needs homogeneous conditions in the eigen-directions, so a large part of the craft is shifting the temperature datum until they become homogeneous.

Physical interpretation: the Robin tangent

Rearrange the convection condition as \(-\partial T/\partial x|_L = (T_L - T_\infty)/(k/h)\). The tangent to the profile at the wall, extended outward, hits \(T_\infty\) at a distance \(k/h\) beyond the surface. The fluid behaves like an extra slab of solid of thickness \(k/h\) whose far face is held at \(T_\infty\). As \(h\to\infty\) the virtual slab vanishes and the condition becomes Dirichlet. As \(h\to0\) the slab becomes infinitely thick, the tangent goes flat, and the condition becomes adiabatic. Watch the dashed line in the simulation.

Lecture 5

One-dimensional steady conduction and the Kirchhoff transformation

Example: stop the heat leak from the warm side

A slab of thickness \(L\) separates a warm fluid \((T_i, h_i)\) at \(x=0\) from a cold one \((T_o, h_o)\) at \(x=L\). How much uniform internal heating \(\dot q\) makes the warm-side loss exactly zero? The required profile has \(T(0)=T_i\) and \(dT/dx|_0 = 0\). Integrating \(T'' = -\dot q/k\) twice and applying the convective condition at \(x=L\) gives:

$$\frac{T(x)-T_o}{\dot q L^2/k} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{L^2}\right) + \frac{k}{h_oL},\qquad \dot q_{\text{req}} = \frac{T_i - T_o}{L^2/(2k) + L/h_o}$$

All of that heat leaves through the cold face, \(q''_L = \dot q L\). This is the logic of guard heaters, which keep a sensitive sample in a heat-flux meter from leaking sideways.

Example: heat-generating cylinder (fuel rod, wire)

$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r\frac{dT}{dr}\right) + \frac{\dot q}{k} = 0,\quad \frac{dT}{dr}\Big|_0=0,\quad -k\frac{dT}{dr}\Big|_{r_0} = h(T-T_\infty)\ \Rightarrow\ T(r) = T_\infty + \frac{\dot q r_0^2}{4k}\left[1-\frac{r^2}{r_0^2} + \frac{2k}{hr_0}\right]$$

The Kirchhoff transformation

When \(k=k(T)\), the steady equation \(\nabla\cdot(k(T)\nabla T)+\dot q = 0\) is nonlinear. Define a new temperature that absorbs the nonlinearity:

Kirchhoff transform$$\theta = \frac{1}{k_R}\int_{T_R}^{T} k(T')\,dT'\quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\theta = \frac{k(T)}{k_R}\nabla T\quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla^2\theta + \frac{\dot q}{k_R} = 0$$

The PDE is now linear. Dirichlet conditions transform exactly, \(T=T_s\Rightarrow\theta=\theta(T_s)\), and so do prescribed-flux conditions, \(-k\,\partial T/\partial n = -k_R\,\partial\theta/\partial n\). Convective conditions stay nonlinear because they contain \(T\) itself. Solve for \(\theta\), then invert the integral to recover \(T\).

Physical interpretation

For a slab with \(k = k_0(1+\beta T)\), \(\theta\) is linear in \(x\), but \(T\) bows. It bows toward the side where \(k\) is lower, because the same flux needs a steeper gradient there. Integrate \(k\,dT\) across the slab and you get a striking result: the exact heat flux equals the constant-\(k\) formula evaluated with \(k\) at the arithmetic-mean temperature. This holds exactly for linear \(k(T)\) and is very close for gentle nonlinearity. It is why handbook tables quote "mean conductivity".

Worked example: the fuel-rod limit

A fuel rod of radius \(r_0=1\) cm has \(k(T) = 3200/T\) W/(m·K) with \(T\) in °C. Its surface is held at 573 °C, and the centreline must not exceed 2773 °C. With \(\theta\) referenced to the wall, the transformed solution is \(\int_{T_w}^{T}k\,dT' = \tfrac{\dot q r_0^2}{4}(1 - r^2/r_0^2)\). At the axis:

$$\dot q_{\max} = \frac{4}{r_0^2}\int_{T_w}^{T_c}\frac{3200}{T}\,dT = \frac{4\times3200}{(0.01)^2}\ln\frac{2773}{573} = 2.02\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{W/m^3},\qquad q''_s = \frac{\dot q r_0}{2} = 1.01\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$$
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.optimize import brentq

r0, Tw, Tc_max = 0.01, 573.0, 2773.0
k = lambda T: 3200.0/T                       # W/(m K), T in °C

# Kirchhoff: ∫_{Tw}^{Tc} k dT = q r0^2 / 4
K = quad(k, Tw, Tc_max)[0]
q_max = 4*K/r0**2
print(f"∫k dT = {K:.1f} W/m   ->   q_max = {q_max:.3e} W/m^3")
print(f"surface heat flux = {q_max*r0/2:.3e} W/m^2")

# What if we had assumed constant k = k(Tw)?
kw = k(Tw)
print(f"constant-k estimate: q = {4*kw*(Tc_max-Tw)/r0**2:.3e} W/m^3  (unsafe by a factor "
      f"{4*kw*(Tc_max-Tw)/r0**2/q_max:.2f})")

# Recover T(r) by inverting the Kirchhoff integral at each radius
r = np.linspace(0, r0, 6)
for ri in r:
    target = q_max*(r0**2 - ri**2)/4
    T = brentq(lambda T: quad(k, Tw, T)[0] - target, Tw, Tc_max + 1)
    print(f"r = {ri*1e3:4.1f} mm   T = {T:7.1f} °C")
// Live version: change q (W/m^3) and see the centreline temperature.
const r0 = 0.01, Tw = 573, q = 2.02e8;
// ∫ 3200/T dT = 3200 ln(T/Tw)  ->  T(r) = Tw exp( q (r0²-r²) / (4·3200) )
const r = M.linspace(0, r0, 50);
const Tvar = r.map(x => Tw*Math.exp(q*(r0*r0 - x*x)/(4*3200)));
const kw = 3200/Tw;
const Tconst = r.map(x => Tw + q*(r0*r0 - x*x)/(4*kw));
print("centreline, variable k:", Tvar[0].toFixed(0), "°C");
print("centreline, k = k(Tw):", Tconst[0].toFixed(0), "°C");
plot(r.map(x=>x*1e3), [Tvar, Tconst], {xlabel:'r (mm)', ylabel:'T (°C)', labels:['k = 3200/T (exact)','k = k(Tw)']});
Lecture 6

Dimensionless analysis and extended surfaces

Scale lengths by \(L\), temperature by \(\theta = (T-T_\infty)/(T_0-T_\infty)\) and time by \(L^2/\alpha\). The heat equation with a convective boundary collapses to

$$\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\tau} = \nabla_\xi^2\theta + \Phi,\qquad \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial N} + \mathrm{Bi}\,\theta = 0,\qquad \tau = \frac{\alpha t}{L^2}\ (\text{Fourier}),\quad \mathrm{Bi} = \frac{hL}{k}\ (\text{Biot}),\quad \Phi = \frac{\dot q L^2}{k(T_0-T_\infty)}$$

Instead of varying \(k, \rho, c, h, L, t\) separately, you vary two or three groups, and one chart covers every material.

  • Fourier number \(\tau = \alpha t/L^2 = \dfrac{kL^2/L}{\rho c L^3/t}\): the rate heat is conducted across \(L\) divided by the rate it is stored in \(L^3\). A large \(\tau\) means the disturbance has penetrated the whole body.
  • Biot number \(\mathrm{Bi} = \dfrac{L/kA}{1/hA}\): internal conduction resistance divided by external surface resistance. It looks like the Nusselt number, but uses the solid's \(k\).

Fins

To increase \(q = hA(T_s-T_\infty)\) when \(h\) and \(\Delta T\) are fixed, you increase \(A\) by adding fins. Fins are thin enough that temperature varies only along their length, so they are one-dimensional. An energy balance on a slice \(dx\) with cross-section \(A_c(x)\) and perimeter \(P(x)\) gives:

General fin equation$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(A_c\frac{dT}{dx}\right) - \frac{hP}{k}(T-T_\infty) = 0\quad\xrightarrow{A_c,\,P\ \text{const}}\quad \frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2\theta = 0,\quad m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA_c}$$
Tip condition\(\theta/\theta_b\)Fin heat rate \(q_f\)
Infinitely long\(e^{-mx}\)\(M\), where \(M = \sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\)
Adiabatic tip\(\dfrac{\cosh m(L-x)}{\cosh mL}\)\(M\tanh mL\)
Convective tip\(\dfrac{\cosh m(L-x) + \frac{h}{mk}\sinh m(L-x)}{\cosh mL + \frac{h}{mk}\sinh mL}\)\(M\,\dfrac{\sinh mL + \frac{h}{mk}\cosh mL}{\cosh mL + \frac{h}{mk}\sinh mL}\)
Prescribed tip \(\theta_L\)\(\dfrac{(\theta_L/\theta_b)\sinh mx + \sinh m(L-x)}{\sinh mL}\)\(M\,\dfrac{\cosh mL - \theta_L/\theta_b}{\sinh mL}\)

Two figures of merit: efficiency \(\eta_f = q_f/(hA_f\theta_b)\), actual over ideal with the whole fin at base temperature, and effectiveness \(\varepsilon_f = q_f/(hA_c\theta_b)\), with the fin over without it. A fin is rarely worthwhile unless \(\varepsilon_f \gtrsim 2\).

Physical interpretation

\(1/m\) is the fin's natural decay length, the distance over which the excess temperature falls by \(e\). Beyond \(mL\approx 2.5\), \(\tanh mL > 0.987\): the far end of the fin is almost at fluid temperature and does nothing except add mass and cost. That is why real heat-sink fins are short and closely spaced. Raising \(k\) (aluminium to copper) or thickening the fin lowers \(m\), so the fin "reaches" further.

A fin reduces heat transfer when \(\varepsilon_f\lt 1\). This happens if \(\mathrm{Bi} = h(A_c/P)/k > 1\), for instance a stainless-steel fin in boiling water. The fin's conduction resistance then outweighs the extra area.

In the real world

Motor housings, transformer tanks, finned-tube exchangers, CPU heat sinks and air-cooled engine cylinders all use fins. In cryogenics the fin equation runs in reverse. Current leads and thermometer wires are "fins" that draw heat into the cold stage, and long, thin, low-\(k\) wires are chosen precisely so that \(mL\) is large and the parasitic heat leak \(\approx\sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\) stays small.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_bvp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Aluminium pin fin, D = 5 mm, L = 50 mm, h = 25 W/m²K, base 100 °C above ambient
k, D, L, h, thb = 205.0, 5e-3, 50e-3, 25.0, 100.0
P, Ac = np.pi*D, np.pi*D**2/4
m = np.sqrt(h*P/(k*Ac)); Mf = np.sqrt(h*P*k*Ac)*thb; a = h/(m*k)

x = np.linspace(0, L, 200)
th = thb*(np.cosh(m*(L-x)) + a*np.sinh(m*(L-x)))/(np.cosh(m*L) + a*np.sinh(m*L))
qf = Mf*(np.sinh(m*L) + a*np.cosh(m*L))/(np.cosh(m*L) + a*np.sinh(m*L))
eta = qf/(h*(P*L + Ac)*thb); eps = qf/(h*Ac*thb)
print(f"mL = {m*L:.3f}, q_fin = {qf:.3f} W, efficiency = {eta:.3f}, effectiveness = {eps:.1f}")

# Cross-check with a general BVP solver (works for variable area or k(T) too)
f  = lambda x, y: np.vstack([y[1], m**2*y[0]])
bc = lambda ya, yb: np.array([ya[0]-thb, k*yb[1] + h*yb[0]])
