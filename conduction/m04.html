Lecture 19

Lumped heat capacity systems

Drop a hot steel ball into a cooler fluid. If the ball conducts heat internally much faster than its surface can hand it to the fluid, its interior stays nearly uniform. Temperature becomes a function of time alone, and the heat equation collapses to one ODE from a global energy balance:

$$\rho cV\frac{dT}{dt} = -hA_s\,(T - T_\infty)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{T - T_\infty}{T_i - T_\infty} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{hA_s}{\rho cV}t\right) = e^{-t/\tau_{th}} = e^{-\mathrm{Bi}\,\mathrm{Fo}}$$

Here \(\tau_{th} = \rho cV/(hA_s) = R_{conv}C_{th}\) is the thermal time constant, an RC circuit. With the characteristic length \(L_c = V/A_s\), \(\mathrm{Bi} = hL_c/k\) and \(\mathrm{Fo} = \alpha t/L_c^2\). The criterion is

Lumped criterion$$\mathrm{Bi} = \frac{hL_c}{k}\lt 0.1,\qquad L_c = \frac{V}{A_s} = \begin{cases}L & \text{plate of thickness }2L\\ r_0/2 & \text{long cylinder}\\ r_0/3 & \text{sphere}\end{cases}$$

At \(\mathrm{Bi} = 0.1\) the centre-to-surface temperature difference is about 5%, which is the source of the rule. Heat released up to time \(t\) is \(Q = \rho cV(T_i - T_\infty)(1 - e^{-t/\tau_{th}})\).

Worked example. A steel ball, 5 cm in diameter (\(k = 35\), \(\rho = 7800\), \(c = 460\) in SI units), at 450 °C is placed in air at 100 °C with \(h = 10\) W/m²K. When does it reach 150 °C? \(\mathrm{Bi} = h(r_0/3)/k = 0.0024\), so lumping is excellent. Then \(hA/\rho cV = 3.344\times10^{-4}\) s⁻¹ and \(t = \ln(350/50)/3.344\times10^{-4} = 5819\) s \(\approx 1.62\) h.

Physical interpretation

Bi compares two resistances in series: conduction inside, \(L_c/k\), and convection outside, \(1/h\). When Bi is small, the surface film is the bottleneck. All the temperature drop happens in the fluid, and the solid is "isothermal" by comparison. Raise h or lower k in the simulation and watch the exact centre and surface curves peel apart from the lumped curve. Lumping overpredicts the surface temperature and underpredicts the centre.

Cryogenic angle

Quenching a part in liquid nitrogen is not a constant-h problem. While the surface is hot, film boiling blankets it with vapour and h is low, around 100–200 W/m²K. Below the Leidenfrost point the film collapses and nucleate boiling drives h into the thousands. The cool-down curve has a slow phase followed by a sudden fast drop. The lumped ODE still works if you let h depend on surface temperature and integrate numerically, which is a good exercise with the code below.

Partial lumping

A body may be lumped in some directions and not others. A long thin rod with a heated end has a small Biot number across its radius but not along its length. Lump the cross-section and keep \(x\), and you recover the transient fin equation \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2} - m^2\theta\). The test is applied direction by direction: \(\mathrm{Bi}_y, \mathrm{Bi}_z\lt0.1\) lets you lump \(y\) and \(z\).

Lecture 20

Distributed systems: exact solution by separation of variables

When Bi is not small, temperature varies inside the body and we solve the PDE. Take a slab \(0\le x\le L\) at initial temperature \(F(x)\), with both faces suddenly held at \(T = 0\):

$$\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2};\qquad T = X(x)\Gamma(t)\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{X''}{X} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma} = -\lambda^2$$

The negative sign is forced: a positive constant would make \(\Gamma\) grow without bound, which the second law forbids. Space gives a Sturm–Liouville problem, \(X_n = \sin\lambda_nx\) with \(\lambda_n = n\pi/L\), and time gives pure decay, \(\Gamma_n = e^{-\alpha\lambda_n^2t}\):

Slab, faces at zero$$T(x,t) = \frac{2}{L}\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\alpha\lambda_n^2t}\sin\lambda_nx\int_0^LF(x')\sin\lambda_nx'\,dx'$$

Superposition for non-homogeneous boundaries

If the faces are held at different non-zero temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), split \(T(x,t) = T_s(x) + \theta(x,t)\). The steady part \(T_s = T_1 + (T_2 - T_1)x/L\) absorbs the boundary conditions, and the transient \(\theta\) has homogeneous conditions and initial data \(F(x) - T_s(x)\). In 2D, a transient plate with fixed edges becomes the steady 2D solution of Module 3 plus a decaying double series.

Physical interpretation

Each mode decays with its own time constant \(\tau_n = 1/(\alpha\lambda_n^2) = L^2/(n^2\pi^2\alpha)\). Mode 3 dies nine times faster than mode 1. After \(\mathrm{Fo} = \alpha t/L^2\approx0.2\), only the first mode is left. The temperature shape has "locked" into \(\sin(\pi x/L)\) and simply shrinks. This regular regime is why one-term approximations and Heisler charts work.

Lecture 21

Convective boundaries: the plane wall

A plate of thickness \(2L\) at uniform \(T_i\) is suddenly exposed on both faces to a fluid at \(T_\infty\). By symmetry we solve \(0\le x\le L\) with \(\partial\theta/\partial x = 0\) at the midplane and \(-k\,\partial\theta/\partial x = h\theta\) at \(x = L\), with \(\theta = T - T_\infty\). The eigenfunctions are \(\cos\lambda_nx\), and the convective face gives a transcendental eigen-condition:

Plane wall, convective faces$$\frac{\theta}{\theta_i} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2\sin\gamma_n}{\gamma_n + \sin\gamma_n\cos\gamma_n}\cos\!\left(\gamma_n\frac{x}{L}\right)e^{-\gamma_n^2\mathrm{Fo}},\qquad \gamma_n\tan\gamma_n = \mathrm{Bi} = \frac{hL}{k}$$

The roots \(\gamma_n\) lie in \((n-1)\pi\lt\gamma_n\lt(n-\tfrac12)\pi\). As Bi→∞ they approach \((n-\tfrac12)\pi\), the fixed-temperature case. As Bi→0, \(\gamma_1\to\sqrt{\mathrm{Bi}}\) and the solution reduces to the lumped exponential. Heat released as a fraction of the maximum:

$$\frac{Q}{Q_i} = 1 - \sum_n\frac{2\sin^2\gamma_n}{\gamma_n(\gamma_n + \sin\gamma_n\cos\gamma_n)}e^{-\gamma_n^2\mathrm{Fo}},\qquad Q_i = \rho c\,(2LA)(T_i - T_\infty)$$
Lecture 22

Convective boundaries: long cylinder and sphere

The same structure holds for the other two classical shapes. Only the eigenfunctions, the eigen-condition and the coefficients change:

ShapeEigenfunctionEigen-condition (Bi = hr₀/k)Coefficient \(C_n\)
Plane wall\(\cos(\gamma_n x/L)\)\(\gamma_n\tan\gamma_n = \mathrm{Bi}\)\(\dfrac{4\sin\gamma_n}{2\gamma_n + \sin2\gamma_n}\)
Long cylinder\(J_0(\gamma_n r/r_0)\)\(\gamma_n\dfrac{J_1(\gamma_n)}{J_0(\gamma_n)} = \mathrm{Bi}\)\(\dfrac{2}{\gamma_n}\dfrac{J_1(\gamma_n)}{J_0^2(\gamma_n) + J_1^2(\gamma_n)}\)
Sphere\(\dfrac{\sin(\gamma_nr/r_0)}{\gamma_nr/r_0}\)\(1 - \gamma_n\cot\gamma_n = \mathrm{Bi}\)\(\dfrac{4(\sin\gamma_n - \gamma_n\cos\gamma_n)}{2\gamma_n - \sin2\gamma_n}\)

For the sphere, the substitution \(U = r\theta\) turns \(\frac{1}{r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r\theta)\) into \(\frac{1}{r}\partial_r^2U\). The sphere problem becomes a slab problem for \(U\), which explains the \(\sin(\gamma r)/r\) eigenfunction. The simulation above handles all three shapes. Pick one and compare how quickly each cools at the same Bi. The sphere is fastest, because it has the most surface per unit volume.

Lecture 23

Heisler charts, one-term solutions and multidimensional products

For \(\mathrm{Fo}>0.2\), the series is accurate to within 2% with one term:

$$\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_i}\approx C_1e^{-\gamma_1^2\mathrm{Fo}}\ (\text{centre}),\qquad \frac{\theta}{\theta_0} = \cos(\gamma_1x/L),\ J_0(\gamma_1r/r_0),\ \frac{\sin(\gamma_1r/r_0)}{\gamma_1r/r_0}$$

Heisler (1947) plotted the centre temperature against Fo for families of \(1/\mathrm{Bi}\), plus a position-correction chart and Gröber's heat-loss chart against \(\mathrm{Bi}^2\mathrm{Fo}\). Printed charts are hard to read at small values. Below, the charts are recomputed live from the exact series, so you can read them to any precision.

Worked example. A 16 mm carbon-steel plate (\(k = 40\) W/mK, \(\alpha = 0.1\) cm²/s) at 600 °C is quenched in water at 15 °C with \(h = 10\,000\) W/m²K.

  1. \(L = 8\) mm, so \(\mathrm{Bi} = hL/k = 2\) and lumping fails. \(\theta_0/\theta_i = (100-15)/(600-15) = 0.145\). With \(\gamma_1 = 1.0769\) and \(C_1 = 1.1785\), the one-term formula gives \(\mathrm{Fo} = 1.805\), so \(t = \mathrm{Fo}\,L^2/\alpha = 11.6\) s for the midplane to reach 100 °C. A printed Heisler chart gives \(\mathrm{Fo}\approx1.8\).
  2. At 2 mm below the surface, \(x/L = 0.75\): \(\theta/\theta_0 = \cos(0.75\gamma_1) = 0.69\), so \(T\approx 74\) °C.
  3. \(\mathrm{Bi}^2\mathrm{Fo} = 7.22\). Reading a printed Gröber chart gives \(Q/Q_i\approx0.84\). The exact series gives \(0.881\): 88% of the plate's excess energy is gone. Charts are fine for estimates, but a few lines of code remove the reading error.

Multidimensional bodies as products

For a body that is the intersection of 1D bodies (a long rectangular bar = plate × plate, a short cylinder = infinite cylinder × plate, a brick = plate × plate × plate), with uniform initial temperature and convection to one \(T_\infty\), the dimensionless solution is the product of the 1D solutions:

Product solution$$\frac{\theta(x,y,t)}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{bar}} = \frac{\theta(x,t)}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{plate }2L_1}\times\frac{\theta(y,t)}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{plate }2L_2},\qquad \frac{\theta}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{short cyl.}} = \frac{\theta(r,t)}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{cyl.}}\times\frac{\theta(z,t)}{\theta_i}\bigg|_{\text{plate}}$$

This works because \(\partial_t(PQ) = P\partial_tQ + Q\partial_tP\) and \(\nabla^2(PQ) = P\nabla^2Q + Q\nabla^2P\) separate exactly when each factor solves its own 1D problem. The convective conditions involve only one factor on each face, and the initial condition \(1\times1 = 1\) holds.

In the real world

Heat-treatment shops size quench times this way. So do food engineers checking that the centre of a canned product reaches sterilisation temperature, and the chocolate conveyor from Lecture 1, where the conveyor speed comes from the time for the centre of a bar (a product of three plates) to fall below its set point.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
from scipy.special import j0, j1

def eig(Bi, shape='plate', n=6):
    f = {'plate':  lambda g: g*np.tan(g) - Bi,
         'cyl':    lambda g: g*j1(g)/j0(g) - Bi,
         'sphere': lambda g: 1 - g/np.tan(g) - Bi}[shape]
    roots, g = [], 1e-6
    grid = np.linspace(1e-6, (n+1)*np.pi, 20000)
    v = f(grid)
    for a, b, fa, fb in zip(grid[:-1], grid[1:], v[:-1], v[1:]):
        if np.sign(fa) != np.sign(fb) and abs(fa) < 50 and abs(fb) < 50:
            roots.append(brentq(f, a, b))
        if len(roots) == n: break
    return np.array(roots)

def centre(Bi, Fo, shape='plate', n=40):
    g = eig(Bi, shape, n)
    C = {'plate': 4*np.sin(g)/(2*g + np.sin(2*g)),
         'cyl':   2/g*j1(g)/(j0(g)**2 + j1(g)**2),
         'sphere':4*(np.sin(g) - g*np.cos(g))/(2*g - np.sin(2*g))}[shape]
    return np.sum(C*np.exp(-g**2*Fo)), g, C

# Quench example: 16 mm steel plate, h = 10 000, k = 40, alpha = 0.1 cm²/s
L, k, h, alpha = 0.008, 40.0, 1e4, 0.1e-4
Bi = h*L/k; target = (100-15)/(600-15)
Fo = brentq(lambda F: centre(Bi, F)[0] - target, 0.01, 10)
_, g, C = centre(Bi, Fo)
print(f"Bi = {Bi}, gamma1 = {g[0]:.4f}, C1 = {C[0]:.4f}")
print(f"Fo = {Fo:.3f}  ->  t = {Fo*L**2/alpha:.2f} s")
print(f"T at x/L = 0.75: {15 + target*np.cos(0.75*g[0])*(600-15)/1:.1f} °C (one-term position factor)")
Qfrac = 1 - np.sum(2*np.sin(g)**2/(g*(g+np.sin(g)*np.cos(g)))*np.exp(-g**2*Fo))
print(f"Q/Qi = {Qfrac:.3f}")

# Lumped steel-ball example
r0, h2, k2, rho, c = 0.025, 10, 35, 7800, 460
print(f"steel ball: Bi = {h2*r0/3/k2:.4f}, t(150 °C) = {np.log(350/50)*rho*c*r0/(3*h2):.0f} s")
// Plane wall with convection: centre and surface temperature histories for several Bi
function eigs(Bi, n) { return M.roots(g => g*Math.sin(g) - Bi*Math.cos(g), 1e-6, n, 0.002); }  // γ tan γ = Bi without poles
function theta(Bi, Fo, xi) {
  const g = eigs(Bi, 30); let s = 0;
  g.forEach(gn => { s += 4*Math.sin(gn)/(2*gn + Math.sin(2*gn))*Math.cos(gn*xi)*Math.exp(-gn*gn*Fo); });
  return s;
}
const Fo = M.linspace(0.001, 2, 120), Bis = [0.1, 1, 10];
plot(Fo, Bis.map(b => Fo.map(f => theta(b, f, 0))), {xlabel:'Fo', ylabel:'θ0/θi (centre)', labels:Bis.map(b => 'Bi = '+b)});
print("quench example: θ0/θi at Fo = 1.805, Bi = 2:", theta(2, 1.805, 0), "(target 0.1453)");
