Lecture 24

Semi-infinite and infinite solids: the Fourier integral

A body is "semi-infinite" when one face matters and the far boundary never feels the disturbance on the time scale of interest. That is true when \(L\gg\sqrt{\alpha t}\). The ground under a frosty night, a thick steel ingot just after quench, skin touching a hot plate, and a weld bead's base metal all qualify. A slab of 1 m of concrete (\(\alpha\approx 7\times10^{-7}\) m²/s) behaves as semi-infinite for about \(t\lt L^2/(16\alpha)\approx 25\) hours.

On a semi-infinite domain the separation constant is no longer quantized. With no second wall, every \(\lambda\ge 0\) is allowed, and the Fourier series becomes a Fourier integral:

Fourier integral representations$$\begin{aligned} (-\infty,\infty):\ & f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x')\cos\lambda(x - x')\,dx'\,d\lambda\\ (0,\infty),\ f = 0\text{ at }0:\ & f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\sin\lambda x\int_0^\infty f(x')\sin\lambda x'\,dx'\,d\lambda\\ (0,\infty),\ f' = 0\text{ at }0:\ & f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\cos\lambda x\int_0^\infty f(x')\cos\lambda x'\,dx'\,d\lambda \end{aligned}$$

The discrete sum over \(n\) with spacing \(\Delta\lambda = \pi/L\) becomes, as \(L\to\infty\), an integral over a continuous spectrum. Each wavenumber \(\lambda\) still decays as \(e^{-\alpha\lambda^2t}\), and short wavelengths still disappear first.

Lecture 25

Cartesian problems: the error function and the half-plane

A semi-infinite solid at \(T_i\) has its surface suddenly set to \(T_s\). With \(\theta = (T - T_i)/(T_s - T_i)\), apply the sine-integral representation and evaluate the inner integral, which is a Gaussian. The result is one of the most used formulas in heat transfer:

Step change in surface temperature$$\frac{T - T_i}{T_s - T_i} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right),\qquad q''_s(t) = \frac{k\,(T_s - T_i)}{\sqrt{\pi\alpha t}},\qquad \operatorname{erf}z = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^ze^{-u^2}du$$

The surface flux is infinite at \(t = 0\) and decays like \(t^{-1/2}\). The two other classical surface conditions give:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{constant flux } q''_0:\ & T - T_i = \frac{2q''_0\sqrt{\alpha t/\pi}}{k}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\alpha t}\right) - \frac{q''_0x}{k}\operatorname{erfc}\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\\ \text{convection }(h, T_\infty):\ & \frac{T - T_i}{T_\infty - T_i} = \operatorname{erfc}\eta - \exp\!\left(\frac{hx}{k} + \frac{h^2\alpha t}{k^2}\right)\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\eta + \frac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k}\right),\quad \eta = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} \end{aligned}$$
Physical interpretation: the penetration depth

Since \(\operatorname{erfc}(2) = 0.005\), the disturbance has effectively not arrived beyond \(x\approx 4\sqrt{\alpha t}\). This thermal penetration depth grows like \(\sqrt t\). Doubling the depth takes four times as long. A frost wave that reaches 0.5 m in a month needs four months to reach 1 m. This square-root law is the signature of diffusion, as opposed to the linear-in-time travel of a wave or a flow.

Worked example (frozen water pipe). Dry soil at 20 °C (\(\alpha = 5\times10^{-4}\) m²/h) has its surface held at −15 °C for a month (720 h). How deep must a water pipe be? Set \((T - T_s)/(T_i - T_s) = \operatorname{erf}\eta = (0 + 15)/35 = 0.428\). Then \(\eta = 0.40\) and \(x = 2\eta\sqrt{\alpha t} = 0.8\sqrt{0.36} = 0.48\) m.

Two semi-infinite bodies in contact

Two bodies at \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) touch at \(t = 0\). Each responds with an erfc profile, and flux continuity at the interface fixes a contact temperature that is constant in time:

$$T_c = \frac{e_1T_1 + e_2T_2}{e_1 + e_2},\qquad e = \sqrt{k\rho c}\ \ (\text{thermal effusivity, } \mathrm{W\,s^{1/2}m^{-2}K^{-1}})$$
Physical interpretation

Your fingertip does not sense the temperature of what you touch. It senses the contact temperature, which is weighted by effusivity. Steel (\(e\approx 14\,000\)) pulls the interface almost to its own temperature. Wood (\(e\approx 400\)) lets your skin (\(e\approx 1\,800\)) dominate. At the same 20 °C, metal "feels cold" and wood "feels warm". The same physics explains why touching a cold-head at 80 K with a gloved hand is fine for a moment but bare skin freezes instantly.

Periodic surface temperature

If the surface oscillates as \(T_s = T_m + A\cos\omega t\), the long-time solution is a damped, lagging thermal wave:

$$T(x,t) = T_m + A\,e^{-x/d}\cos\!\left(\omega t - \frac{x}{d}\right),\qquad d = \sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{\omega}}$$
In the real world

For soil, the daily wave is damped to about 1% within roughly 0.5 m, while the annual wave reaches several metres and arrives months late. That is why cellars are cool in summer and warm in winter, and why buried water mains are laid below the frost line. The same formula sizes the wall thickness of a building's thermal mass, and gives the temperature swing in an engine cylinder wall at a combustion frequency of several kHz, where \(d\) is a few tens of micrometres.

2D steady state in a half-plane

A strip \(|x|\lt a\) on the surface \(y = 0\) of a half-plane is held at \(T_0\), and the rest of the surface at 0. The Fourier integral in \(x\) gives a closed form: the temperature is proportional to the angle the hot strip subtends at the point,

$$T(x,y) = \frac{T_0}{\pi}\left[\tan^{-1}\frac{a - x}{y} + \tan^{-1}\frac{a + x}{y}\right]$$

So isotherms are circular arcs through the strip's edges, and the flux lines are their orthogonal family. This is the steady field under a heated road, a hot electronic trace on a thick substrate, or a laser line on a large workpiece.

Lecture 26

Semi-infinite media in cylindrical and spherical coordinates

For an unbounded radial direction, the Fourier–Bessel series becomes the Fourier–Bessel (Hankel) integral:

$$f(r) = \int_0^\infty\lambda J_\nu(\lambda r)\int_0^\infty r'f(r')J_\nu(\lambda r')\,dr'\,d\lambda$$

An infinite medium with an initial temperature \(F(r)\) about an axis then evolves as \(T(r,t) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha\lambda^2t}\lambda J_0(\lambda r)\int_0^\infty r'F(r')J_0(\lambda r')dr'd\lambda\). For a hot cylinder of radius \(b\) at \(T_0\) in an infinite cold medium, this can be evaluated to show the centre temperature falling as \(1 - e^{-b^2/4\alpha t}\).

The region outside a spherical cavity, whose surface is suddenly raised to \(T_s\), is simpler. The substitution \(U = r\theta\) makes it a planar problem in \(r - r_0\):

Outside a sphere, step surface temperature$$\frac{T - T_i}{T_s - T_i} = \frac{r_0}{r}\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{r - r_0}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right),\qquad q''_s = \frac{k(T_s - T_i)}{r_0}\left[1 + \frac{r_0}{\sqrt{\pi\alpha t}}\right]$$
Physical interpretation

The planar solution never reaches a steady state: an erfc profile keeps penetrating forever, and its flux decays to zero. The spherical solution does reach one, \(T = r_0/r\), with a finite steady flux \(k\Delta T/r_0\). Spreading over an area that grows like \(r^2\) lets the far field absorb heat indefinitely at a fixed rate. The cylinder is the borderline case: its steady solution would be \(\ln r\), which diverges, so the flux decays only logarithmically slowly. This is why a long hot wire (a transient hot-wire conductivity probe) shows the characteristic \(\Delta T\propto\ln t\) used to measure \(k\). Select "outside a sphere" in the first simulation to see the steady state appear.

Lecture 27

The similarity method

The semi-infinite problem has no length scale of its own. The only way to make \(x\) dimensionless is with \(\sqrt{\alpha t}\). So the solution must depend on the single combination

$$\eta = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}},\qquad \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} = -\frac{\eta}{2t}\theta',\quad \frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\theta''}{4\alpha t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta'' + 2\eta\,\theta' = 0$$

The PDE in \((x, t)\) has become an ODE in \(\eta\). Integrating once gives \(\theta' = Ce^{-\eta^2}\), and again \(\theta = C_1\operatorname{erf}\eta + C_2\). The three original conditions \(\theta(x,0) = 0\), \(\theta(\infty,t) = 0\) and \(\theta(0,t) = 1\) collapse to two, \(\theta(\infty) = 0\) and \(\theta(0) = 1\), which is exactly what a second-order ODE needs. Ludwig Prandtl used the same idea in 1904 for the boundary layer.

When does similarity work?

When the initial and far-field conditions coincide, so that \(t = 0\) and \(x\to\infty\) both map to \(\eta\to\infty\), and when no other length scale enters. A finite slab, or a convective boundary with its length \(k/h\), breaks the similarity. The convective solution above therefore depends on two groups, \(\eta\) and \(h\sqrt{\alpha t}/k\). Phase-change problems (Module 8) are the most important class where similarity survives with a moving boundary.

Lecture 28

The Laplace transform method

The Laplace transform \(\bar\theta(x,s) = \int_0^\infty\theta(x,t)e^{-st}dt\) removes time. Since \(\mathcal L\{\partial\theta/\partial t\} = s\bar\theta - \theta(x,0)\), the diffusion equation with zero initial temperature becomes an ODE in \(x\):

$$\frac{d^2\bar\theta}{dx^2} - \frac{s}{\alpha}\bar\theta = 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar\theta = A(s)\,e^{-x\sqrt{s/\alpha}}\quad(\text{bounded as }x\to\infty)$$

The surface condition fixes \(A(s)\):

Surface condition\(\bar\theta(x,s)\)Inverse (from tables)
\(\theta(0,t) = \theta_s\)\(\dfrac{\theta_s}{s}e^{-x\sqrt{s/\alpha}}\)\(\theta_s\operatorname{erfc}\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\)
\(-k\theta_x(0,t) = q''_0\)\(\dfrac{q''_0\sqrt\alpha}{k\,s^{3/2}}e^{-x\sqrt{s/\alpha}}\)\(\dfrac{2q''_0\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k}\operatorname{ierfc}\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\)
\(-k\theta_x = h(\theta_\infty - \theta)\)\(\dfrac{\theta_\infty H}{s(H + \sqrt{s/\alpha})}e^{-x\sqrt{s/\alpha}}\), with \(H = h/k\)convection formula of Lecture 25

When no table entry exists, invert numerically. The Stehfest algorithm evaluates \(\bar\theta\) at a dozen real values of \(s\) and combines them with fixed weights. It is shown live, as orange dots, in the first simulation of Lecture 25, where it reproduces the analytical convective solution.

Intersections of 1D solutions

As with Heisler products in Module 4, a semi-infinite corner (a quarter-space \(x>0\), \(y>0\)) suddenly heated on both faces has the product solution \(\theta = 1 - [1 - \operatorname{erfc}\eta_x][1 - \operatorname{erfc}\eta_y]\), with the semi-infinite solution taking the place of the plate. Products of semi-infinite and finite-plate solutions describe the edge of a thick plate.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erf, erfc, erfinv
import mpmath as mp

# Frost-line example: soil at 20 °C, surface at -15 °C for 720 h, alpha = 5e-4 m²/h
Ti, Ts, Tf, alpha, t = 20, -15, 0, 5e-4, 720
eta = erfinv((Tf - Ts)/(Ti - Ts))
print(f"eta = {eta:.4f}  ->  burial depth = {2*eta*np.sqrt(alpha*t):.3f} m")

# Convective surface: analytic vs numerical Laplace inversion (Talbot via mpmath)
k, rho, c, h = 1.0, 2000, 1000, 20.0          # soil-like, W/m K etc.
a = k/(rho*c); H = h/k
def analytic(x, t):
    e = x/(2*np.sqrt(a*t))
    return erfc(e) - np.exp(H*x + H*H*a*t)*erfc(e + H*np.sqrt(a*t))
def lap(s, x):                                 # theta_bar for T_inf - Ti = 1
    return H/(s*(H + mp.sqrt(s/a)))*mp.exp(-x*mp.sqrt(s/a))
for x in (0.0, 0.05, 0.2):
    t = 3600*24
    num = float(mp.invertlaplace(lambda s: lap(s, x), t, method='talbot'))
    print(f"x = {x:4.2f} m: analytic {analytic(x, t):.6f}   Laplace-inverted {num:.6f}")
// Penetration of an erfc profile into steel, concrete and skin after 60 s
const mats = {steel:1.2e-5, concrete:7e-7, skin:1.1e-7};
const x = M.linspace(0, 0.06, 200), t = 60;
plot(x.map(v=>v*1000), Object.values(mats).map(a => x.map(v => M.erfc(v/(2*Math.sqrt(a*t))))),
     {xlabel:'depth (mm)', ylabel:'θ = (T−Ti)/(Ts−Ti)', labels:Object.keys(mats)});
for (const [m, a] of Object.entries(mats)) print(m, "penetration depth 4√(αt) =", (4*Math.sqrt(a*t)*1000).toFixed(1), "mm");
// Stehfest inversion of θ̄ = e^{-x√(s/α)}/s at x = 5 mm in steel:
const a = 1.2e-5, xx = 0.005;
print("Stehfest:", M.stehfest(s => Math.exp(-xx*Math.sqrt(s/a))/s, t), "  exact erfc:", M.erfc(xx/(2*Math.sqrt(a*t))));
