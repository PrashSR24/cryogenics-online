Lectures 40–41

Moving interface problems

Freezing lakes, casting metals, laser welding, latent-heat storage in buildings, ablation of re-entry shields, freeze-drying of food and cryosurgery all have a solid–liquid interface that moves as heat is conducted to or from it. Its location \(s(t)\) is unknown and must be found as part of the solution. That is what makes these Stefan problems nonlinear, even when every property is constant.

In each phase the ordinary heat equation holds. At the interface, two conditions apply: the temperature equals the fusion temperature, and an energy balance on the moving front, which can store nothing, links the jump in conduction flux to the latent heat released or absorbed:

Interface conditions (solidification, solid on the left)$$T_s(s,t) = T_\ell(s,t) = T_f,\qquad k_s\frac{\partial T_s}{\partial x}\bigg|_{s} - k_\ell\frac{\partial T_\ell}{\partial x}\bigg|_{s} = \rho L\frac{ds}{dt}\quad(\text{Stefan condition})$$

Heat conducted away through the solid exceeds heat arriving through the liquid. The difference is the latent heat liberated as the front advances by \(ds\). In cylindrical or spherical coordinates, replace \(x\) by \(r\). If the densities differ, the liquid moves to fill the volume change, which adds an advective term. Here we assume \(\rho_s = \rho_\ell\). With \(\theta = (T - T_f)/(T_f - T_0)\), \(\xi = x/L\) and \(\tau = \alpha_st/L^2\), the problem depends on

$$\mathrm{Ste} = \frac{c_s\,(T_f - T_0)}{L_f}\ \ (\text{Stefan number}),\qquad \frac{k_\ell}{k_s},\quad \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\alpha_s},\quad \text{superheat }\frac{T_i - T_f}{T_f - T_0}$$
Physical interpretation: the Stefan number

Ste is sensible heat over latent heat: how much energy it takes to cool the solid through the driving temperature difference, compared with the energy that must be removed to freeze it. Metals solidifying with modest undercooling, and phase-change materials (PCMs) in thermal storage that operate over a few kelvin, have small Ste. For them, the latent heat dominates and the front moves slowly compared with the speed at which the temperature field adjusts. Large Ste, such as freezing water against a very cold wall, means sensible heat matters and the front moves quickly.

Lecture 42

A single-region problem: Stefan’s exact solution

A solid initially at its melting temperature \(T_f\) occupies \(x>0\). At \(t = 0\) the face is raised to \(T_0>T_f\). Only the liquid has a temperature gradient; the solid stays at \(T_f\). Guided by Module 5, try \(\theta = A + B\operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha t})\) in the liquid. The interface condition \(T(s) = T_f\) for all \(t\) is possible only if \(s/2\sqrt{\alpha t}\) is constant:

$$s(t) = 2\lambda\sqrt{\alpha_\ell t},\qquad \frac{T - T_f}{T_0 - T_f} = 1 - \frac{\operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha_\ell t})}{\operatorname{erf}\lambda}$$

Inserting into the Stefan condition gives a transcendental equation for \(\lambda\):

Stefan’s eigenvalue equation$$\lambda\,e^{\lambda^2}\operatorname{erf}\lambda = \frac{\mathrm{Ste}}{\sqrt\pi},\qquad \mathrm{Ste} = \frac{c_\ell(T_0 - T_f)}{L_f}$$

For small Ste, \(\lambda\approx\sqrt{\mathrm{Ste}/2}\), so \(s\approx\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Ste}\,\alpha t}\). This is exactly the quasi-steady result obtained by assuming a linear temperature profile across the melt at each instant and integrating \(k(T_0 - T_f)/s = \rho L\,ds/dt\).

Physical interpretation

The front advances like \(\sqrt t\) for the same reason a diffusion front does: as the melt layer thickens, its conduction resistance grows and the heat supply to the front falls as \(1/s\). Melting the second centimetre takes three times as long as the first. The quasi-steady model works when the temperature field relaxes much faster than the front moves. That is the small-Ste limit: the quasi-steady front position is within 2% of the exact one at Ste = 0.1 and about 8% at Ste = 0.5.

Lecture 43

A two-region problem: Neumann’s exact solution

A liquid at uniform \(T_i > T_f\) fills \(x>0\). At \(t = 0\) its face is cooled to \(T_0\lt T_f\). A solid layer grows from the wall while the superheated liquid ahead of it also cools. The similarity form of Module 5 still works in both phases:

$$\frac{T_s - T_0}{T_f - T_0} = \frac{\operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha_st})}{\operatorname{erf}\lambda},\qquad \frac{T_\ell - T_i}{T_f - T_i} = \frac{\operatorname{erfc}(x/2\sqrt{\alpha_\ell t})}{\operatorname{erfc}(\lambda\sqrt{\alpha_s/\alpha_\ell})},\qquad s = 2\lambda\sqrt{\alpha_st}$$

The Stefan condition then fixes \(\lambda\):

Neumann’s equation$$\frac{e^{-\lambda^2}}{\operatorname{erf}\lambda} - \frac{k_\ell}{k_s}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_s}{\alpha_\ell}}\,\frac{T_i - T_f}{T_f - T_0}\,\frac{e^{-\lambda^2\alpha_s/\alpha_\ell}}{\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\lambda\sqrt{\alpha_s/\alpha_\ell}\right)} = \frac{\lambda\sqrt\pi}{\mathrm{Ste}},\qquad \mathrm{Ste} = \frac{c_s(T_f - T_0)}{L_f}$$

With no superheat (\(T_i = T_f\)), the second term vanishes and the equation reduces to Stefan's. Superheat always slows freezing, because the front must also remove the sensible heat delivered by the liquid. The simulation overlays a finite-difference enthalpy-method solution, the workhorse numerical method for phase change, so you can check the exact solution against brute force.

In the real world

Lake ice thickens roughly as \(\sqrt{\text{freezing degree-days}}\). This is Stefan's \(\sqrt t\) law, used by engineers to judge when ice will bear a vehicle. In continuous casting of steel, the shell thickness follows \(s = K\sqrt t\), with K of order 25–30 mm/min\(^{1/2}\), a direct descendant of Neumann's solution, and it sets the casting speed so that the shell is strong enough at the mould exit.

Lecture 44

Similarity and quasi-steady approximations, and radial geometries

Exact similarity solutions exist only for semi-infinite planar domains with constant conditions. For everything else, such as finite slabs, convective cooling or cylinders, the quasi-steady approximation is the engineer's tool. Assume the frozen layer's temperature profile is the steady one for its current thickness, then integrate the Stefan condition in time.

Plane wall with convective cooling. A liquid at \(T_f\) is cooled through its face by a fluid at \(T_\infty\) with coefficient \(h\). The series resistances are \(s/k\) (ice) and \(1/h\) (film):

$$\rho L\frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{T_f - T_\infty}{s/k + 1/h}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \frac{\rho L}{T_f - T_\infty}\left(\frac{s^2}{2k} + \frac{s}{h}\right)$$

The front starts out linear in time, limited by the film, and becomes \(\sqrt t\) once the ice dominates the resistance.

Freezing outward from a cold cylinder (cryoprobe, LNG pipeline, ground freezing). A probe of radius \(r_0\) at \(T_p\) sits in tissue or liquid at \(T_f\). The steady radial profile through the ice is logarithmic:

Quasi-steady radial freezing$$\rho L\,R\,\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{k\,(T_f - T_p)}{\ln(R/r_0)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t(R) = \frac{\rho L}{k\,(T_f - T_p)}\left[\frac{R^2}{2}\ln\frac{R}{r_0} - \frac{R^2 - r_0^2}{4}\right]$$
Cryogenic angle

In cryosurgery, a liquid-nitrogen probe at about −196 °C freezes tissue around it. The radius of the lethal zone (below about −40 °C) is smaller than the visible ice ball (0 °C), and both grow ever more slowly with time because of the \(\ln(R/r_0)\) resistance. Blood perfusion (Module 11) eventually balances the heat extraction and the ice ball stops growing. Surgeons use repeated freeze–thaw cycles rather than one long freeze, because the gain from a longer single freeze is so small.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erf, erfc
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# --- Neumann solution: freezing of water from a cold wall ---
ks, kl = 2.22, 0.556            # ice, water  W/m K
as_, al = 1.15e-6, 1.33e-7      # m²/s
cs, Lf = 2040.0, 334e3          # J/kg K, J/kg
Tf, T0, Ti = 0.0, -10.0, 5.0
Ste = cs*(Tf - T0)/Lf
nu = np.sqrt(as_/al)
f = lambda lam: (np.exp(-lam**2)/erf(lam)
                 - kl/ks*nu*(Ti - Tf)/(Tf - T0)*np.exp(-(lam*nu)**2)/erfc(lam*nu)
                 - lam*np.sqrt(np.pi)/Ste)
lam = brentq(f, 1e-6, 3)
print(f"Ste = {Ste:.4f}, lambda = {lam:.5f}")
for hrs in (1, 6, 24):
    print(f"ice thickness after {hrs:2d} h: {2*lam*np.sqrt(as_*hrs*3600)*1000:.1f} mm")

# --- Enthalpy method check (explicit, 1D) ---
N, Lx = 400, 0.2; dx = Lx/N; x = (np.arange(N)+0.5)*dx
rho = 1000.0; cl = 4200.0
H = rho*(cl*(Ti - Tf) + Lf)*np.ones(N)                         # liquid enthalpy, ref = solid at Tf
def T_of_H(H):
    return np.where(H < 0, H/(rho*cs) + Tf, np.where(H > rho*Lf, (H - rho*Lf)/(rho*cl) + Tf, Tf))
k_of_H = lambda H: np.where(H < rho*Lf/2, ks, kl)
dt = 0.2*dx**2*rho*cs/ks; t = 0.0
while t < 6*3600:
    T = T_of_H(H); k = k_of_H(H)
    kf = 2*k[1:]*k[:-1]/(k[1:] + k[:-1])                        # harmonic face conductivity
    q = np.empty(N+1); q[1:-1] = -kf*(T[1:] - T[:-1])/dx
    q[0] = -ks*(T[0] - T0)/(dx/2); q[-1] = 0
    H -= dt*(q[1:] - q[:-1])/dx; t += dt
front = x[np.argmax(T_of_H(H) >= Tf - 1e-9)]
print(f"enthalpy method front at 6 h: {front*1000:.1f} mm  (Neumann: {2*lam*np.sqrt(as_*6*3600)*1000:.1f} mm)")
// Stefan problem: λ e^{λ²} erf(λ) = Ste/√π, compared with the quasi-steady λ = √(Ste/2)
const lam = Ste => M.bisect(l => l*Math.exp(l*l)*M.erf(l) - Ste/Math.sqrt(Math.PI), 1e-9, 4);
const St = M.linspace(0.01, 3, 120);
plot(St, [St.map(lam), St.map(s => Math.sqrt(s/2))], {xlabel:'Ste', ylabel:'λ', labels:['exact (Stefan)', 'quasi-steady √(Ste/2)']});
[0.1, 0.5, 1, 2].forEach(s => print("Ste =", s, " λ =", lam(s), " quasi-steady error:", ((Math.sqrt(s/2)/lam(s)-1)*100).toFixed(2)+" %"));
