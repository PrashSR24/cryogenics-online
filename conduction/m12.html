Lecture 60

Introduction to numerical solution

Analytical methods give insight and exact benchmarks, but real problems often have irregular geometry, temperature-dependent properties, radiation boundaries, or coupled phenomena. Numerical methods replace the differential equation by algebraic equations for the temperature at a finite set of points (nodes). The main families are finite differences (FDM), finite volumes (FVM, which conserve energy cell by cell and are the basis of most CFD codes) and finite elements (FEM, the Galerkin method of Module 10 on small elements).

Finite-difference approximations from Taylor series

$$T_{i\pm1} = T_i \pm \Delta x\,T'_i + \frac{\Delta x^2}{2}T''_i \pm \frac{\Delta x^3}{6}T'''_i + \dots$$
Basic difference formulas$$T'_i\approx\frac{T_{i+1}-T_i}{\Delta x}\ \ \mathcal O(\Delta x)\ \text{(forward)},\qquad T'_i\approx\frac{T_{i+1}-T_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}\ \ \mathcal O(\Delta x^2)\ \text{(central)},\qquad T''_i\approx\frac{T_{i+1}-2T_i+T_{i-1}}{\Delta x^2}\ \ \mathcal O(\Delta x^2)$$

The leading neglected term is the truncation error. Halving \(\Delta x\) halves a first-order error and quarters a second-order one, until round-off takes over at very small \(\Delta x\), because you are subtracting nearly equal numbers.

Physical interpretation: the energy-balance view

The central-difference equation at node \(i\), \(k(T_{i+1} - 2T_i + T_{i-1})/\Delta x^2 + \dot q = 0\), is also a heat balance on a control volume of width \(\Delta x\) around the node. The heat conducted in from the left, \(k(T_{i-1}-T_i)/\Delta x\), plus that from the right, plus the heat generated, sums to zero. Each link between nodes is a thermal resistor \(\Delta x/kA\). Writing nodal equations as energy balances is the safest way to derive boundary nodes, interfaces, and variable-\(k\) cases, and it guarantees conservation.

Lecture 61

Finite differences for 1D steady problems

A pin fin of length \(L\) with base at \(\theta_b\) and a convective tip, \(\theta'' - m^2\theta = 0\). With \(N\) intervals and \(\Delta x = L/N\), interior nodes give

$$\theta_{i-1} - (2 + m^2\Delta x^2)\,\theta_i + \theta_{i+1} = 0,\qquad i = 1,\dots,N-1$$

The tip node \(N\) owns half a control volume. Its energy balance, conduction in from node \(N-1\) equal to convection off the half-volume's side and off the tip face, is

$$\frac{kA_c}{\Delta x}(\theta_{N-1} - \theta_N) - hP\frac{\Delta x}{2}\theta_N - hA_c\theta_N = 0$$

The system is tridiagonal, and the Thomas algorithm (TDMA) solves it in \(\mathcal O(N)\) operations by forward elimination and back substitution. A million-node 1D problem solves in milliseconds.

Lecture 62

Finite differences for 2D steady problems

On a uniform grid with \(\Delta x = \Delta y\), Laplace's equation becomes the five-point rule: each interior temperature is the average of its four neighbours.

$$T_{i,j} = \tfrac14\left(T_{i+1,j} + T_{i-1,j} + T_{i,j+1} + T_{i,j-1}\right) + \frac{\dot q\Delta x^2}{4k}$$

For \(n\times n\) nodes this is a sparse system of \(n^2\) equations. Direct sparse solvers work well up to a few million unknowns. Iterative methods are simple and memory-light:

  • Jacobi: update every node from the old values. Simple, but slow.
  • Gauss–Seidel: use new values as soon as they are available. About twice as fast as Jacobi.
  • Successive over-relaxation (SOR): \(T^{new} = T^{old} + \omega(T^{GS} - T^{old})\) with \(1\lt\omega\lt2\). With the optimal \(\omega\approx 2/(1 + \sin(\pi/n))\), the iteration count drops from \(\mathcal O(n^2)\) to \(\mathcal O(n)\).
Physical interpretation

Watch the Jacobi iterations. Information spreads one node per sweep, much like a diffusion process in "iteration time". Boundary values must seep across the whole grid, which takes about \(n^2\) sweeps, the discrete version of the diffusion time \(L^2/\alpha\). Over-relaxation deliberately overshoots, anticipating where each node is heading, and propagates information far faster. Multigrid methods go further by smoothing the error on coarse grids, where long wavelengths are cheap to remove. They reach \(\mathcal O(n^2)\) total work, optimal for a 2D grid.

Lecture 63

Transient problems: explicit, implicit and Crank–Nicolson

With time levels \(p\), \(\Delta t\) and the mesh Fourier number \(\mathrm{Fo} = \alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2\), the 1D heat equation can be discretised three classical ways:

Time-marching schemes$$\begin{aligned} \text{explicit (FTCS):}\quad & T_i^{p+1} = \mathrm{Fo}\,(T_{i+1}^p + T_{i-1}^p) + (1 - 2\mathrm{Fo})\,T_i^p\\ \text{fully implicit:}\quad & -\mathrm{Fo}\,T_{i-1}^{p+1} + (1 + 2\mathrm{Fo})\,T_i^{p+1} - \mathrm{Fo}\,T_{i+1}^{p+1} = T_i^p\\ \text{Crank–Nicolson:}\quad & \text{average of the two, second order in }\Delta t \end{aligned}$$

Stability. The explicit scheme requires \(1 - 2\mathrm{Fo}\ge0\), i.e. \(\mathrm{Fo}\le\tfrac12\) in 1D (\(\tfrac14\) in 2D, \(\tfrac16\) in 3D). Otherwise the coefficient of \(T_i^p\) is negative, which says a hotter node becomes colder than its neighbours: a violation of the second law that grows into oscillations. Von Neumann analysis gives the amplification factor \(G = 1 - 4\mathrm{Fo}\sin^2(\kappa\Delta x/2)\), and \(|G|\le1\) for all wavenumbers requires \(\mathrm{Fo}\le\tfrac12\). Implicit schemes are unconditionally stable. Crank–Nicolson can still produce damped oscillations near discontinuities when Fo is large.

Choosing a scheme

Explicit: trivial to code, good for short transients and nonlinear problems, but \(\Delta t\propto\Delta x^2\) makes fine meshes expensive. Implicit: one tridiagonal solve per step and any \(\Delta t\), but only first-order accurate in time. Crank–Nicolson: second order at the same cost, the default for smooth problems. For production work, use the method of lines: discretise space and hand the ODE system to an adaptive stiff integrator (BDF), as MATLAB's pdepe and SciPy's solve_ivp do.

Lecture 64

Built-in solvers: pdepe, bvp4c and their Python equivalents

pdepe solves 1D parabolic and elliptic systems of the form

$$c\!\left(x,t,u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = x^{-m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(x^mf\!\left(x,t,u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)\right) + s\!\left(x,t,u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right),\qquad p + q\,f = 0\ \text{at each end}$$

with \(m = 0, 1, 2\) for slab, cylinder and sphere. For conduction, \(c = \rho c_p\), \(f = k\,\partial T/\partial x\), \(s = \dot q\). A convective boundary \(-k\,\partial_xT = h(T - T_\infty)\) at the right end becomes \(p_r = h(T - T_\infty)\), \(q_r = 1\). bvp4c solves two-point boundary-value ODEs, written as first-order systems, by collocation. A classic example is a nonlinear fin losing heat by both convection and radiation:

$$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} = h'(T - T_\infty) + \sigma'(T^4 - T_\infty^4),\quad h' = 0.05\ \mathrm{m^{-2}},\ \sigma' = 2.7\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{K^{-3}m^{-2}},\ T_\infty = 200\ \mathrm K,\ T(0) = 300,\ T(10) = 400\ \mathrm K$$
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_bvp, solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- bvp4c equivalent: nonlinear convective-radiative rod ---
h, sig, Tinf = 0.05, 2.7e-9, 200.0
f  = lambda x, y: np.vstack([y[1], h*(y[0]-Tinf) + sig*(y[0]**4 - Tinf**4)])
bc = lambda ya, yb: np.array([ya[0]-300.0, yb[0]-400.0])
x = np.linspace(0, 10, 50)
sol = solve_bvp(f, bc, x, np.vstack([np.linspace(300, 400, 50), np.full(50, 10.0)]))
print("bvp converged:", sol.success, "  T(5) =", float(sol.sol(5.0)[0]), "K")

# --- pdepe equivalent: method of lines, convective cylinder (m = 1) ---
k, rho, cp, hc, Tf, r0 = 15.0, 8000.0, 500.0, 200.0, 20.0, 0.02
N = 60; r = np.linspace(0, r0, N); dr = r[1] - r[0]; alpha = k/(rho*cp)
def rhs(t, T):
    d = np.empty_like(T)
    # r = 0: symmetry -> 1/r d/dr(r dT/dr) -> 2 d2T/dr2
    d[0] = alpha*4*(T[1] - T[0])/dr**2
    rp, rm = r[1:-1] + dr/2, r[1:-1] - dr/2
    d[1:-1] = alpha*(rp*(T[2:] - T[1:-1]) - rm*(T[1:-1] - T[:-2]))/(r[1:-1]*dr**2)
    # convective surface: half control volume energy balance
    qin = k*(r0 - dr/2)*(T[-2] - T[-1])/dr
    d[-1] = (qin - hc*r0*(T[-1] - Tf))/(rho*cp*(r0*dr/2 - dr**2/8))
    return d
s = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 600), np.full(N, 400.0), method='BDF', t_eval=[0, 60, 180, 600])
for t, T in zip(s.t, s.y.T): plt.plot(r*1e3, T, label=f't = {t:.0f} s')
plt.xlabel('r (mm)'); plt.ylabel('T (°C)'); plt.legend(); plt.title('Method of lines (pdepe-style) cooling of a steel rod')
print("centre temperature after 600 s:", s.y[0, -1])
% ---- pdepe: transient conduction in a slab, convective right face ----
m = 0; x = linspace(0, 0.05, 51); t = linspace(0, 600, 61);
sol = pdepe(m, @heatpde, @heatic, @heatbc, x, t);
surf(x, t, sol(:,:,1)); xlabel('x (m)'); ylabel('t (s)'); zlabel('T (°C)');

function [c, f, s] = heatpde(x, t, u, dudx)
    k = 15; rho = 8000; cp = 500;
    c = rho*cp; f = k*dudx; s = 0;
end
function u0 = heatic(x)
    u0 = 400;
end
function [pl, ql, pr, qr] = heatbc(xl, ul, xr, ur, t)
    h = 200; Tinf = 20;
    pl = 0;            ql = 1;        % insulated (symmetry) at x = 0
    pr = h*(ur - Tinf); qr = 1;       % -k dT/dx = h (T - Tinf) at x = L
end

% ---- bvp4c: nonlinear convective-radiative rod ----
solinit = bvpinit(linspace(0, 10, 50), [300 10]);
sol = bvp4c(@(x,y)[y(2); 0.05*(y(1)-200) + 2.7e-9*(y(1)^4 - 200^4)], ...
            @(ya,yb)[ya(1)-300; yb(1)-400], solinit);
xv = linspace(0,10); yv = deval(sol, xv); plot(xv, yv(1,:)); xlabel('x (m)'); ylabel('T (K)');
// Live: nonlinear rod T'' = h'(T−T∞) + σ'(T⁴−T∞⁴) by finite differences + Newton iteration
const h = 0.05, s = 2.7e-9, Ti = 200, N = 100, L = 10, dx = L/N;
let T = M.linspace(300, 400, N+1);
for (let it = 0; it < 20; it++) {
  const a = new Float64Array(N+1), b = new Float64Array(N+1), c = new Float64Array(N+1), d = new Float64Array(N+1);
  b[0] = 1; d[0] = 0; b[N] = 1; d[N] = 0;                  // Newton corrections vanish at fixed ends
  let res = 0;
  for (let i = 1; i < N; i++) {
    const F = (T[i-1] - 2*T[i] + T[i+1])/(dx*dx) - h*(T[i]-Ti) - s*(T[i]**4 - Ti**4);
    a[i] = 1/(dx*dx); c[i] = 1/(dx*dx); b[i] = -2/(dx*dx) - h - 4*s*T[i]**3; d[i] = -F; res = Math.max(res, Math.abs(F));
  }
  const dT = M.tdma(a, b, c, d); T = T.map((v, i) => v + dT[i]);
  if (res < 1e-10) { print("Newton converged in", it, "iterations"); break; }
}
print("T(5 m) =", T[N/2].toFixed(3), "K");
plot(M.linspace(0, L, N+1), T, {xlabel:'x (m)', ylabel:'T (K)', labels:['FD + Newton']});
Lecture 65

PDE toolboxes and finite elements for 2D/3D conduction

For irregular 2D and 3D geometries, finite-element toolboxes such as MATLAB's PDE Toolbox, FEniCS, Elmer, COMSOL and ANSYS Mechanical handle meshing, assembly and solution. They solve the general form \(m\ddot u + d\dot u - \nabla\cdot(c\nabla u) + au = f\). For heat conduction, \(d = \rho c_p\), \(c = k\) (a tensor for anisotropic media, Module 11), \(a = 0\) and \(f = \dot q\). The workflow is the same in all of them: geometry, material properties, boundary and initial conditions, mesh, solve, post-process. Always check mesh independence, and validate against an analytical benchmark. Every exact solution in Modules 3–9 is a ready-made benchmark.

# Transient 2D conduction in a plate with a hole-like cold region, implicit Euler on a sparse grid
import numpy as np, scipy.sparse as sp, scipy.sparse.linalg as spla
n, L, alpha, dt = 60, 0.1, 1e-5, 2.0
dx = L/(n-1); Fo = alpha*dt/dx**2
X, Y = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, L, n), np.linspace(0, L, n))
fixed = (X == 0) | (X == L) | (Y == 0) | (Y == L) | ((X-0.05)**2 + (Y-0.05)**2 < 0.015**2)
Tfix = np.where((X-0.05)**2 + (Y-0.05)**2 < 0.015**2, 0.0, 100.0)
idx = np.arange(n*n).reshape(n, n)
rows, cols, vals = [], [], []
for j in range(n):
    for i in range(n):
        p = idx[j, i]
        if fixed[j, i]:
            rows.append(p); cols.append(p); vals.append(1.0); continue
        rows += [p]*5; cols += [p, idx[j, i-1], idx[j, i+1], idx[j-1, i], idx[j+1, i]]
        vals += [1 + 4*Fo, -Fo, -Fo, -Fo, -Fo]
A = sp.csr_matrix((vals, (rows, cols)), shape=(n*n, n*n)); lu = spla.splu(A.tocsc())
T = np.where(fixed, Tfix, 50.0).ravel()
for step in range(300):
    rhs = np.where(fixed.ravel(), Tfix.ravel(), T); T = lu.solve(rhs)
print("temperature at (0.025, 0.05) after 600 s:", T.reshape(n, n)[n//2, n//4].round(2), "°C")
% MATLAB PDE Toolbox: transient conduction in a square plate with a cold circular hole
thermalmodel = createpde('thermal','transient');
R = [3 4 0 0.1 0.1 0 0 0 0.1 0.1]'; C = [1 0.05 0.05 0.015 0 0 0 0 0 0]';
gd = [R C]; ns = char('R','C')'; sf = 'R-C';
geometryFromEdges(thermalmodel, decsg(gd, sf, ns));
thermalProperties(thermalmodel, 'ThermalConductivity', 15, 'MassDensity', 8000, 'SpecificHeat', 500);
thermalBC(thermalmodel, 'Edge', 1:4, 'Temperature', 100);       % outer edges
thermalBC(thermalmodel, 'Edge', 5:8, 'Temperature', 0);         % hole
thermalIC(thermalmodel, 50);
generateMesh(thermalmodel, 'Hmax', 0.003);
tlist = 0:10:600;
res = solve(thermalmodel, tlist);
pdeplot(thermalmodel, 'XYData', res.Temperature(:, end), 'Contour', 'on'); axis equal
title('T at t = 600 s')
In the real world

The workflow in this module, discretise, solve, verify against an exact solution, and check grid convergence, is what separates a trustworthy thermal simulation from a colourful picture. The same steps apply when you move to OpenFOAM (finite volume) for conjugate heat transfer and sloshing problems, or to FEM codes for thermal stress. They also apply to emerging physics-informed neural networks, where the residual of the heat equation becomes the loss function, a direct descendant of the weighted-residual idea in Module 10.
