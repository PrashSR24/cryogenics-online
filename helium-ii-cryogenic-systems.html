Module 11 · Systems and storage

Helium II cryogenic systems

How a 2 K plant is built: pumping the vapour down with cold and warm compressors to make superfluid helium for accelerators.

Three ways to make He II

Above the lambda line the liquid is He I; below it, He II, with very low viscosity and huge heat conductivity, widely used to cool large superconducting magnets and accelerator cavities. Three routes lead there, depending on what you want:

  • Pump on a bath of He I. Lower the vapour pressure and the liquid follows the saturation line to a saturated He II bath.
  • Cool a sealed bath at constant pressure (typically above 1 bar) by convection, giving a pressurised He II bath. Large systems such as the LHC cool the pressurised bath with a two-phase He II bayonet heat exchanger.
  • Joule–Thomson expansion of normal or supercritical helium into two-phase He II, which is saturated. A JT valve drops the supply to about 3.1 kPa, equivalent to 2.0 K, and counterflow heat exchangers use the cold vapour to pre-cool the incoming stream. Pre-cooling matters: the less warm the stream entering the valve, the more of it stays liquid after the isenthalpic expansion.

A He II plant uses the same warm compressors, oil removal, purifiers, cold box (valves, heat exchangers, turbo-expanders), controls and distribution as a 4.5 K plant; the extra equipment is on the return path, to compress the sub-atmospheric vapour back up to ambient pressure.

Common equipment

  • Warm compressors: reciprocating or oil-flooded screw machines (twin helical rotors), followed by an oil removal system.
  • Turbo-expanders in the cold box (gas-bearing turbines) and reciprocating expanders, as in all helium liquefiers.
  • Recuperative heat exchangers. An ideal one recovers all the enthalpy (the two streams leave at the same temperature as the other stream's inlet, with no axial conduction and no pressure drop). Real ones have an effectiveness below one, conduct heat along the exchanger and drop pressure, and these losses are what set the plant efficiency.
  • Purifiers and cryogenic control valves.

Pumping the vapour down

Pumping to a low vapour pressure is the heart of a He II plant. Three arrangements:

SchemeStrengthsWeaknesses
Room-temperature pumping (Roots blower plus liquid-ring pump)Low capital cost, naturally stable, easy turn-downLimited capacity, high maintenance and operating cost, low reliability, limited run time
Cold compression (stages of cold compressors in series)Unlimited cryogenic power, low maintenance, high reliability, low running costNarrow turn-down, delicate stability, higher capital cost
Hybrid (cold compressors plus warm vacuum pumps)Combines both; wider turn-downMore complex

A warm Roots blower handles about 20,000 normal m³/h at 10⁻⁴ to 20 torr, and a liquid-ring pump about 4,000 normal m³/h at 20 to 760 torr; oil injection takes away the heat of compression, and the machines are reworked for helium with helium-guarded seals. Cold compressors are high-speed turbo machines (40,000 to 100,000 rpm) with gas, ceramic ball or magnetic bearings, each giving a pressure ratio of about 2 to 4, so several are stacked in series.

The load and the mass flow are linked by Q = ṁ × Hvap. The calculator uses Hvap = 23 J/g, a typical value for He II near 2 K taken from standard property tables rather than from the lecture. The suction pressures are the lecture's. For a plant like the LHC sector (2.4 kW at 1.8 K) it finds a vapour flow of about 100 g/s and about four cold-compressor stages to reach atmosphere, matching the four-stage cold-compression units shown in the lecture.

Cold versus hybrid: turn-down and temperature

Because Q = ṁ Hvap, capacity control is a mass-flow problem. A pure cold-compressor cycle keeps a nearly constant pressure ratio, so the flow can only vary over a narrow band: about 75% to 100% of the design. A hybrid cycle with a variable pressure ratio allows about 50% to 100%. The hybrid also reaches colder:

SchemeBath temperature and suction pressure
Cold compressors only2.0 K at 3130 Pa; 1.96 K at 2780 Pa; 1.92 K at 2450 Pa
Hybrid2.0 K at 3130 Pa; 1.9 K at 2300 Pa; 1.8 K at 1640 Pa

In the lecture's comparison a cold-only plant cannot reach 1.9 K, while a hybrid supports any level from 1.8 K to 2.0 K.
