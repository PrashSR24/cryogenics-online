Module 14 · Experimental cryogenics

Lab: Building a cryostat, step by step

From a pile of parts to a vacuum-tight cryostat: what goes in, in what order, and how to estimate the heat load before you cut any metal.

About this module. The lessons so far explained the physics. This module is the bench side: how an experiment is actually built, tested and run. It is general laboratory practice, not taken from the lectures, so treat the numbers as planning values and follow your own institution's safety rules and the maker's manuals for every pump, sensor and cryogen.

Start with the heat budget, not the drawing

A cryostat is a cold object kept apart from a warm room. Everything you build is an answer to one question: how much heat reaches the cold part, and can your cooling source remove it? Heat gets in by three routes, and a good design lists all three before any metal is cut.

  • Solid conduction down supports, fill tubes and wires. It is set by the area, the length and the thermal conductivity integral of the material between the warm and cold ends, so thin, long, low-conductivity parts win.
  • Radiation from the warm walls. It falls with surface emissivity and with the number of reflective layers or intercepting shields between the two surfaces.
  • Residual gas. Below about 10-5 mbar this is usually negligible; above it, gas molecules carry heat across the gap in proportion to the pressure (see the gas conduction lesson).

Write down the target first: the temperature, the size of the sample, how many wires you need, and how long you want it to hold. The heat-budget calculator further down turns those choices into watts and into litres of cryogen per hour.

The assembly order

Most laboratory cryostats are built from the inside out, hanging from the top flange so that everything can be inspected and then enclosed. Press Play, or drag the slider, and watch the build.

  1. Hang the vessel. The inner vessel hangs from the top flange on a thin-walled stainless-steel tube, which doubles as the fill and vent line. Stainless steel conducts heat poorly, and a thin wall means a small cross-section.
  2. Fit the sensors and the heater. Put thermometers where you want to know the temperature, in a copper block or plate for good thermal contact. Use a four-wire connection, a non-inductive heater, and a thin film of vacuum grease or varnish for contact. Log the room-temperature resistance of every device now; it is your first check later.
  3. Route the wires. Use fine wire of a low-conductivity alloy on the long runs (manganin or constantan), copper where you need low resistance, and wrap each run around a copper post at every temperature stage, the thermal anchor. Leave a little slack for thermal contraction.
  4. Wrap the radiation shield. Bolt the shield to a cold anchor and wrap it in multilayer insulation: many thin aluminised polyester layers kept apart by a loose spacer. Do not press the layers tight, because contact between layers carries heat. Perforate or vent the layers so the gas between them can pump out.
  5. Close the vacuum can. Clean the sealing faces, fit a fresh O-ring or indium wire, and tighten the bolts in a star pattern, a little at a time. Check that no part touches the can wall: a single contact is a thermal short.
  6. Pump and leak test. The next lesson covers this in detail. Do not fill with cryogen until the vacuum is good.
  7. Fill. Precool, then fill slowly, as described in the cooldown lesson.

Heat-budget calculator

Set up your own cryostat. The calculator uses power-law fits to the thermal conductivity of each material, k = k300(T/300)n, and the radiation formula with floating shields, plus the free-molecular gas conduction formula from the gas conduction lesson. All material constants are rough handbook values from outside the lectures and are good to about ±30%: enough to size a design, not to replace a measured value.

Notice the pattern. At 77 K the radiation usually dominates until you add shield layers; at 4.2 K the same wires and supports cost far less per watt but each watt boils away about 60 times more liquid, so the budget is much tighter. Try the copper support tube: its conduction dwarfs everything else, which is exactly why no cryostat hangs from copper tubes.

Check before you close the can

  • Everything is clean and free of oil and finger grease: gloves, then a solvent wipe.
  • Every sensor and heater has been checked at room temperature and its value logged; every wire is checked for shorts to ground.
  • No wire, support or shield touches the vacuum can. Look all round with a torch.
  • Screws that end in blind holes are vented (slotted or drilled), or gas trapped behind them will leak out slowly for weeks.
  • Photograph the assembly and write the build into the logbook. When something misbehaves at 4 K, you will want to know exactly how it was built.
