Module 11 · Systems and storage

Designing a cryogenic system

From requirements and interfaces to technology choice, build-or-buy and commissioning, with a worked cost comparison.

A process, not a calculation

The thermodynamics gets you a heat load and a cycle, but delivering a big cryogenic system from start to finish is systems engineering. The ideal order is requirements, interfaces, strategy (make or buy, prototyping, technology), cost and schedule estimate, detailed design, procurement and manufacturing, installation and commissioning. The real process is multidimensional: each step feeds back to the earlier ones, and the main message of the lecture is to keep stakeholders engaged and to separate requirements from wishes.

The stages run left to right and top to bottom; the dashed loop shows that real projects iterate.

Requirements

Requirements are tiered and traceable up and down: high-level programmatic, scientific, engineering and conventional-facilities requirements, flowing down from stakeholders (scientists, funding agency, laboratory, advisory panels). A requirements database records for each item its ID, type, name, text, rationale, value, status, and its parents and children, so the origin of every number can be followed. They fall into groups:

  • Process: operating temperature and pressure, allowable heat leak, expected heat load, coolant flow and allowable pressure drop.
  • Safety and regulatory: loads from pressure and temperature, maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP), minimum design metal temperature (MDMT), size and weight for transport, compliance with design codes (ASME, CGA, API, EN).
  • Instrumentation: warm or cold; temperature, pressure, flow; how many, where, what range and sensitivity, what readout.
  • Environment and infrastructure: magnetic fields, radiation, access; electrical power, cooling water, HVAC, compressed air; lifting, rigging and transport.

Constraints to keep in mind from the start: suitability of materials for cryogenic use, residual magnetism and radiopurity, the effect of materials on purity in a liquid-argon detector (does the material outgas or poison the lifetime, has it been tested?), schedule and cost limits, and logistics (weight, size, transport, underground access).

Interfaces and the statement of work

An interface is a boundary with another system (detector and cryogenics, cryogenics and cryogenics, or project and vendor) covering electrical, mechanical, instrumentation, controls and utilities, with location, type, size and who supplies what. They are documented with interface control documents, tables, drawings and three-dimensional envelopes. The requirements and interfaces for anything that is bought or built go into a statement of work together with applicable codes and standards, deliverables, quality assurance and environment, safety and health provisions, whatever the acquisition strategy.

Strategy: where judgement is needed

Technology choice, design in-house or by industry, prototyping, and procurement approach depend on cost, available resources, complexity, experience and what gives the best chance of success. The lecture's example is a large liquid-argon detector programme needing 4 × 100 kW of cooling for about 20 years, running 24/7.

Open-loop liquid nitrogen is simple, but the lecture's numbers show why it is not feasible here: about 2050 g/s of liquid nitrogen, about $10.8 million a year, and 15 tanker trucks a day for 20 years (and, as a safety matter, no cryogens should be piped down a shaft). A refrigeration plant on site draws about 3 MW of electricity at about $0.068 per kWh (about $1.8 million a year) plus cooling water, giving about $2.1 million a year in running cost. The refrigeration plant's capital cost was not disclosed in the lecture, so the slider for it is yours to set; the calculator reproduces the lecture's yearly figures at its defaults, taking nitrogen at 195 kJ/kg (latent heat plus a little sensible heat), a price calibrated to $10.8 million, and 11.8 tonnes per tanker (derived from 15 trucks a day).

In-house or industry?

In-houseIndustry
Design36 months10 months
Procurement and manufacture24 months14 months
Total60 months24 months
ExperienceHighly skilled, but not every day; must research vendors and large-scale start-upDo this every day at 10 to 100 times the scale; qualified vendors for start-up; turnkey warranty

Doing it in-house is possible but, as in the lecture's case, usually not the best choice. Equally, practical layout questions (for example compressors above ground versus underground, with the plant close to the detector) need their own trade study. For procurement, choose among design-build with or without vendor oversight, fixed-price, firm-fixed-price (with or without economic price adjustment) or cost-reimbursable contracts, single-phase or multi-phase, with options. The aim is a partnership with the vendor.

Estimates

Cost and schedule estimates come from vendor quotes (pipes and valves), independent cost estimates (a refrigeration system), direct industry engagement, online quotes (standard items), engineering judgement and previous projects. Requests for quotation or information, and persistent follow-up, build relationships and good numbers.

Design: analyses and drawings

Conceptual or detailed, design follows codes and standards and meets the requirements within the interfaces. It typically contains analyses (heat load and heat leak; first- and second-law thermodynamic analysis; sizing for flow, pressure drop and vacuum; thermomechanical analysis of loads and contraction; pressure relief and safety device sizing; failure modes and effects; what-if), the piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs) and 3D models (envelopes, actual models, fabrication drawings). A P&ID shows the key piping and instruments, control and shutdown schemes, safety and regulatory features and start-up and operation information, and is the common language for controls programming, operating documents, cost estimates and discussions with interfacing systems.

A worked refrigeration-sizing example from the lecture: a bank of four 100 kW refrigerators serves four large cryostats, with three enough to maintain purity and one a full spare. The load to recondense argon in one cryostat is the sum of its heat ingress (48.7 kW), two recirculation pumps (11.0 kW), piping and purification vessel ingress (3.7 kW) and detector electronics (23.7 kW), which gives 87.1 kW; when the only duty is protecting inventory it is just the 48.7 kW heat ingress. Fill loads are much larger (hundreds of kilowatts) which is why the fill time of a cryostat is measured in months.
