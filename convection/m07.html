Lecture 35

Living with chaos: Reynolds averaging

A turbulent boundary layer is a tangle of eddies of every size, from the layer thickness down to fractions of a millimetre. Their details are unpredictable, but their averages are steady and reproducible. Reynolds' idea was to split every quantity into a time mean and a fluctuation, \(u = \bar u + u'\), \(T = \bar T + T'\), with \(\overline{u'} = 0\), and to average the governing equations. The continuity equation keeps its form. The momentum and energy equations each gain one new term, because the average of a product of fluctuations is not zero:

Averaged boundary layer equations$$\bar u\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial x} + \bar v\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\nu\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial y} - \overline{u'v'}\right),\qquad \bar u\frac{\partial\bar T}{\partial x} + \bar v\frac{\partial\bar T}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\alpha\frac{\partial\bar T}{\partial y} - \overline{v'T'}\right)$$

\(-\rho\overline{u'v'}\) is the Reynolds stress, and \(\rho c_P\overline{v'T'}\) is the turbulent heat flux. They represent momentum and heat carried across the mean flow by the eddies. Near a wall, fluid moving away from the wall (\(v'\gt0\)) comes from slower, cooler regions (\(u'\lt0\), \(T'\lt0\) for a hot stream). Fluid moving toward the wall brings fast, hot fluid. So \(\overline{u'v'}\lt0\): the eddies carry momentum toward the wall, which is precisely why turbulent friction is so much higher than laminar friction. The simulation shows this correlation in a synthetic velocity signal.

The averaged equations have more unknowns than equations: this is the closure problem. Every practical turbulence model, from the mixing length below to the \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) and \(k\)–\(\omega\) models used in CFD, is a way of relating the Reynolds stress to the mean flow.

Lecture 36

Eddy viscosity and the mixing length

By analogy with molecular viscosity, write the Reynolds stress as an eddy viscosity times the mean gradient, and the turbulent heat flux as an eddy diffusivity times the temperature gradient:

$$-\overline{u'v'} = \varepsilon_M\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial y},\qquad -\overline{v'T'} = \varepsilon_H\frac{\partial\bar T}{\partial y},\qquad \mathrm{Pr}_t = \frac{\varepsilon_M}{\varepsilon_H}\approx0.85$$

Unlike \(\nu\), \(\varepsilon_M\) is a property of the flow, not the fluid. Prandtl's mixing-length model estimates it as an eddy size times an eddy velocity. An eddy of size \(l\) moving across a gradient produces velocity fluctuations \(\sim l\,|\partial\bar u/\partial y|\), so

Mixing length with van Driest damping$$\varepsilon_M = l^2\left|\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial y}\right|,\qquad l = \kappa y\left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{y^+}{A^+}\right)\right],\qquad \kappa\approx0.41,\ A^+\approx26$$

Near the wall, eddies are limited by the distance to the wall itself, so \(l\propto y\), and \(\kappa\) is the von Kármán constant. Very close to the wall, viscosity damps the eddies completely. Van Driest's exponential factor switches the mixing length off within a few wall units.

Lecture 37

The universal velocity profile: the law of the wall

Close to the wall, the total shear stress is nearly constant and equal to \(\tau_w\). That suggests wall units: the friction velocity \(u_\tau = (\tau_w/\rho)^{1/2}\), the viscous length \(\nu/u_\tau\), and

$$u^+ = \frac{\bar u}{u_\tau},\qquad y^+ = \frac{yu_\tau}{\nu},\qquad \frac{du^+}{dy^+}\left(1 + \frac{\varepsilon_M}{\nu}\right) = 1$$

With the mixing-length model this is an ODE that can be integrated outward from the wall. Its solution contains three layers, the same for every smooth wall:

  • Viscous sublayer, \(y^+\lesssim5\): eddies are damped, \(u^+ = y^+\).
  • Buffer layer, \(5\lesssim y^+\lesssim30\): eddies and viscosity share the stress. Turbulence is produced most intensely here.
  • Log layer, \(y^+\gtrsim30\): eddies dominate, \(\varepsilon_M\gg\nu\), and \(u^+ = \kappa^{-1}\ln y^+ + B\) with \(B\approx5.0\)–\(5.5\).

The temperature profile has the same structure. In the sublayer, \(T^+ = \mathrm{Pr}\,y^+\), where \(T^+ = (T_w - \bar T)\rho c_Pu_\tau/q''_w\). In the log layer it is logarithmic with slope \(\mathrm{Pr}_t/\kappa\). For Pr ≫ 1 almost the entire thermal resistance sits in the viscous sublayer. For a liquid metal it is spread across the whole layer.

Physical interpretation

In a typical turbulent air boundary layer with \(u_\tau\approx0.3\) m/s, a wall unit is \(\nu/u_\tau\approx50\) µm. The viscous sublayer, where most of the velocity change and much of the temperature change happen, is only about 0.25 mm thick. Roughness elements taller than that, around \(k^+\gtrsim5\), poke through it and change friction and heat transfer. This is why "hydraulically smooth" depends on the flow as well as the surface.

Lecture 38

Turbulent wall friction

Away from the wall the log law can be approximated by a power law, \(\bar u/U_\infty\approx(y/\delta)^{1/7}\). Combining it with the momentum integral of Module 2 and a wall-friction law consistent with the same profile, \(\tau_w = 0.0225\rho U_\infty^2(\nu/U_\infty\delta)^{1/4}\), gives the classic turbulent flat-plate results:

Turbulent flat plate (5×10⁵ < Re_x < 10⁷)$$\frac{\delta}{x} = 0.37\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{-1/5},\qquad C_{f,x} = 0.0592\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{-1/5}$$

Compare the laminar layer: \(\delta\propto x^{1/2}\) and \(C_f\propto\mathrm{Re}_x^{-1/2}\). The turbulent layer grows faster (\(x^{4/5}\)), and its friction falls much more slowly. At \(\mathrm{Re}_x = 10^6\), the turbulent \(C_f\) is about five times the laminar value. For higher Reynolds numbers, log-law based fits such as \(C_{f,x} = 0.455/(\ln(0.06\,\mathrm{Re}_x))^2\) are more accurate.

Lecture 39

Heat transfer: the Reynolds–Colburn analogy

If \(\mathrm{Pr} = \mathrm{Pr}_t = 1\), the averaged momentum and energy equations for a flat plate are identical in form, and the velocity and temperature profiles are the same shape. Then heat and momentum are transported in exact proportion. This is the Reynolds analogy, \(\mathrm{St} = C_f/2\), with the Stanton number \(\mathrm{St} = h/(\rho c_PU_\infty) = \mathrm{Nu}/(\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Pr})\). Colburn extended it empirically to other Prandtl numbers:

Colburn analogy$$\mathrm{St}_x\,\mathrm{Pr}^{2/3} = \frac{C_{f,x}}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.0296\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{4/5}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\qquad(0.6\lt\mathrm{Pr}\lt60)$$

A real plate has a laminar leading section. Integrating the laminar result up to \(\mathrm{Re}_{x,c} = 5\times10^5\) and the turbulent one beyond it gives the plate-average

$$\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_L = \left(0.037\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{4/5} - 871\right)\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$$

At \(\mathrm{Re}_L = 10^6\) in air, this gives 1306, against 590 if the layer stayed laminar. Near the transition point, the local \(h\) jumps by a factor of three to five.

Cryogenic angle

Because the Reynolds number is \(\rho UL/\mu\), cooling a gas raises it twice over: density rises and viscosity falls. Cryogenic wind tunnels exploit this. They run on nitrogen gas cooled to roughly 100–120 K, reaching full-scale aircraft Reynolds numbers with models that fit in the test section. The same physics means that cold helium or hydrogen gas flowing past cryogenic hardware is turbulent at modest speeds, and its heat transfer follows the turbulent correlations of this module.

Lecture 40

Bluff bodies: cylinders, spheres and tube banks

Flow around a cylinder is a sequence of the phenomena in this course. A laminar boundary layer starts at the front stagnation point, where \(h\) is highest (Module 2). It thickens around the sides under an adverse pressure gradient and separates near 80° from the front. Behind it, a wake sheds vortices alternately from each side at a frequency fixed by the Strouhal number, \(\mathrm{St} = fD/U\approx0.2\) over a wide range of Re. Above \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2\times10^5\), the boundary layer turns turbulent before it separates, separation moves back to about 120°, the wake narrows, and drag drops sharply: the drag crisis. Local heat transfer follows every one of these events.

For design, the average coefficient over the whole surface is what matters. A single expression covers the full range of Re and Pr:

Cylinder in cross-flow (Re Pr > 0.2)$$\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_D = 0.3 + \frac{0.62\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}}{\left[1 + (0.4/\mathrm{Pr})^{2/3}\right]^{1/4}}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{Re}_D}{282\,000}\right)^{5/8}\right]^{4/5}$$

For a sphere, \(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_D = 2 + (0.4\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{1/2} + 0.06\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{2/3})\mathrm{Pr}^{0.4}(\mu_\infty/\mu_w)^{1/4}\), where the 2 is pure conduction from a sphere into a still infinite fluid. For banks of tubes, as in heat exchangers, the deeper rows see the turbulence generated by the rows in front and have higher coefficients. Correlations of the form \(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_D = C\,\mathrm{Re}_{D,max}^m\mathrm{Pr}^{0.36}(\mathrm{Pr}/\mathrm{Pr}_w)^{1/4}\) use the velocity in the narrowest gap between tubes, with \(C\) and \(m\) tabulated for in-line and staggered layouts.
