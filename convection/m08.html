Turbulent Duct Flow

Velocity profiles, the Moody chart, Dittus–Boelter, Gnielinski and liquid-metal correlations, and the temperature of a fluid flowing through a heated tube.

Lecture 41

The turbulent velocity profile in a pipe

Above \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2300\), and certainly above 4000, pipe flow is turbulent. The law of the wall of Module 7 applies across almost the whole radius, with distance measured from the wall: a thin viscous sublayer, a buffer layer, and a logarithmic region that extends nearly to the centreline. The result is a profile much flatter than Poiseuille's parabola. The core moves almost as a plug, and nearly all the velocity change is crammed into a thin region next to the wall.

$$\frac{\bar u}{u_\tau} = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln\frac{(r_0 - r)u_\tau}{\nu} + B,\qquad\text{or approximately}\qquad\frac{\bar u}{u_{max}} = \left(1 - \frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{1/n},\quad n\approx7\text{ at }\mathrm{Re}\sim10^5$$

For the 1/7 power law, the mean velocity is 82% of the centreline value, compared with 50% in laminar flow. The exponent grows slowly with Re, so the profile keeps flattening. Turbulent entrance lengths are short, about 10–60 diameters for the velocity and roughly 10 for the temperature, because turbulent mixing fills the core quickly. In most turbulent heat exchanger tubes, fully developed correlations can be applied over the whole length.

Lecture 42

Friction factor, roughness and the Moody chart

Integrating the log law across the pipe gives a relation between the friction factor and the Reynolds number that has to be solved iteratively. Colebrook combined it with the behaviour of rough pipes in one formula, the basis of the Moody chart:

Colebrook equation (Darcy factor)$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_D}} = -2\log_{10}\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\mathrm{Re}_D\sqrt{f_D}}\right),\qquad \Delta P = f_D\frac{L}{D}\frac{\rho U^2}{2}$$

For smooth pipes up to \(\mathrm{Re}\approx10^5\), the Blasius power law \(f_D = 0.316\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{-1/4}\) is a good explicit substitute, and Haaland's explicit form \(1/\sqrt{f_D} = -1.8\log_{10}[(\varepsilon/D/3.7)^{1.11} + 6.9/\mathrm{Re}]\) is within 2% of Colebrook everywhere. Roughness matters once its height exceeds a few wall units. In the "fully rough" regime the friction factor stops depending on Re at all: the sublayer has been destroyed, and drag is set by form drag on the roughness elements.

Physical interpretation

Why does the hydraulic diameter work so much better for turbulent flow than for laminar flow (Module 3)? Because turbulent resistance is concentrated in a thin wall region whose behaviour depends only on the local wall shear, not on the shape of the cross-section. A square duct and a round pipe with the same \(D_h\) have friction factors within a few percent in turbulent flow, against 11% in laminar flow. Only sharp corners and very narrow gaps break the rule.

Lecture 43

Turbulent heat transfer coefficients

Apply the Colburn analogy, \(\mathrm{St}\,\mathrm{Pr}^{2/3} = f/8\) (Darcy \(f\)), with the Blasius friction factor and you get roughly \(\mathrm{Nu}\propto\mathrm{Re}^{0.8}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\). The classic engineering correlation, still the first one most engineers reach for, is

Dittus–Boelter$$\mathrm{Nu}_D = 0.023\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{4/5}\mathrm{Pr}^n,\qquad n = 0.4\text{ (fluid heated)},\ 0.3\text{ (fluid cooled)};\quad 10^4\lt\mathrm{Re}\lt1.2\times10^5,\ 0.7\lt\mathrm{Pr}\lt120$$

It can be off by 25% and fails in the transitional range. Gnielinski's correlation, built on the friction factor and valid from \(\mathrm{Re} = 3000\) to \(5\times10^6\), is accurate to about 10% and should be preferred for design:

Gnielinski$$\mathrm{Nu}_D = \frac{(f/8)(\mathrm{Re}_D - 1000)\,\mathrm{Pr}}{1 + 12.7\,(f/8)^{1/2}(\mathrm{Pr}^{2/3} - 1)},\qquad f = (0.790\ln\mathrm{Re}_D - 1.64)^{-2}\quad(0.5\lt\mathrm{Pr}\lt2000)$$

In turbulent flow the wall boundary condition matters little for Pr ≳ 0.7: uniform heat flux and uniform wall temperature give Nusselt numbers within a few percent. Liquid metals are the exception. Their thermal resistance is spread across the whole radius, so molecular conduction never stops mattering, and the Péclet number replaces Re Pr:

$$\mathrm{Nu}_D = 4.82 + 0.0185\,\mathrm{Pe}_D^{0.827}\ (\text{uniform flux}),\qquad \mathrm{Nu}_D = 5.0 + 0.025\,\mathrm{Pe}_D^{0.8}\ (\text{uniform wall temperature})$$

Variable properties. With large temperature differences, evaluate properties at the bulk temperature and correct with \((\mu_b/\mu_w)^{0.11}\) for liquids, or \((T_b/T_w)^n\) for gases. Near a fluid's critical point, where \(c_P\) has a sharp peak, as with supercritical helium cooling a superconducting magnet, both of these fail. Dedicated correlations with a strong wall-to-bulk temperature ratio factor are used instead.

Lecture 44

The fluid temperature along a heated tube

The energy balance of Lecture 15 is the same in turbulent flow: \(\dot mc_P\,dT_m/dx = q''_wP_w\). With uniform heating the bulk temperature rises linearly and the wall stays a fixed distance \(q''_w/h\) above it. With uniform wall temperature the difference decays exponentially with the number of transfer units \(hPx/\dot mc_P\). Because turbulent \(h\) is large and the entrance length short, the fully developed results apply almost from the inlet.

Worked example: cooling a superconducting cable. Subcooled liquid nitrogen at 70 K (\(\rho = 838\) kg/m³, \(\mu = 2.2\times10^{-4}\) Pa s, \(k = 0.151\) W/mK, \(c_P = 2010\) J/kgK, Pr = 2.93) flows at 0.2 kg/s through a 20 mm channel. That gives \(U = 0.76\) m/s and \(\mathrm{Re}_D = 5.8\times10^4\), turbulent. Gnielinski gives \(\mathrm{Nu} = 253\) (Dittus–Boelter: 228), so \(h\approx1900\) W/m²K. If the cable picks up 2 W per metre from its cryostat, the wall flux is only 32 W/m², and the wall runs just 0.02 K above the liquid. But the liquid itself warms by \(q'L/(\dot mc_P)\approx5\) K per kilometre, and the Colebrook friction factor \(f_D = 0.0202\) gives a pressure drop of about 245 kPa per kilometre. Both numbers, the temperature rise and the pumping, set the spacing of the cooling stations along a superconducting power line.

Lecture 45

Beyond the basic correlations

Better turbulence models. Correlations summarise experiments. To predict the flow in a new geometry, CFD solves the averaged equations with a turbulence model. Mixing-length models need the length specified everywhere. Two-equation models such as \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) and \(k\)–\(\omega\) SST carry transport equations for the turbulence itself, so they handle separation and recirculation better. Near walls they either resolve the sublayer (\(y^+\approx1\) at the first cell) or bridge it with wall functions based on the law of the wall. Heat transfer predictions are only as good as the near-wall treatment, a point that matters for every CFD heat transfer study.

Enhancement. Roughness, ribs, twisted-tape inserts, coiled tubes and internal fins all raise \(h\), usually by disturbing the viscous sublayer, but they also raise friction. A fair comparison holds pumping power or pressure drop fixed. The entropy-generation method of Lecture 4 does this naturally.

Cryogenic angle

Forced-flow supercritical helium near 4.5 K and 3–6 bar cools large superconducting magnets, including fusion and accelerator magnets, through cable-in-conduit conductors. Near the pseudo-critical temperature its specific heat and density change steeply. Heat transfer can then improve or, in the worst case, deteriorate sharply, and the simple correlations need wall-to-bulk temperature corrections. Designers also track pressure drop carefully, because helium circulation power at 4.5 K costs several hundred watts at room temperature for every watt of heat removed.
