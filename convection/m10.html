Boiling and Condensation

Lecture 50

Film condensation: Nusselt's analysis

Vapour touching a wall colder than its saturation temperature condenses. On most clean metal surfaces the condensate wets the wall and forms a continuous liquid film that drains under gravity, thickening as it flows down because more vapour condenses onto it. The vapour itself is at \(T_{sat}\) and offers no resistance. All the resistance to heat flow is conduction across the liquid film, so the problem is to find how thick the film is.

Nusselt's analysis (1916) assumes a laminar film so thin and slow that its inertia is negligible. Gravity on the film is balanced by viscous shear, and the temperature profile across it is linear:

$$\mu_l\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2} = -g(\rho_l - \rho_v)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Gamma = \frac{g\rho_l(\rho_l - \rho_v)\delta^3}{3\mu_l},\qquad h'_{fg}\,d\Gamma = k_l\frac{T_{sat} - T_w}{\delta}\,dx$$

Here \(\Gamma\) is the condensate mass flow per unit width. Eliminating \(\Gamma\) gives the film thickness and the heat transfer coefficient:

Laminar film condensation, vertical wall$$\delta(x) = \left[\frac{4k_l\mu_l(T_{sat} - T_w)\,x}{g\rho_l(\rho_l - \rho_v)h'_{fg}}\right]^{1/4},\qquad \bar h_L = 0.943\left[\frac{g\rho_l(\rho_l - \rho_v)k_l^3h'_{fg}}{\mu_l(T_{sat} - T_w)L}\right]^{1/4}$$

The modified latent heat \(h'_{fg} = h_{fg} + 0.68\,c_{P,l}(T_{sat} - T_w)\) accounts for the extra heat released as the condensate subcools below saturation. The Jakob number \(\mathrm{Ja} = c_{P,l}\Delta T/h_{fg}\) measures how important that is. Note that \(h\propto\Delta T^{-1/4}\): a colder wall makes a thicker film, so \(h\) falls even as the heat flux rises.

Worked examples. Saturated steam at 1 atm on a 0.5 m plate held at 90 °C: \(\bar h = 7650\) W/m²K, the film is only 0.12 mm thick at the bottom, and 77 kW/m² is transferred. Nitrogen vapour at 1 bar condensing on a 5 cm cold surface at 70 K, the cold finger of a cryocooler re-liquefier: \(\bar h\approx2700\) W/m²K and \(q''\approx19\) kW/m² for a 7 K subcooling, with a film just 70 µm thick.

Lecture 51

Wavy and turbulent films, tubes and dropwise condensation

The film Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\delta = 4\Gamma/\mu_l\) decides the regime. Above about 30, waves appear on the film surface and thin it locally, raising \(h\) by 10–20% over Nusselt's value. Above about 1800 the film is turbulent, and \(h\) rises with film flow instead of falling, because turbulent mixing now carries heat across the film. The steam example above has \(\mathrm{Re}_\delta\approx225\): wavy-laminar.

Other geometries follow from the same analysis with a different length:

$$\bar h_D = 0.729\left[\frac{g\rho_l(\rho_l - \rho_v)k_l^3h'_{fg}}{\mu_l(T_{sat} - T_w)D}\right]^{1/4}\ (\text{horizontal tube}),\qquad \bar h_{N\text{ tubes}} = \bar h_DN^{-1/4}\ (\text{vertical column})$$

A horizontal tube of diameter \(D\) is usually far better than a vertical tube of length \(L\), because \(D\ll L\) and the film never gets thick. That is why most condensers are banks of horizontal tubes. In a column of tubes, condensate drips from each tube onto the one below and thickens its film, giving the \(N^{-1/4}\) penalty.

Dropwise condensation. On a non-wetting surface the condensate forms droplets that grow, coalesce and roll off, sweeping the surface clean. Much of the wall is then exposed directly to vapour, and \(h\) is often ten times higher than for film condensation. Keeping surfaces non-wetting for long periods is hard, which is the motivation for much research on promoter coatings.

Cryogenic angle

Condensation is how cryocoolers re-liquefy boil-off. In zero-boil-off liquid helium systems, a pulse-tube cooler's second stage near 4 K sits in the neck of the dewar and condenses helium vapour back into the bath. Helium's tiny latent heat and surface tension make its films thin and its coefficients high, but the condensing area must still be sized carefully, because the cooler offers only about a watt at 4.2 K.

Lecture 52

Pool boiling and the boiling curve

Heat a submerged wire in a pool of saturated liquid and plot the heat flux against the wall superheat \(\Delta T_e = T_w - T_{sat}\). The result is the boiling curve, one of the most important graphs in heat transfer:

  1. Natural convection (small \(\Delta T_e\)): no bubbles yet. The superheated liquid rises and evaporates at the free surface.
  2. Nucleate boiling: bubbles form at cavities in the surface, grow and depart. Each departing bubble pumps cold liquid onto the wall and carries latent heat away. Heat flux rises roughly as \(\Delta T_e^3\), and \(h\) is enormous.
  3. Critical heat flux (CHF): so much vapour leaves the wall that liquid can no longer reach it. The vapour columns become unstable and the curve peaks.
  4. Transition boiling: patches of the wall are blanketed by vapour. Raising the wall temperature lowers the heat flux. This regime is unstable under heat-flux control.
  5. Film boiling: a continuous vapour film insulates the wall. Heat crosses it by conduction and, at high temperatures, radiation. The lowest point at the start of this branch is the minimum heat flux, the Leidenfrost point.

If you control the heat flux, as with an electrically heated wire or a fuel rod, and raise it past CHF, the wall cannot stay on the nucleate branch. It jumps across to the film-boiling branch at the same heat flux, a far higher temperature. For water that jump is from about 120 °C to above 1000 °C, and the wire often melts. This is burnout, and avoiding it limits the power of boilers, nuclear reactors and high-power electronics.

Lecture 53

Nucleate boiling and the critical heat flux

Nucleate boiling depends on the surface: how many cavities it has, of what size, and how well the liquid wets it. No correlation can be better than about ±100% in heat flux for an unknown surface. The classic Rohsenow correlation treats bubble agitation as a kind of forced convection, with a surface–fluid constant \(C_{sf}\) to be measured:

Rohsenow$$q''_s = \mu_lh_{fg}\left[\frac{g(\rho_l - \rho_v)}{\sigma}\right]^{1/2}\left(\frac{c_{P,l}\,\Delta T_e}{C_{sf}h_{fg}\mathrm{Pr}_l^n}\right)^3,\qquad n = 1\text{ (water)},\ 1.7\text{ (other fluids)},\ C_{sf}\approx0.006\text{–}0.013$$

The critical heat flux is a hydrodynamic limit, set by the stability of vapour columns leaving the wall and the liquid flowing down between them, so it depends on fluid properties but hardly at all on the surface. Kutateladze and Zuber found

Critical heat flux$$q''_{max} = C\,h_{fg}\,\rho_v\left[\frac{\sigma g(\rho_l - \rho_v)}{\rho_v^2}\right]^{1/4},\qquad C\approx0.131\text{–}0.149$$

Lecture 54

Film boiling and the Leidenfrost point

In stable film boiling, a smooth vapour film covers the surface, and the analysis mirrors Nusselt's condensation analysis with the roles of liquid and vapour exchanged. For a horizontal cylinder (Bromley),

$$\bar h_{conv} = 0.62\left[\frac{k_v^3\rho_v(\rho_l - \rho_v)g\,h'_{fg}}{\mu_vD\,\Delta T_e}\right]^{1/4},\qquad h'_{fg} = h_{fg} + 0.80\,c_{P,v}\Delta T_e$$

At high wall temperatures radiation across the vapour film adds to this, roughly \(\bar h\approx\bar h_{conv} + \tfrac34\bar h_{rad}\). The film collapses when the heat flux falls to the minimum value

$$q''_{min} = 0.09\,\rho_vh_{fg}\left[\frac{\sigma g(\rho_l - \rho_v)}{(\rho_l + \rho_v)^2}\right]^{1/4}$$

The Leidenfrost effect is why a drop of water skates across a very hot pan and lasts much longer than on a merely hot one. It is also why liquid nitrogen poured on the floor balls up and darts around: the floor, at roughly 220 K above the nitrogen's boiling point, is deep in the film-boiling regime.

Lecture 55

Quenching: cooling a part down the boiling curve

Plunge a warm metal part into a cryogen and it travels down the boiling curve from right to left. At first it sits in film boiling: a quiet vapour blanket, a slow, almost linear temperature fall, and the sizzling sound of a part that seems reluctant to cool. When the heat flux falls to the Leidenfrost value, the film collapses. The surface passes through transition boiling to nucleate boiling, the liquid erupts, and the last stretch of cooling takes only seconds. A lumped energy balance with the boiling curve as the boundary condition captures all of this:

$$\rho\,c(T)\,V\frac{dT}{dt} = -A_s\,q''_{boil}(T - T_{sat})$$

where \(c(T)\) is the temperature-dependent specific heat. For copper it falls from 385 J/kgK at room temperature to 192 at 77 K and to almost nothing below 20 K. The simulation integrates this for a copper sphere in the cryogen you choose. A lumped model is adequate because copper's conductivity keeps the Biot number small even at nucleate-boiling coefficients.

How much cryogen does a cooldown use? Cooling copper from 300 K to 77 K removes about 74 kJ/kg. If that heat only evaporated liquid nitrogen (latent heat 199 kJ/kg), it would boil off 0.37 kg, or 0.46 litres, per kilogram of copper. Letting the cold vapour flow past the part, so that its sensible heat is used as well, can cut that by about a third. That is why large cryogenic systems are cooled down slowly with cold gas rather than flooded with liquid.

Physical interpretation

The surprising lesson is that a colder bath does not always cool faster at first. While the part is far above the Leidenfrost point, heat transfer is limited by conduction through the vapour film, about 100–200 W/m²K for nitrogen. Coating the part with a thin layer of insulating grease or tape can actually speed up a quench: the coating's surface temperature drops below the Leidenfrost point sooner, nucleate boiling starts, and the extra resistance costs less than film boiling would. Cryogenic engineers use this trick.

Lecture 56

Flow boiling, chilldown, and melting

Flow boiling. In a heated tube the fluid enters as liquid and evaporates along the way. The flow pattern changes from bubbly flow to slug flow, then annular flow (a liquid film on the wall around a vapour core), then mist flow once the film dries out. Heat transfer combines a forced-convection part, enhanced by the vapour, with a nucleate-boiling part that is suppressed as the flow accelerates. Superposition correlations of the Chen type add the two with an enhancement factor and a suppression factor. Dryout, where the wall film disappears, is the flow-boiling equivalent of critical heat flux.

Chilldown of cryogenic lines. When liquid nitrogen, oxygen or hydrogen is first admitted to a warm transfer line, it meets walls far above its Leidenfrost point. The leading front boils violently, producing vapour that accelerates down the line ahead of the liquid. Pressure surges, flow oscillations and even geysering in vertical lines are common until the wall reaches the nucleate-boiling regime. Rocket engine feed lines are chilled down before ignition for exactly this reason: vapour reaching a pump makes it cavitate.

Melting. Phase change also drives convection in the other direction. A heated body pressed into a meltable solid sinks into it on a thin liquid film, a lubrication flow, in what is called contact melting. A solid melted from the side develops a growing liquid region in which natural convection takes over from conduction once its Rayleigh number grows large enough. The melting front then becomes faster at the top than at the bottom. Phase-change thermal storage, including cold storage using ice or other materials below 0 °C, is designed around these regimes.
