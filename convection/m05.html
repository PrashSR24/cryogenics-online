Lecture 25

Enclosures heated from the side: four flow regimes

A rectangular cavity of height \(H\) and width \(L\) has one vertical wall warmer than the other by \(\Delta T\); the top and bottom are insulated. This is a double-glazed window, a wall cavity, a solar collector, a cooled electronics box, or the annular gap between the vessels of a cryostat. Fluid rises along the hot wall, crosses the top, sinks along the cold wall and returns along the bottom: a single circulating cell. How strong that cell is, and how much heat it carries, depends on \(\mathrm{Ra}_H\) and on the aspect ratio \(H/L\).

The start-up transient, by scale analysis

Suppose the walls are suddenly heated and cooled. At first, heat diffuses into the fluid by conduction: \(\delta_T\sim(\alpha t)^{1/2}\). The buoyant layer accelerates until the convection that it causes balances the conduction feeding it. That happens at \(t_f\sim H^2/(\alpha\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/2})\), when \(\delta_T\sim H\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{-1/4}\), the boundary-layer thickness of Module 4. Two conditions then decide what the final flow looks like:

  • Distinct wall layers exist only if the layers are thinner than the cavity is wide: \(H\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{-1/4}\lt L\), that is, \(\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/4}\gt H/L\).
  • Distinct horizontal jets along the top and bottom need a similar condition for the layers they feed.

These lead to four regimes. In the conduction regime (small Ra), isotherms are nearly vertical and \(\mathrm{Nu}\approx1\). In the tall-cavity regime, the wall layers merge across the narrow gap. In the boundary-layer regime, thin layers line the walls around a stagnant, thermally stratified core, and \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim(L/H)\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/4}\). In the shallow-cavity regime (\(H\ll L\)), the flow is a long counterflow along the cavity with end turns.

Physical interpretation

In the boundary-layer regime the core is not well mixed but stratified: warm on top, cold on the bottom, almost motionless. Each wall layer therefore sees a driving temperature difference that varies with height. This is why the cavity Nusselt number is lower than two independent plates would give, and why the isotherms in the solver below turn horizontal in the middle of the cavity as Ra rises.

Lecture 26

Solving the cavity: a benchmark in your browser

The square cavity heated from the side is the most famous test problem in computational heat transfer. Its steady solution at \(\mathrm{Pr} = 0.71\) has been computed to high accuracy for \(\mathrm{Ra} = 10^3\) to \(10^8\). In dimensionless vorticity–stream-function form, with lengths scaled by \(L\) and velocities by \(\alpha/L\):

Vorticity–stream function form$$\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial y} = \mathrm{Pr}\,\nabla^2\omega + \mathrm{Ra}\,\mathrm{Pr}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x},\qquad \nabla^2\psi = -\omega,\qquad \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \mathbf v\cdot\nabla T = \nabla^2T$$

with \(u = \partial\psi/\partial y\) and \(v = -\partial\psi/\partial x\). Pressure has disappeared. Buoyancy enters only as a source of vorticity, proportional to the horizontal temperature gradient: a vertical temperature gradient alone, warm above cold, produces no motion at all. The solver below marches these equations to steady state on a uniform grid with central differences, using Thom's condition for the wall vorticity. Its results agree with the benchmark values to within about 3% up to \(\mathrm{Ra} = 10^5\).

Lecture 27

Design correlations for side-heated enclosures

For engineering use, the numerical and experimental data are summarised by correlations of the form \(\mathrm{Nu}\propto\mathrm{Ra}^{0.28}\)–\(\mathrm{Ra}^{0.30}\), slightly steeper than the single-wall \(\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4}\) because of the interaction between the layers and the stratified core. With \(\mathrm{Nu} = q''L/(k\,\Delta T)\) and Ra based on the wall spacing \(L\):

$$\mathrm{Nu} = 0.18\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}}{0.2 + \mathrm{Pr}}\mathrm{Ra}_L\right)^{0.29}\quad(1\lt H/L\lt2),\qquad \mathrm{Nu} = 0.22\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}}{0.2 + \mathrm{Pr}}\mathrm{Ra}_L\right)^{0.28}\left(\frac{H}{L}\right)^{-1/4}\quad(2\lt H/L\lt10)$$

The first gives 4.72 at \(\mathrm{Ra} = 10^5\) for a square air cavity, against the benchmark 4.52. Correlations like these are accurate to 10–20%, adequate for design, and the solver above is a good way to see where they come from.

Partitions and baffles. Inserting \(n\) thin vertical partitions divides the cavity into \(n + 1\) narrower ones in series. Each sees \(\Delta T/(n+1)\) and has a smaller Ra, so heat transfer drops faster than \(1/(n+1)\). Horizontal partitions that cut the circulating cell are just as effective. Both are how glazing and wall cavities are made to insulate better.

Lecture 28

Heating from below: Rayleigh–Bénard convection

A horizontal fluid layer of depth \(H\) is heated from below. Heavy fluid sits on top of light fluid, but nothing moves at first: this top-heavy arrangement is in equilibrium, just an unstable one. A small rising blob is buoyant and wants to keep rising, but viscosity resists its motion and conduction drains its excess heat. Motion starts only when buoyancy beats both, at a critical Rayleigh number:

Onset of convection$$\mathrm{Ra}_H = \frac{g\beta\,\Delta T\,H^3}{\nu\alpha}\gt\mathrm{Ra}_c = \begin{cases}1707.8 & \text{both surfaces rigid},\ k_c = 3.117\\ 1100.7 & \text{rigid bottom, free top},\ k_c = 2.682\\ 657.5 = 27\pi^4/4 & \text{both free},\ k_c = \pi/\sqrt2\end{cases}$$

These numbers come from linear stability theory. Perturb the conduction state with a disturbance \(\propto e^{\sigma t}\cos kx\), linearise, and find the Ra at which the growth rate \(\sigma\) first becomes zero for some wavenumber \(k\). For two free surfaces the answer is analytic, \(\mathrm{Ra} = (k^2 + \pi^2)^3/k^2\). For rigid surfaces it is an eigenvalue problem, which the simulation solves numerically. The critical wavenumber fixes the size of the first convection cells: rolls about as wide as the layer is deep.

Above onset the heat transfer grows quickly. Rolls give way to three-dimensional cells and then to turbulent plumes. Experiments in the turbulent range are well fitted by \(\mathrm{Nu} = 0.069\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}\mathrm{Pr}^{0.074}\) for \(3\times10^5\lt\mathrm{Ra}\lt7\times10^9\). The exponent 1/3 means \(h\) becomes independent of the layer depth. The heat transfer is controlled by two thin conduction layers at the top and bottom walls, each of which goes unstable on its own, and the turbulent core in between is almost isothermal. Use the cavity solver above with heated from below to find the onset yourself. In a square box with insulated sides it lies near \(\mathrm{Ra}\approx2.6\times10^3\), higher than for an infinite layer because the side walls hold the fluid back.

Cryogenic angle

Liquid helium near its critical point is the fluid of choice for experiments that reach \(\mathrm{Ra}\sim10^{17}\) in a laboratory cell. Its kinematic viscosity and diffusivity are tiny, and \(\beta\) is enormous near the critical point, so a centimetre-scale cell behaves like the Earth's atmosphere. The other direction matters too: in a cryogenic tank, the vapour above a cold liquid surface is stably stratified (warm above cold) and does not convect at all, so the ullage gas warms in layers.

Lecture 29

Inclined layers, annuli and concentric spheres

Inclined layers. Tilting a layer heated from below by an angle \(\theta\) from horizontal turns part of gravity along the layer. Rayleigh–Bénard cells give way to a single sloping cell. For tilts up to about 60°, the onset criterion uses the normal component, \(\mathrm{Ra}\cos\theta\gt1708\), and the Nusselt number is close to that of a horizontal layer evaluated at \(\mathrm{Ra}\cos\theta\). At 90° the layer is a side-heated cavity again. Heat transfer has a shallow minimum near 60° for typical air layers, which matters for tilted solar collectors and skylights.

Horizontal annuli and concentric spheres. For the gap between two horizontal cylinders (or spheres), convection is conveniently expressed as an effective conductivity: the gap behaves as if filled with a still material of conductivity \(k_{eff}\). For cylinders,

$$\frac{k_{eff}}{k} = 0.386\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}}{0.861 + \mathrm{Pr}}\right)^{1/4}\mathrm{Ra}_c^{1/4}$$

where \(\mathrm{Ra}_c\) uses a length scale built from both radii and the gap. A similar form with constant 0.74 holds for spheres. If the formula gives \(k_{eff}\lt k\), convection is negligible and the gap conducts as a still fluid.

Lecture 30

Designing enclosures that insulate

A gas gap is a good insulator only while it does not convect. That puts an upper limit on useful gap width. In a horizontal layer heated from below, keep \(\mathrm{Ra}_L\lt1708\). For air between panes 20 K apart, with \(\nu\approx1.4\times10^{-5}\) and \(\alpha\approx2.0\times10^{-5}\) m²/s, that limit is \(L\approx9\) mm. A vertical gap convects at any Ra, but the heat transfer stays close to pure conduction until Ra reaches a few thousand. Beyond that, a wider gap no longer helps: conduction falls as \(1/L\), while convection rises with \(L\). The best double-glazing gaps, 12–16 mm for air, sit close to this crossover. Heavier gases such as argon or krypton have lower conductivity and lower \(\nu\alpha\), which shifts the optimum and lowers the heat loss.

The same reasoning explains why foams and fibre insulations work. Divide the space into tiny cells and Ra in each cell becomes negligible: a 0.3 mm cell with a 1 K difference across it has \(\mathrm{Ra}\sim10^{-2}\). Convection is suppressed entirely, and only gas conduction and radiation remain.

Cryogenic angle

Cryogenic insulation goes one step further and removes the gas. In a vacuum-jacketed transfer line or a dewar, the space between the walls is pumped to \(10^{-3}\) Pa or lower. Convection then becomes impossible and gas conduction becomes negligible, leaving radiation, which multilayer insulation then suppresses. If the vacuum degrades, the annulus first becomes a conduction gap, and at higher pressures a convecting enclosure. The heat leak can rise a hundredfold, which is why a "soft" vacuum shows up immediately as frost or condensation on the outer jacket.
