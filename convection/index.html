How to use this course

Read, run, then break things

Each module follows its lectures in order. Read the derivation, then open the simulation beside it and try to break the result. Push the Prandtl number to a liquid metal, raise the Rayleigh number past onset, switch a wall from isothermal to uniform flux, or drop a copper sphere into liquid helium. When a limit behaves the way the physical-interpretation box says it should, you understand the result. When it doesn't, you have found where a model stops being valid, which is even more useful.

Simulations marked Interactive are quantitative: they solve the equations in the text and are checked against exact or benchmark values. Those marked Live flow use a fast qualitative solver to show what a flow looks like and how it responds. Trust their pictures, not their numbers.

Prerequisites

Undergraduate fluid mechanics and heat transfer, ordinary differential equations and some vector calculus. The companion course on Advanced Heat Conduction covers the diffusion mathematics used here.

Why a cryogenics site teaches convection

Cryogenic systems are full of convection problems. Heat leaks drive stratification in storage tanks. Cooldown runs down the boiling curve. Subcooled nitrogen flows through superconducting cables, and helium vapour is re-condensed on cryocooler cold heads. Regenerators are porous media, and spills produce dense, oxygen-free plumes. Look for the green Cryogenic angle boxes throughout.
