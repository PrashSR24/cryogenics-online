Laminar Boundary Layers

Why a thin layer near the wall controls everything: scale analysis, integral methods, the Blasius and Pohlhausen similarity solutions, wedge flows and suction.

Lecture 7

The boundary layer: where the wall makes itself felt

A uniform stream \(U_\infty\) at temperature \(T_\infty\) meets a thin flat plate at temperature \(T_w\). Right at the surface the fluid must stop (no slip) and must take the wall temperature. Far away it does not notice the plate at all. When the Reynolds number is large, the transition between the two happens in a thin region whose thickness grows with distance from the leading edge. That region is the boundary layer. Prandtl's insight in 1904 was that outside it the flow is almost frictionless, so the wall's influence on both the drag and the heat transfer can be found by solving simplified equations in the thin layer alone.

There are two layers, and they need not have the same thickness. The velocity boundary layer \(\delta\) is where \(u\) rises from 0 to \(U_\infty\). The thermal boundary layer \(\delta_T\) is where \(T\) changes from \(T_w\) to \(T_\infty\). Their ratio is controlled by the Prandtl number, the ratio of momentum diffusivity to heat diffusivity.

Inside a thin layer, \(\delta\ll x\), two simplifications follow from scale analysis: diffusion along \(x\) is negligible next to diffusion across \(y\), and the pressure does not vary across the layer, so \(P(x)\) is imposed by the outer flow. The boundary layer equations are

Boundary layer equations$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0,\qquad u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dP_\infty}{dx} + \nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2},\qquad u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} = \alpha\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}$$

These are parabolic in \(x\): information travels downstream only. A disturbance at \(x_2\) cannot affect the layer at \(x_1\lt x_2\). That is why the heat transfer near the leading edge of a plate does not know how long the plate is.

Lecture 8

Scale analysis: thickness, friction and heat transfer in a few lines

Take the region of length \(L\) and thickness \(\delta\). Continuity gives \(U_\infty/L\sim v/\delta\), so \(v\sim U_\infty\delta/L\ll U_\infty\). In the momentum equation both convective terms scale as \(U_\infty^2/L\), and the friction term as \(\nu U_\infty/\delta^2\). The layer exists because these balance:

$$\frac{U_\infty^2}{L}\sim\frac{\nu U_\infty}{\delta^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\delta}{L}\sim\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2},\qquad \tau_w\sim\mu\frac{U_\infty}{\delta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C_f = \frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2}\sim\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2}$$

Heat transfer: two limits

The energy equation balances convection, \(u\,\Delta T/L\), against conduction, \(\alpha\,\Delta T/\delta_T^2\). The velocity \(u\) seen inside the thermal layer depends on which layer is thicker:

  • Pr ≫ 1 (oils, water, most liquids): \(\delta_T\ll\delta\). The thermal layer sits deep inside the velocity layer, where \(u\sim U_\infty\delta_T/\delta\). The balance gives \(\delta_T/\delta\sim\mathrm{Pr}^{-1/3}\) and \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\).
  • Pr ≪ 1 (liquid metals): \(\delta_T\gg\delta\). Most of the thermal layer sees \(u\sim U_\infty\), so \(\delta_T/L\sim\mathrm{Pe}^{-1/2}\) and \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/2} = \mathrm{Pe}^{1/2}\).

In both cases \(h\sim k/\delta_T\), the conductivity divided by the thermal layer thickness. Because \(\delta_T\propto x^{1/2}\), the local coefficient falls as \(h\propto x^{-1/2}\): it is infinite at the leading edge, where the layer has zero thickness, and the plate's front part does most of the work.

Physical interpretation

Pr tells you how far heat diffuses while momentum diffuses a given distance. In water (Pr ≈ 7) heat diffuses slowly, so the thermal layer is thinner than the velocity layer and is swept along by the slow fluid near the wall. In liquid sodium (Pr ≈ 0.005) heat races ahead of momentum, so the thermal layer is far thicker and is carried by essentially uniform flow. Liquid helium, liquid hydrogen and cold helium gas all sit near Pr ≈ 1, where the two layers have similar thickness.

Lecture 9

The integral method: one ODE instead of a PDE

Integrate the momentum equation across the layer, using continuity to eliminate \(v\). The result, von Kármán's momentum integral, holds exactly for any profile:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^\delta u\,(U_\infty - u)\,dy = \nu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0}\qquad(\text{zero pressure gradient})$$

Now guess a profile shape that satisfies the obvious conditions: \(u = 0\) at the wall, \(u = U_\infty\) and \(\partial u/\partial y = 0\) at \(y = \delta\), and \(\partial^2u/\partial y^2 = 0\) at the wall (from the momentum equation with \(u = v = 0\)). The cubic \(u/U_\infty = \tfrac32(y/\delta) - \tfrac12(y/\delta)^3\) does this. Substituting turns the integral equation into \(\delta\,d\delta/dx = 140\nu/(13U_\infty)\), and

$$\frac{\delta}{x} = \frac{4.64}{\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}},\qquad C_{f,x} = \frac{0.646}{\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}}\qquad(\text{exact: } 0.664)$$

The friction coefficient is within 3% of the exact answer, from a profile we simply made up. The same trick on the energy integral, with a cubic temperature profile and \(\delta_T\lt\delta\), gives \(\delta_T/\delta\approx\mathrm{Pr}^{-1/3}/1.026\) and \(\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.331\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\), against the exact 0.332. Integral methods are forgiving because the integrals average away the details of the profile shape.

Lecture 10

Similarity: the Blasius and Pohlhausen solutions

Scale analysis said the only length in the problem at station \(x\) is \(\delta\sim(\nu x/U_\infty)^{1/2}\). If the profile shape is the same at every \(x\) when \(y\) is measured in units of \(\delta\), then \(u/U_\infty\) depends on a single variable. Define

$$\eta = y\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}},\qquad \psi = \sqrt{\nu xU_\infty}\,f(\eta),\qquad u = U_\infty f'(\eta),\qquad \theta = \frac{T - T_w}{T_\infty - T_w}$$

The stream function \(\psi\) satisfies continuity automatically. The momentum and energy equations collapse into two ODEs:

Blasius & Pohlhausen$$f''' + \tfrac12ff'' = 0,\quad f(0) = f'(0) = 0,\ f'(\infty) = 1;\qquad \theta'' + \tfrac{\mathrm{Pr}}{2}f\theta' = 0,\quad \theta(0) = 0,\ \theta(\infty) = 1$$

The momentum equation is solved by shooting: guess \(f''(0)\), integrate outward, and adjust until \(f'\to1\). The answer is \(f''(0) = 0.33206\). The energy equation is linear once \(f\) is known and integrates in closed form: \(\theta'(0) = 1/\int_0^\infty\exp\left(-\tfrac{\mathrm{Pr}}{2}\int_0^\eta f\,d\eta'\right)d\eta\). The engineering results are

Laminar flat plate, isothermal wall$$\frac{\delta_{99}}{x} = \frac{4.91}{\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}},\quad C_{f,x} = \frac{0.664}{\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}},\quad \mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.332\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\ (\mathrm{Pr}\gtrsim0.5),\quad \overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_L = 0.664\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$$

For any Prandtl number, a single fit spans the liquid-metal and viscous-liquid limits to within about 1%:

$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = \frac{0.3387\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}}{\left[1 + (0.0468/\mathrm{Pr})^{2/3}\right]^{1/4}}\qquad(\mathrm{Re}_x\,\mathrm{Pr}\gt100)$$

Worked example. Air at 20 °C flows at 5 m/s along a 0.5 m plate held at 60 °C. Properties at the film temperature of 40 °C are \(\nu = 1.70\times10^{-5}\) m²/s, \(k = 0.0271\) W/mK and \(\mathrm{Pr} = 0.705\). Then \(\mathrm{Re}_L = 1.47\times10^5\), which is laminar, and \(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_L = 0.664\times383.5\times0.890 = 227\). So \(\bar h = 12.3\) W/m²K, and the plate loses 246 W per metre of width from one face. The boundary layer at the trailing edge is only \(\delta_{99} = 6.4\) mm thick, and the thermal layer is 12% thicker because Pr is slightly below one.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_bvp, cumulative_trapezoid

eta = np.linspace(0, 12, 600)
def rhs(e, y):            # y = [f, f', f'']
    return np.vstack([y[1], y[2], -0.5*y[0]*y[2]])
def bc(ya, yb):
    return np.array([ya[0], ya[1], yb[1] - 1])
guess = np.vstack([eta - 1.72*(1 - np.exp(-eta)), 1 - np.exp(-eta), np.exp(-eta)])
sol = solve_bvp(rhs, bc, eta, guess, tol=1e-8)
f, fp = sol.sol(eta)[0], sol.sol(eta)[1]
print(f"f''(0) = {sol.sol(0)[2]:.5f}   (Blasius: 0.33206)")
print(f"eta at u = 0.99 U: {np.interp(0.99, fp, eta):.3f}")

def theta_wall(Pr):
    em = max(12, 40/np.sqrt(Pr)); e = np.linspace(0, em, 200001)
    fx = np.where(e <= 12, np.interp(e, eta, f), f[-1] + (e - 12))   # f grows linearly outside the layer
    I = cumulative_trapezoid(fx, e, initial=0)
    return 1/np.trapezoid(np.exp(-0.5*Pr*I), e)

for Pr in [0.01, 0.7, 1, 7, 100]:
    t = theta_wall(Pr)
    co = 0.3387*Pr**(1/3)/(1 + (0.0468/Pr)**(2/3))**0.25
    print(f"Pr = {Pr:6}: Nu_x/Re_x^0.5 = {t:.4f}   fit = {co:.4f}")
// Shooting for Blasius, then quadrature for the Pohlhausen temperature gradient
function endSlope(s){ const r=M.rk4((x,y)=>[y[1],y[2],-0.5*y[0]*y[2]],0,[0,0,s],12,2400); return r.y[r.y.length-1][1]-1; }
let lo=0.2, hi=0.5; for(let k=0;k<50;k++){ const m=(lo+hi)/2; endSlope(m)>0?hi=m:lo=m; }
const s=(lo+hi)/2; print("f''(0) =", s);
const r=M.rk4((x,y)=>[y[1],y[2],-0.5*y[0]*y[2],y[0]],0,[0,0,s,0],12,2400);
const eta=r.x, fp=r.y.map(v=>v[1]);
const PrList=[0.7,7]; const th=PrList.map(Pr=>{ const g=r.y.map(v=>Math.exp(-Pr/2*v[3])); const tot=M.trapz(g,eta);
  let acc=0; return g.map((gi,i)=>{ if(i>0) acc+=0.5*(gi+g[i-1])*(eta[i]-eta[i-1]); return acc/tot; }); });
plot(eta,[fp,...th],{xlabel:'η',ylabel:"u/U∞ and θ",labels:["u/U∞","θ, Pr = 0.7","θ, Pr = 7"]});
Lecture 11

Other wall conditions: starting lengths, uniform flux and film temperature

Unheated starting length

If the plate is heated only beyond \(x_0\), the velocity layer is already established when the thermal layer starts. The thermal layer begins with zero thickness at \(x_0\), so the local coefficient there is very large. An integral analysis gives

$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = \frac{0.332\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}}{\left[1 - (x_0/x)^{3/4}\right]^{1/3}}$$

Because the energy equation is linear in \(T\), this result is a building block. An arbitrary wall temperature \(T_w(x)\) is a superposition of small steps at different \(x_0\), exactly the Duhamel idea from conduction theory.

Uniform heat flux

With constant \(q''_w\), the wall temperature rises along the plate as the thermal layer thickens, \(T_w - T_\infty\propto x^{1/2}\). The coefficient is about 36% higher than for an isothermal wall:

Uniform flux$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.453\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\qquad(\mathrm{Pr}\gtrsim0.6)$$

Why higher? Upstream wall regions are cooler than downstream ones, so the fluid arriving at \(x\) has been pre-heated less than it would be over an isothermal wall. The local temperature difference across the layer is steeper.

Film temperature

Real properties vary with temperature. The constant-property results remain accurate if \(\nu\), \(k\) and Pr are evaluated at the film temperature \(T_f = (T_w + T_\infty)/2\). For very large temperature differences, and that includes nearly every cryogenic surface exposed to room-temperature gas, the error grows. Property-ratio corrections of the form \((T_w/T_\infty)^n\) for gases, or \((\mu_\infty/\mu_w)^n\) for liquids, are then applied.

Lecture 12

Pressure gradients, separation, blowing and suction

On a wedge or near a stagnation point the outer velocity is not uniform. It varies as \(U(x) = Cx^m\). A favourable pressure gradient (\(m\gt0\), accelerating flow) thins the layer and raises \(h\). An adverse gradient (\(m\lt0\)) thickens it until the wall friction vanishes and the flow separates. Similarity survives with \(\beta = 2m/(m+1)\), giving the Falkner–Skan equation

Falkner–Skan$$f''' + ff'' + \beta\,(1 - f'^2) = 0,\qquad f(0) = f'(0) = 0,\ f'(\infty) = 1,\qquad \eta = y\sqrt{\frac{(m+1)U}{2\nu x}}$$

The included wedge angle is \(\pi\beta\). \(\beta = 0\) is the flat plate (with this scaling \(f''(0) = 0.4696\)), and \(\beta = 1\) is plane stagnation flow against a wall (\(f''(0) = 1.2326\)). As \(\beta\) is lowered below zero, \(f''(0)\) falls, reaching zero at \(\beta = -0.1988\). Beyond that the attached solution does not exist, and the flow reverses near the wall. At the front of a cylinder in cross-flow the stagnation-point result gives \(\mathrm{Nu}_D\approx1.14\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{0.4}\), the highest local heat transfer anywhere on the cylinder.

Blowing and suction

If the wall is porous and fluid is drawn through it with velocity \(v_0\lt0\) (suction), the layer stops growing. Far downstream, the momentum equation reduces to \(v_0\,du/dy = \nu\,d^2u/dy^2\), with solution

$$\frac{u}{U_\infty} = 1 - \exp\!\left(\frac{v_0y}{\nu}\right),\qquad \delta\sim\frac{\nu}{|v_0|},\qquad \tau_w = \rho U_\infty|v_0|$$

The layer thickness and the friction no longer depend on \(x\) at all. The temperature profile has the same form with \(\alpha\) in place of \(\nu\). Blowing (\(v_0\gt0\)) does the opposite. It pushes the layer away from the wall, thickens it and lowers both friction and heat transfer. That is the principle of transpiration cooling, used on rocket nozzles and turbine blades, where a coolant seeps through a porous wall and shields it from the hot stream.

Cryogenic angle

Cryogenic propellants often serve as the transpiration coolant. In regeneratively and transpiration-cooled rocket engines, hydrogen leaves a porous injector face or chamber liner and forms a cold blowing layer. Its low molecular weight and high specific heat make it the most effective coolant there is. The same physics, run in reverse, explains why a cold surface exposed to humid air is coated with frost so quickly: water vapour diffuses through the concentration boundary layer toward the wall, a mass-transfer problem we revisit in Module 11.
