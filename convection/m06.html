Transition to Turbulence

When and why laminar flow breaks down: transition data, the local Reynolds-number criterion, Kelvin–Helmholtz and inflection-point instabilities.

Lecture 31

When laminar flow breaks down: the empirical facts

Every laminar result in Modules 2 to 5 has a limit beyond which the flow stops being smooth and orderly. Small disturbances, always present, grow instead of dying away. Eddies appear, mix the fluid across the layer, and raise friction and heat transfer several-fold. Transition is not a sharp switch. It starts with growing waves, then turbulent spots that are carried downstream and merge, until the flow is fully turbulent. But the location where it happens is reproducible enough for engineering:

FlowTransition atNotes
Flat-plate boundary layer\(\mathrm{Re}_x\approx5\times10^5\)from \(10^5\) (rough, noisy stream) to \(3\times10^6\) (very quiet stream)
Pipe flow\(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2300\)delayed to \(10^5\) with great care at the inlet
Vertical natural-convection layer\(\mathrm{Ra}_x\approx10^9\)Grashof number of order \(10^9\)
Wake of a cylinder\(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx47\)onset of periodic vortex shedding
Plane jet\(\mathrm{Re}\sim30\)based on nozzle width; jets are extremely unstable

The numbers span five orders of magnitude, which seems to say that each flow has its own rules. The next lecture shows that the scatter mostly reflects a poor choice of length scale.

Lecture 32

A local Reynolds number criterion

The overall Reynolds numbers above use lengths that are convenient to measure (distance from the leading edge, pipe diameter, nozzle width), not the length over which the flow is actually sheared. Recompute each one using the thickness \(\delta\) of the shear layer and the velocity difference \(\Delta u\) across it:

Flow at transitionLocal Reynolds number \(\Delta u\,\delta/\nu\)
Flat-plate layer, \(\mathrm{Re}_x = 5\times10^5\)\(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx1200\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\theta\approx470\)
Pipe, \(\mathrm{Re}_D = 2300\)\(\mathrm{Re}_{r_0}\approx1150\)
Natural convection, \(\mathrm{Ra} = 10^9\), Pr = 0.71\(\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4}/\mathrm{Pr}\approx250\)
Cylinder wake, \(\mathrm{Re}_D = 47\)\(\sim50\) (wake width \(\sim D\))

The spread shrinks from five orders of magnitude to about one and a half. Viewed locally, laminar shear layers become unstable when their own Reynolds number reaches a few hundred, give or take a factor of three. This is a useful rule of thumb. The boundary-layer momentum thickness \(\theta = \int_0^\infty(u/U)(1 - u/U)\,dy\) is especially convenient, and transition correlations are written in terms of \(\mathrm{Re}_\theta\). The effect of free-stream turbulence intensity \(Tu\) (in percent) is captured by an empirical fit, \(\mathrm{Re}_{\theta,t}\approx163 + \exp(6.91 - Tu)\). It gives \(\mathrm{Re}_{x,t}\approx3\times10^6\) for a perfectly quiet stream and \(10^5\) for 3% turbulence.

Physical interpretation

A local Reynolds number compares how fast a disturbance is carried and stretched by the shear, \(\Delta u/\delta\), with how fast viscosity damps it, \(\nu/\delta^2\). When the first rate wins by a factor of a few hundred, small eddies survive long enough to extract energy from the mean flow and multiply. The transition zone that follows is where the flow is intermittent: sometimes laminar, sometimes turbulent, at any given point. Heat transfer there rises steeply from the laminar curve to the turbulent one.

Lecture 33

Why shear layers roll up: Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

Take two streams sliding past each other with velocities \(U_1\) and \(U_2\), separated by a thin interface. Give the interface a small wavy displacement, \(\eta = \hat\eta\,e^{ikx + \sigma t}\). Over each crest, the fluid on the convex side speeds up and its pressure drops (Bernoulli). The low pressure pulls the crest further out, and the disturbance grows. Linearising the inviscid equations for two layers of densities \(\rho_1\) (top) and \(\rho_2\) (bottom), with gravity and interfacial tension \(\gamma\), gives

Kelvin–Helmholtz dispersion relation$$\sigma^2 = \frac{\rho_1\rho_2\,k^2(U_1 - U_2)^2}{(\rho_1 + \rho_2)^2} - \frac{g\,k\,(\rho_2 - \rho_1)}{\rho_1 + \rho_2} - \frac{\gamma\,k^3}{\rho_1 + \rho_2}$$

Shear always destabilises, and more so at short wavelengths, where the growth rate rises in proportion to \(k\). Gravity (with the heavier fluid below) stabilises the long waves, and interfacial tension stabilises the short ones. Without gravity or tension, every wavelength grows. In a real shear layer of finite thickness \(\delta\), the fastest-growing wave has \(k\delta\approx0.4\)–\(0.9\): the layer rolls up into a row of vortices spaced a few thicknesses apart, then those vortices pair, and the layer grows by engulfing outer fluid. That is how jets, wakes and mixing layers spread (Module 9).

Rayleigh's inflection-point theorem. For an inviscid parallel flow \(u(y)\), a necessary condition for instability is that the profile has an inflection point. Jets, wakes and mixing layers all have one, which is why they are unstable at very low Reynolds numbers. The Blasius profile and Poiseuille flow have none. Their instabilities are viscous, slower, and set in at much higher Re: the Blasius layer at \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*} = 520\) through Tollmien–Schlichting waves, the plane channel at a centreline Reynolds number of 5772. Pipe Poiseuille flow is linearly stable at every Re. It becomes turbulent only through finite disturbances.

In a continuously stratified shear flow the competition between shear and stratification is measured by the gradient Richardson number \(\mathrm{Ri} = N^2/(du/dy)^2\), where \(N^2 = -(g/\rho)\,d\rho/dy\) is the buoyancy frequency squared. Stability is guaranteed everywhere that \(\mathrm{Ri}\gt1/4\). In the live simulation, raise the stratification and watch the billows weaken and then fail to form.

Lecture 34

Transition in practice: boundary layers and natural convection

Boundary layers. In a quiet free stream, two-dimensional Tollmien–Schlichting waves grow slowly downstream of \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*} = 520\) (\(\mathrm{Re}_x\approx9\times10^4\)). They become three-dimensional, break into turbulent spots and finally merge. With more than about 1% free-stream turbulence, or with surface roughness, this sequence is bypassed: turbulent spots form directly, and transition moves far upstream. A favourable pressure gradient (accelerating flow) delays transition, and an adverse one hastens it. Heat transfer follows the velocity field. In the transition zone the local Nusselt number may overshoot the fully turbulent value briefly before settling onto it.

Natural convection. On a vertical wall, waves appear in the boundary layer at local Grashof numbers around \(10^8\). The layer is fully turbulent by \(\mathrm{Ra}_x\approx10^{10}\). Because the velocity scales with height, transition on a tall wall happens at a fixed height for a given \(\Delta T\): about 0.3 m for a wall 40 K above room air, and only a few centimetres for a wall in water or liquid nitrogen.

Cryogenic angle

Cryogenic liquids have very low kinematic viscosities: liquid helium about \(2.6\times10^{-8}\) m²/s, forty times less than water, and liquid hydrogen about \(1.9\times10^{-7}\) m²/s. For a given velocity and size their Reynolds and Rayleigh numbers are therefore huge, and flows that would be laminar with water are turbulent with a cryogen. A liquid-hydrogen transfer line flowing at 1 m/s in a 25 mm pipe has \(\mathrm{Re}\approx1.3\times10^5\). Tank stratification layers, boil-off plumes and sloshing flows in cryogenic tanks are almost always turbulent.
