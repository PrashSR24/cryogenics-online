Jets, Plumes and Wakes

Free turbulent flows: shear layers, two-dimensional and round jets, the entrainment hypothesis, buoyant plumes in stratified surroundings and thermal wakes.

Lecture 46

Free shear flows: turbulence away from walls

Jets, plumes, wakes and mixing layers have no wall. They are shear layers in the open, and as Module 6 showed, they are violently unstable: their velocity profiles have inflection points, so they become turbulent at Reynolds numbers of only tens to hundreds. Once turbulent, they forget the details of how they started and settle into self-similar shapes. Measured in units of the local width and the local velocity scale, the profile is the same at every distance downstream.

Without a wall to limit eddy size, the eddies in a free shear layer grow with the layer itself. Prandtl's model for free flows takes the eddy viscosity as proportional to the layer width \(b\) and the velocity difference across it:

$$\varepsilon_M = \kappa_1\,b(x)\,|U_{max} - U_{min}|,\qquad \kappa_1\approx0.01\text{–}0.04\ (\text{flow dependent})$$

Because \(\varepsilon_M\) is constant across each section, the averaged equations become linear in the transverse direction and have closed-form similarity solutions. For a mixing layer between streams \(U_1\) and \(U_2\), the profile is an error function, \(\bar u = \tfrac12(U_1 + U_2) + \tfrac12(U_1 - U_2)\,\mathrm{erf}(\sigma y/x)\), and the layer grows linearly with distance. Temperature or concentration differences mix the same way, slightly faster, because the turbulent Prandtl number in free flows is about 0.5–0.7.

Lecture 47

Jets: momentum conserved, mass entrained

A jet issuing into still surroundings at constant pressure has no force acting on it, so its momentum flux \(J = \int\rho\bar u^2\,dA\) is the same at every section. Meanwhile it drags surrounding fluid in at its edges, so its mass flux grows. These two facts, plus self-similarity and a linearly growing width (every turbulent jet spreads at a fixed angle), give the decay laws directly:

Plane (2D) jetRound jet
Width\(b\propto x\), \(b_{1/2}\approx0.10\,x\)\(b\propto x\), \(b_{1/2}\approx0.095\,x\)
Centreline velocity\(U_c\propto x^{-1/2}\)\(U_c/U_0\approx6\,D/x\)
Mass flux\(\propto x^{1/2}\)\(\dot m/\dot m_0\approx0.32\,x/D\)
Centreline temperature excess (passive)\(\propto x^{-1/2}\)\(\Delta T_c/\Delta T_0\approx5\,D/x\)

The round-jet scaling follows from \(J\sim\rho U_c^2b^2\) = constant with \(b\propto x\), which forces \(U_c\propto1/x\). The mass flux \(\rho U_cb^2\propto x\) then grows without limit. By 30 diameters downstream the jet carries about ten times its original mass flow, all of it entrained from the surroundings. That entrainment is how jets mix, ventilate and dilute: the jet is a pump.

Lecture 48

Plumes and the entrainment hypothesis

A plume rises above a source of heat: a fire, a radiator, a hot exhaust, a buoyant gas leak. Unlike a jet, its momentum is not conserved, because buoyancy keeps adding to it. What is conserved in uniform surroundings is the buoyancy flux, \(F = g\beta q/(\rho c_P)\) for a heat source of power \(q\). Morton, Taylor and Turner closed the problem with one bold assumption: fluid is drawn in across the plume edge at a speed proportional to the plume's own velocity, \(u_e = \alpha w\), with the entrainment constant \(\alpha\approx0.1\). With top-hat profiles of radius \(b\), velocity \(w\) and reduced gravity \(g' = g\,\Delta\rho/\rho\):

Plume equations$$\frac{d}{dz}(b^2w) = 2\alpha bw,\qquad \frac{d}{dz}(b^2w^2) = b^2g',\qquad \frac{d}{dz}(b^2wg') = -N^2b^2w$$

Here \(N^2 = -(g/\rho)\,d\rho_\infty/dz\) measures the stratification of the surroundings. In uniform surroundings (\(N = 0\)) the solution is a power law: \(b = \tfrac65\alpha z\), \(w\propto F^{1/3}z^{-1/3}\) and \(g'\propto F^{2/3}z^{-5/3}\). The plume widens at a fixed angle and slows down as it rises, while it dilutes its buoyancy by entrainment. In stably stratified surroundings it rises until its fluid is no denser than its surroundings, overshoots, and spreads sideways. Integrating the equations above with \(\alpha = 0.1\) gives a maximum height \(z_{max}\approx4.3\,F^{1/4}N^{-3/4}\). This is why smoke from a fire, or the warm plume above a crowd in an atrium, can stall and spread in a layer instead of reaching the ceiling.

Pool fires puff. A large pool fire does not burn steadily. Its base periodically sheds large buoyant vortices. The puffing frequency is remarkably well described by \(f\approx1.5\,D^{-1/2}\) (Hz, with \(D\) in metres) over pool diameters from centimetres to tens of metres: a 1 m fire puffs about 1.5 times per second. The \(D^{-1/2}\) law is the free-fall time of a buoyant parcel over a distance comparable with the source size.

Cryogenic angle

Cryogen releases make plumes of both signs. Hydrogen boil-off vented to the atmosphere is very light and rises as a buoyant plume, which is one reason outdoor hydrogen venting is relatively safe. Cold nitrogen, oxygen or LNG vapour is heavier than air until it warms: it slumps and spreads along the ground as a negatively buoyant plume or gravity current. A spill of liquid nitrogen indoors therefore pools its cold, oxygen-free vapour in low places such as pits, stairwells and basements, the asphyxiation hazard examined in Module 11.

Lecture 49

Wakes and thermal wakes

Behind a body, the fluid carries a velocity deficit whose momentum flux equals the drag. Far downstream, the deficit is small compared with the free stream, and the averaged equations become linear. A two-dimensional wake (behind a long cylinder) widens as \(x^{1/2}\) and its centreline deficit decays as \(x^{-1/2}\). An axisymmetric wake widens as \(x^{1/3}\) and its deficit decays as \(x^{-2/3}\). A heated body leaves a thermal wake too, whose total heat content is fixed by the body's heat output. A row of heat sources therefore heats the fluid downstream in a pattern that depends on how far apart the sources are compared with the wake width at the next source. The same reasoning governs the placement of heat-generating components in a cooled channel: put the hottest ones where they see the coldest fluid, not in each other's thermal wakes.
