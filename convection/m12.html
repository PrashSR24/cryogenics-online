Lecture 62

Flow through porous media: Darcy, Forchheimer and Brinkman

Soil, sand, packed beds of pebbles, fibrous insulation, metal foams, wire-mesh regenerators: in each, fluid moves through a maze of tiny passages too complex to resolve. Instead we average over a representative elementary volume, large compared with the pores and small compared with the system, and work with the porosity \(\varepsilon\) (void fraction) and the seepage velocity \(\mathbf u\), the volume flow per unit total cross-section. The pore velocity is larger, \(\mathbf u/\varepsilon\).

At low speed, Darcy found that the flow is proportional to the pressure gradient, with a constant that depends only on the medium, the permeability \(K\) (m²):

Darcy and its extensions$$-\nabla P = \frac{\mu}{K}\mathbf u\ (\text{Darcy}),\qquad -\nabla P = \frac{\mu}{K}\mathbf u + \frac{c_F\rho}{\sqrt K}|\mathbf u|\mathbf u\ (\text{Forchheimer}),\qquad -\nabla P = \frac{\mu}{K}\mathbf u - \mu_e\nabla^2\mathbf u\ (\text{Brinkman})$$

The Forchheimer term is the form drag of the solid matrix. It becomes important when the pore Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_K = \rho u\sqrt K/\mu\) exceeds roughly 1–10. The Brinkman term adds viscous diffusion of momentum, needed only within a thin layer of thickness \(\sim\sqrt K\) next to a solid wall or an open fluid region. For a packed bed of spheres of diameter \(d\), the Ergun relations give \(K = d^2\varepsilon^3/[150(1 - \varepsilon)^2]\) and \(c_F = 1.75/(150\varepsilon^3)^{1/2}\). For 2 mm spheres at \(\varepsilon = 0.4\), \(K\approx4.7\times10^{-9}\) m² and \(c_F\approx0.56\). High-porosity metal foams (\(\varepsilon\approx0.9\)–\(0.97\)) have \(K\sim10^{-8}\)–\(10^{-7}\) m² and \(c_F\approx0.1\).

Lecture 63

The energy equation for a saturated porous medium

If the fluid and solid are in local thermal equilibrium, sharing one temperature at each point, the first law over the representative volume gives

Energy equation (local thermal equilibrium)$$(\rho c)_m\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + (\rho c_P)_f\,\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T = k_m\nabla^2T,\qquad (\rho c)_m = \varepsilon(\rho c_P)_f + (1 - \varepsilon)(\rho c)_s$$

The ratio \(\sigma = (\rho c)_m/(\rho c_P)_f\) means a temperature front moves more slowly than the fluid carrying it: the solid has to be heated as well. The effective conductivity \(k_m\) lies between the series and parallel bounds, \([\varepsilon/k_f + (1 - \varepsilon)/k_s]^{-1}\le k_m\le\varepsilon k_f + (1 - \varepsilon)k_s\), and depends on how the solid is connected. For metal foams, where the struts form continuous paths, it is roughly a third of the parallel bound.

Local equilibrium fails when the fluid passes through quickly or the pores are large, as in regenerators and packed-bed storage under rapid cycling. Two equations are then needed, one for each phase, coupled by an interstitial heat transfer coefficient \(h_{sf}\) times the specific surface area \(a_{sf}\) (m²/m³). The number of transfer units of the bed, \(h_{sf}a_{sf}L/(\rho c_Pu)\), decides how close the two temperatures stay.

Lecture 64

Forced convection in porous media

In Darcy flow there is no no-slip condition: the seepage velocity is uniform right up to a wall (apart from the thin Brinkman layer). A heated wall bathed by a uniform Darcy flow \(U\) therefore grows a thermal boundary layer in slug flow, the same situation as a liquid metal in Module 2. Scale analysis gives \(\delta_T\sim x\,\mathrm{Pe}_x^{-1/2}\), and the exact solution is

$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.564\,\mathrm{Pe}_x^{1/2}\ (\text{isothermal wall}),\qquad \mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.886\,\mathrm{Pe}_x^{1/2}\ (\text{uniform flux}),\qquad \mathrm{Pe}_x = \frac{Ux}{\alpha_m},\ \alpha_m = \frac{k_m}{(\rho c_P)_f}$$

In ducts filled with a porous medium the fully developed Nusselt numbers are the slug-flow values: 5.78 (tube, isothermal wall), 8.00 (tube, uniform flux), and 4.93 and 6.00 for parallel plates (based on the plate spacing). They are higher than the Poiseuille values because the fluid near the wall moves as fast as the core. Bodies immersed in Darcy flow follow \(\mathrm{Nu}\propto\mathrm{Pe}^{1/2}\) as well, with coefficients of about 1.0 for a cylinder and 1.1 for a sphere.

Physical interpretation

Filling a channel with a high-conductivity foam raises heat transfer for two reasons. The slug-like flow brings fresh fluid to the wall. More importantly, the conducting matrix carries heat into the core, so \(k_m\) can exceed \(k_f\) by a factor of ten to a hundred for aluminium or copper foams in air. The price is pressure drop, which the Forchheimer term makes grow almost with the square of the velocity.

Lecture 65

Natural convection boundary layers in porous media

Combine Darcy's law with the Boussinesq buoyancy force and the vertical seepage velocity next to a heated wall becomes \(u = (Kg\beta/\nu)(T - T_\infty)\): buoyancy is balanced directly by Darcy friction, with no inertia at all. The natural group is the Darcy-modified Rayleigh number,

$$\mathrm{Ra}_x = \frac{Kg\beta\,\Delta T\,x}{\nu\alpha_m},\qquad\text{scale analysis: }\delta_T\sim x\,\mathrm{Ra}_x^{-1/2},\quad \mathrm{Nu}_x\sim\mathrm{Ra}_x^{1/2}$$

The exact similarity solutions are

Vertical wall in a porous medium$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.444\,\mathrm{Ra}_x^{1/2}\ (\text{isothermal}),\qquad \mathrm{Nu}_x = 0.772\,\mathrm{Ra}_{*x}^{1/3}\ (\text{uniform flux},\ \mathrm{Ra}_{*x} = Kg\beta q''x^2/(\nu\alpha_mk_m))$$

Note the exponent 1/2 rather than the 1/4 of a clear fluid. In a porous medium the velocity is set by the local temperature alone, so the layer reacts more strongly to the driving temperature difference. Other shapes (cylinders, spheres, horizontal surfaces, stratified surroundings) have analogous results.

Lecture 66

Porous enclosures heated from the side

A porous layer between a hot and a cold vertical wall, such as wall insulation, a packed storage bin or a fibrous blanket, circulates just like the clear-fluid cavity of Module 5. With Darcy's law the vorticity equation collapses into a Poisson equation for the stream function, driven by the horizontal temperature gradient:

$$\nabla^2\psi = -\mathrm{Ra}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x},\qquad \mathbf u\cdot\nabla T = \nabla^2T,\qquad \mathrm{Ra} = \frac{Kg\beta\,\Delta T\,H}{\nu\alpha_m}$$

There is no time derivative in the flow equation: the velocity adjusts instantly to the temperature field. The four regimes of Module 5 reappear (conduction, tall layer, boundary layer, shallow layer). In the boundary-layer regime \(\mathrm{Nu}\approx0.58\,(L/H)\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/2}\). The solver below is the same engine as in Module 5, switched to Darcy mode. Its results are within a few percent of the published benchmarks for the square porous cavity (Nu ≈ 3.1 at Ra = 100 and 13.4 at Ra = 1000).

Lecture 67

Porous layers heated from below: onset of convection

A horizontal porous layer of thickness \(H\), heated from below, stays motionless until the Darcy–Rayleigh number reaches a critical value. Linear stability theory for impermeable, isothermal top and bottom boundaries (the Horton–Rogers–Lapwood problem) gives a neutral curve with a simple closed form:

Horton–Rogers–Lapwood onset$$\mathrm{Ra} = \frac{(k^2 + \pi^2)^2}{k^2},\qquad \mathrm{Ra}_c = 4\pi^2\approx39.48\ \text{at}\ k_c = \pi$$

The first cells are square: as wide as the layer is deep. Above onset, heat transfer grows roughly as \(\mathrm{Nu}\approx\mathrm{Ra}/40\) for Ra up to a few hundred, then more slowly as the flow becomes unsteady and plume-dominated. At higher pore Reynolds numbers the Forchheimer drag damps convection further. This is the mechanism behind geothermal reservoirs, convection in wet insulation and in grain stores, and the warm springs that rise through permeable rock.

Lecture 68

Porous media at work in cryogenics

Regenerators. The heart of every Stirling, Gifford–McMahon and pulse-tube cryocooler is a regenerator: a porous matrix of stainless-steel or phosphor-bronze screens, or lead, erbium-alloy or other rare-earth spheres at the coldest stage, through which helium gas oscillates. On each half-cycle the matrix absorbs heat from the gas and returns it on the next. Its effectiveness must exceed about 99% for the cooler to reach low temperatures at all. The design balances three losses: imperfect heat exchange (too few transfer units), pressure drop (the Forchheimer and Darcy friction of this module), and axial conduction through the matrix. Below about 15 K the heat capacity of ordinary metals collapses (Module 10's copper table), which is why magnetic rare-earth compounds with heat-capacity peaks at a few kelvin are used in 4 K regenerators.

Metal-foam baffles in cryogenic tanks. In a partly filled liquid-hydrogen or liquid-oxygen tank, sloshing driven by vehicle motion splashes warm vapour into contact with cold liquid. That causes pressure collapse, thermal destratification and boil-off variations. Open-cell aluminium foam with a porosity around 0.9, used as a baffle, damps the sloshing through the drag of the porous matrix, in which the Forchheimer term dominates at slosh velocities, while adding very little mass. Modelling the foam as a Darcy–Forchheimer porous zone in CFD (for example as a porous-media source term in OpenFOAM) is the standard approach. The permeability and the Forchheimer coefficient are measured independently, from steady pressure-drop tests or oscillatory drag experiments.

Powder and fibre insulations. Evacuated perlite and glass-fibre insulations use the porous medium to suppress radiation and support the vacuum jacket. Their Darcy–Rayleigh number is tiny at any realistic temperature difference, so natural convection plays no part, and the heat leak is set by solid conduction through grain contacts plus radiation.
