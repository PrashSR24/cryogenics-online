External Natural Convection

Flows driven by their own buoyancy: the Boussinesq model, Rayleigh-number scaling, similarity solutions, correlations and mixed convection.

Lecture 19

Natural convection: a fluid that moves itself

Heat a vertical wall in still air. The air next to it warms, becomes lighter, and rises. Cooler air slides in from the side to replace it, warms in its turn, and the motion sustains itself. No fan is needed, because the temperature difference does two jobs at once: it drives the heat transfer, and it drives the flow that carries the heat away. Think of it as a heat engine whose only output is the kinetic energy of the fluid, which friction immediately dissipates.

The driving force is a density difference, usually tiny. Over a modest temperature range, density varies linearly,

$$\rho\approx\rho_\infty\left[1 - \beta(T - T_\infty)\right],\qquad \beta = -\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_P\quad(\beta = 1/T\text{ for an ideal gas})$$

The Boussinesq approximation keeps this variation only where it multiplies gravity, and treats density as constant everywhere else. Subtracting the hydrostatic pressure of the still surroundings, the vertical momentum equation in the boundary layer along a wall (\(x\) upward) becomes

Natural convection boundary layer$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2} + g\beta\,(T - T_\infty),\qquad u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} = \alpha\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial y^2}$$

The two equations are now coupled both ways: temperature drives velocity through buoyancy, and velocity carries temperature. Making them dimensionless brings out the Grashof number \(\mathrm{Gr}_H = g\beta\Delta TH^3/\nu^2\) and the Rayleigh number \(\mathrm{Ra}_H = \mathrm{Gr}_H\mathrm{Pr} = g\beta\Delta TH^3/(\nu\alpha)\). Ra plays the part that Re played in forced convection.

Cryogenic angle: tank stratification

The simulation is a closed tank whose side wall is slightly warmer than the liquid, as when heat leaks through the insulation of a cryogenic storage vessel. The warm boundary layer rises along the wall and spreads under the lid, and a warm layer grows downward from the top. In a liquid nitrogen, oxygen or hydrogen tank, that warm layer sits at the free surface and sets the vapour pressure. The tank pressure therefore rises faster than a well-mixed estimate predicts, which is why stratification models and mixing jets matter in tank design. In subcooled liquid nitrogen, a 0.3 m wall only 1 K warmer than the bulk already has \(\mathrm{Ra}\approx8\times10^{10}\) and a turbulent boundary layer with \(h\approx270\) W/m²K.

Lecture 20

Scale analysis: why Nu grows as Ra1/4

Take a wall of height \(H\) with a thermal layer of thickness \(\delta_T\). The energy equation balances upward convection against sideways conduction: \(u\,\Delta T/H\sim\alpha\,\Delta T/\delta_T^2\), so \(u\sim\alpha H/\delta_T^2\). The momentum equation has three terms, inertia \(u^2/H\), friction \(\nu u/\delta_T^2\) and buoyancy \(g\beta\Delta T\). Buoyancy is the driver and must be balanced by one of the other two. Which one depends on the Prandtl number:

  • Pr ≳ 1: friction balances buoyancy inside the thermal layer: \(\nu u/\delta_T^2\sim g\beta\Delta T\). Combined with the energy scale, this gives \(\delta_T\sim H\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{-1/4}\) and \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/4}\). The velocity layer is thicker than the thermal layer, \(\delta\sim\delta_T\mathrm{Pr}^{1/2}\): a slow, viscous sheath of fluid is dragged upward by the thin warm layer it surrounds.
  • Pr ≪ 1: inertia balances buoyancy, \(u^2/H\sim g\beta\Delta T\), so \(\delta_T\sim H\,(\mathrm{Ra}_H\mathrm{Pr})^{-1/4}\) and \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim(\mathrm{Ra}_H\mathrm{Pr})^{1/4}\). The product \(\mathrm{Ra}\,\mathrm{Pr}\) is sometimes called the Boussinesq number.

Either way, \(h\sim k/\delta_T\propto(\Delta T/H)^{1/4}\). Unlike forced convection, \(h\) increases with the temperature difference, so the heat flux grows as \(\Delta T^{5/4}\). That nonlinearity is the signature of natural convection.

Physical interpretation

For the 0.5 m plate of the worked example below, \(\mathrm{Ra}\approx4\times10^8\) and \(\delta_T\sim H\,\mathrm{Ra}^{-1/4}\approx4\) mm. All the heat that leaves the plate crosses this few-millimetre layer of air. Doubling the plate height thickens the layer by only 19%. That is why tall plates have only slightly lower average \(h\) than short ones, until the layer turns turbulent.

Lecture 21

The similarity solution for an isothermal vertical wall

The scale analysis suggests the similarity variable \(\eta = (y/x)(\mathrm{Gr}_x/4)^{1/4}\), which is \(y\) measured in units of the local layer thickness. With the stream function \(\psi = 4\nu(\mathrm{Gr}_x/4)^{1/4}f(\eta)\) and \(\theta = (T - T_\infty)/(T_w - T_\infty)\), the equations become

Similarity equations$$f''' + 3ff'' - 2f'^2 + \theta = 0,\qquad \theta'' + 3\,\mathrm{Pr}\,f\theta' = 0;\qquad f = f' = 0,\ \theta = 1\text{ at }\eta = 0;\quad f'\to0,\ \theta\to0\text{ as }\eta\to\infty$$

Unlike Blasius, the two equations are coupled and must be solved together. Shooting from the wall is notoriously unstable here, because a slightly wrong guess makes \(f'\) blow up. The simulation therefore uses relaxation: it alternates between solving the energy equation for \(\theta\) with the current velocity, and the momentum equation for \(f'\) with the current \(\theta\), each a tridiagonal system, until both stop changing. The wall gradient gives the heat transfer:

$$\mathrm{Nu}_x = -\theta'(0)\left(\frac{\mathrm{Gr}_x}{4}\right)^{1/4},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{Nu}_x}{\mathrm{Ra}_x^{1/4}}\approx0.503\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}}{\mathrm{Pr} + 0.986\,\mathrm{Pr}^{1/2} + 0.492}\right)^{1/4},\qquad \overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_H = \tfrac43\mathrm{Nu}_{x=H}$$

For a wall with uniform heat flux the natural group is the flux Rayleigh number \(\mathrm{Ra}^*_x = g\beta q''x^4/(\nu\alpha k)\), and the wall temperature rises along the wall as \(x^{1/5}\). The local result is \(\mathrm{Nu}_x\approx0.60\,(\mathrm{Ra}^*_x\mathrm{Pr}/(4 + 9\mathrm{Pr}^{1/2} + 10\mathrm{Pr}))^{1/5}\), a widely used empirical fit.

Worked example. A 0.5 m tall vertical plate at 60 °C stands in air at 20 °C. At the 40 °C film temperature, \(\nu = 1.70\times10^{-5}\) m²/s, \(\alpha = 2.41\times10^{-5}\) m²/s, \(k = 0.0271\) W/mK, \(\mathrm{Pr} = 0.705\) and \(\beta = 1/313\) K⁻¹. Then \(\mathrm{Ra}_H = 9.81\times40\times0.125/(313\times1.70\times10^{-5}\times2.41\times10^{-5}) = 3.8\times10^8\), laminar. The similarity result gives \(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_H = \tfrac43\times0.387\times\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4} = 72\), so \(\bar h = 3.9\) W/m²K, and the plate loses 78 W per metre of width from one face. Compare 12.3 W/m²K for a 5 m/s breeze over the same plate (Module 2).

Lecture 22

Engineering correlations, turbulence and other shapes

On a vertical wall the laminar layer becomes unstable around \(\mathrm{Ra}_x\approx10^9\). Beyond that, the turbulent layer has \(\mathrm{Nu}\propto\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}\), which means \(h\propto(\Delta T)^{1/3}\) is independent of height: tall turbulent walls behave like a stack of identical short ones. Churchill and Chu fitted a single expression across both regimes:

Vertical wall, all Ra$$\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_H = \left\{0.825 + \frac{0.387\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/6}}{\left[1 + (0.492/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}\right]^{8/27}}\right\}^2$$
Laminar only, Ra < 10⁹ (slightly more accurate)$$\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_H = 0.68 + \frac{0.670\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/4}}{\left[1 + (0.492/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}\right]^{4/9}}$$

Other geometries follow the same pattern: a laminar \(\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4}\) law, a turbulent \(\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}\) law, and a composite formula joining them.

GeometryCorrelationLength scale
Inclined plate (up to ~60° from vertical)vertical-wall formula with \(g\cos\theta\)\(H\)
Horizontal plate, hot face up\(0.54\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4}\) (10⁴–10⁷), \(0.15\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}\) (10⁷–10¹¹)\(A/P\)
Horizontal plate, hot face down\(0.52\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/5}\) (10⁴–10⁹)\(A/P\)
Horizontal cylinder\(\left\{0.60 + 0.387\,\mathrm{Ra}_D^{1/6}/[1 + (0.559/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}]^{8/27}\right\}^2\)\(D\)
Sphere\(2 + 0.589\,\mathrm{Ra}_D^{1/4}/[1 + (0.469/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}]^{4/9}\), Ra < 10¹¹\(D\)

A hot surface facing down (or a cold one facing up) gives the weakest heat transfer, because the warm fluid is trapped under the plate and must creep sideways to escape. The sphere's constant 2 is pure conduction into an infinite fluid, the limit as \(\mathrm{Ra}\to0\).

Cryogenic angle

Turn every rule upside down for a cold surface. Air next to an uninsulated cryogenic pipe cools, becomes denser and falls, so the boundary layer starts at the top and the "hot face down" rule applies to a cold face up. With \(\Delta T\) above 200 K, the film-temperature approximation is stretched, and frost growth adds a conduction layer and changes the surface. A bare 25 mm line carrying liquid nitrogen through room air has \(\mathrm{Ra}_D\approx3\times10^6\) (air properties at the 190 K film temperature), \(h\approx14\) W/m²K, and picks up about 230 W per metre by natural convection alone, before radiation, condensation and frost are added. That is why cold lines are insulated or vacuum-jacketed.

Lecture 23

Stratified surroundings, conjugate walls and vertical channels

Stratification. If the surroundings get warmer with height, as in a stratified tank or a room, the driving temperature difference shrinks with \(x\). The boundary layer weakens along the wall, and if the wall temperature is exceeded by the ambient higher up, the flow can even reverse. A stratification parameter \(b = (dT_\infty/dx)\,H/(T_w - T_{\infty,0})\) measures the effect. As \(b\) approaches 1, where the top of the wall is at the ambient temperature, the overall Nusselt number falls well below the unstratified value, because the upper part of the wall has almost no driving force left.

Conjugate walls. A thin wall separating two fluid reservoirs at different temperatures carries a boundary layer on each side: one rising, one falling. Neither wall temperature is known in advance. The overall heat transfer follows from treating the two layers as resistances in series, each depending on its own \(\Delta T\) to the 1/4 power, which makes the problem mildly nonlinear but easy to iterate.

Vertical channels: the optimal fin spacing

Vertical plates spaced \(D\) apart, open at top and bottom, act as chimneys. If the plates are close, the flow is fully developed and the air leaves at the plate temperature, but there is little flow. If they are far apart, each plate has an independent boundary layer, but there are few plates. Intersecting the two asymptotes, the same method as in Lecture 18, gives the spacing that removes the most heat from a fixed volume:

Optimal spacing, natural convection$$\frac{D_{opt}}{H}\approx2.3\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{-1/4}$$

For aluminium heat sinks in air with \(H = 10\) cm and \(\Delta T = 40\) K, \(\mathrm{Ra}_H\approx3\times10^6\), so the optimum gap is about 5.5 mm. Most commercial heat sinks are close to this.

Lecture 24

Mixed convection: when forced and natural flows compete

When a forced flow passes a heated surface slowly enough, buoyancy adds to or subtracts from it. The ratio of buoyancy to inertia is the Richardson number, \(\mathrm{Ri} = \mathrm{Gr}/\mathrm{Re}^2\). Forced convection dominates when \(\mathrm{Ri}\ll1\), natural convection when \(\mathrm{Ri}\gg1\), and both matter when \(\mathrm{Ri}\sim1\). For a laminar boundary layer, the comparison is made with the layer scalings, \(\mathrm{Gr}^{1/4}\) against \(\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}\), so the crossover sits at \(\mathrm{Gr}_x/\mathrm{Re}_x^2\sim1\) but is spread over a decade or so.

A simple and widely used interpolation adds the two Nusselt numbers as if they were vectors:

Combination rule$$\mathrm{Nu}^n = \mathrm{Nu}_F^n\pm\mathrm{Nu}_N^n,\qquad n\approx3$$

The plus sign is for aiding flow (forced flow upward past a hot wall) and the minus sign for opposing flow (forced flow downward). Opposing flow can thicken the layer to separation, and right at \(\mathrm{Nu}_F\approx\mathrm{Nu}_N\) the rule predicts nearly zero, which overstates the penalty. Real opposing flows become unsteady and mix, so the heat transfer dips but does not vanish.
