Laminar Duct Flow

Lecture 13

The entrance region: boundary layers that run out of room

Fluid enters a tube with a nearly uniform velocity. Boundary layers start to grow on the wall exactly as on a flat plate, while the core, squeezed by the slowing fluid near the wall, accelerates to conserve mass. The layers grow as \(\delta\sim(\nu x/U)^{1/2}\) until they meet at the centreline. Beyond that point the profile stops changing: the flow is fully developed. Setting \(\delta\sim D/2\) gives the scale of the hydrodynamic entrance length,

$$\frac{X}{D}\sim\mathrm{Re}_D\qquad\text{measured: }\frac{X}{D}\approx0.05\,\mathrm{Re}_D\quad(\text{laminar})$$

At \(\mathrm{Re}_D = 2000\) the entrance region is 100 diameters long. In short laminar passages, such as micro-channels, plate heat exchangers and the fine tubes of cryocooler heat exchangers, it can be the whole passage. The temperature profile has its own entrance length. The thermal layer grows like \((\alpha x/U)^{1/2}\) in slug flow, or more slowly when the velocity profile is already parabolic, giving \(X_T/D\approx0.05\,\mathrm{Re}_D\mathrm{Pr}\). For oils (Pr ~ 1000) the thermal entrance can be kilometres long. For gases the two entrance lengths are similar.

Physical interpretation

In the entrance region the boundary layers are thin, so wall gradients and both friction and heat transfer are high. Once the layers fill the tube they can grow no further, so \(h\) and the friction factor settle to constant values. Fully developed flow is the most inefficient part of a duct: it is where heat must cross the whole radius.

Lecture 14

Fully developed flow, hydraulic diameter and pressure drop

In fully developed flow \(v = 0\) and \(u = u(r)\), so the inertia terms vanish exactly. The momentum equation becomes a balance between the pressure gradient and friction alone:

$$\frac{dP}{dx} = \mu\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{du}{dr}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad u = 2U\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{r_0^2}\right),\qquad U = \frac{r_0^2}{8\mu}\left(-\frac{dP}{dx}\right)$$

This is Hagen–Poiseuille flow. The wall shear stress, made dimensionless with the dynamic pressure, is the friction factor. Two definitions are used and differ by a factor of four. The Fanning factor \(f = \tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho U^2)\) gives \(f = 16/\mathrm{Re}_D\). The Darcy factor \(f_D = 4f\) gives \(64/\mathrm{Re}_D\). The pressure drop over length \(L\) is \(\Delta P = f_D(L/D)\tfrac12\rho U^2\).

Non-circular ducts

A force balance on any fully developed duct section gives \(\tau_{w,avg}P_w = A(-dP/dx)\), where \(P_w\) is the wetted perimeter. This suggests the length scale \(D_h = 4A/P_w\), the hydraulic diameter. For a circle \(D_h = D\). For parallel plates spaced \(D\) apart, \(D_h = 2D\). Using \(D_h\), the product \(f\,\mathrm{Re}_{D_h}\) stays close to 16 for many shapes, but not exactly. Sharp corners hold slow fluid and lower it, flat shapes raise it:

Lecture 15

The mean temperature and the duct energy balance

In a duct there is no free-stream temperature to put in Newton's law. The natural reference is the temperature you would measure if you collected the fluid passing a section in a cup and mixed it: the mixed-mean or bulk temperature,

$$T_m = \frac{\int_A\rho c_Pu\,T\,dA}{\int_A\rho c_Pu\,dA},\qquad q''_w = h\,(T_w - T_m),\qquad \dot mc_P\frac{dT_m}{dx} = q''_wP_w$$

The last relation is an energy balance on a slice of duct. It gives \(T_m(x)\) without any knowledge of the profile details. Two classic cases:

  • Uniform heat flux (electric heating, nuclear fuel, an evenly irradiated solar receiver): \(T_m\) rises linearly. In fully developed flow \(T_w - T_m = q''_w/h\) is constant, so the wall runs parallel to the fluid, a fixed distance above it.
  • Uniform wall temperature (a tube in a boiling or condensing bath): \(dT_m/dx = (hP_w/\dot mc_P)(T_w - T_m)\), so the difference decays exponentially, \(\dfrac{T_w - T_m(x)}{T_w - T_{in}} = \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\bar hP_wx}{\dot mc_P}\right)\).

Integrating the second case over length \(L\) gives the total heat \(q = \bar hA_w\Delta T_{lm}\) with the log-mean temperature difference \(\Delta T_{lm} = (\Delta T_{in} - \Delta T_{out})/\ln(\Delta T_{in}/\Delta T_{out})\). The group \(\bar hA_w/\dot mc_P\) is the number of transfer units (NTU) of a heat exchanger with one side at constant temperature.

Worked example: a helium precooler. Helium gas at 1 bar, \(\dot m = 0.02\) g/s, enters a 2 mm copper tube immersed in liquid nitrogen at 77 K. How long must the tube be to cool the gas from 300 K to 80 K? Using properties at about 120 K (\(\mu = 1.1\times10^{-5}\) Pa s, \(k = 0.083\) W/mK, \(c_P = 5193\) J/kgK): \(\mathrm{Re}_D = 4\dot m/(\pi D\mu) = 1160\), laminar, with \(\mathrm{Pr} = 0.69\). Taking the fully developed \(\mathrm{Nu}_T = 3.66\) gives \(h = 152\) W/m²K. The required NTU is \(\ln(223/3) = 4.31\), so \(L = 4.31\,\dot mc_P/(h\pi D) = 0.47\) m. The thermal entrance length, \(0.05\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Pr}\,D\approx8\) cm, is short compared with this, so the estimate is sound. The tube removes about 23 W from the gas. Strong property variation with temperature is the main source of error. A careful design marches along the tube with local properties, which is a good exercise with the code below.

Lecture 16

The fully developed temperature profile

The temperature keeps changing along a heated duct, so "fully developed" must mean something subtler than \(\partial T/\partial x = 0\). It means the shape of the profile stops changing:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{T_w - T}{T_w - T_m}\right) = 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \text{constant}$$

For uniform heat flux, this gives \(\partial T/\partial x = dT_m/dx = dT_w/dx\), a constant, and the energy equation becomes an ODE in \(r\):

$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dT}{dr}\right) = \frac{2U}{\alpha}\frac{dT_m}{dx}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{r_0^2}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad T - T_w = \frac{2Ur_0^2}{\alpha}\frac{dT_m}{dx}\left[\frac{r^2}{4r_0^2} - \frac{r^4}{16r_0^4} - \frac{3}{16}\right]$$

Integrating for \(T_m\) with the parabolic weight and dividing the wall flux by \(T_w - T_m\) gives \(\mathrm{Nu}_D = 48/11 = 4.364\). For an isothermal wall the profile shape is an eigenfunction, and \(\mathrm{Nu}_D = 3.657\). That is about 16% lower, because the wall flux there is concentrated where the fluid is still hottest.

Physical interpretation

In laminar fully developed flow, \(h = \mathrm{Nu}\,k/D\) with Nu a pure number. Halve the diameter and you double \(h\), independent of the flow rate. This is why laminar micro-channel coolers reach heat transfer coefficients of \(10^4\)–\(10^5\) W/m²K without turbulence, and why the fine-bore Hampson-type heat exchangers of small cryocoolers are so effective.

Lecture 17

Thermally developing flow: the Graetz problem

Suppose the velocity is already parabolic when the wall temperature suddenly changes at \(x = 0\). This is the Graetz problem. Its natural axial coordinate is \(x^* = x/(D\,\mathrm{Re}_D\mathrm{Pr})\), the inverse of the Graetz number \(\mathrm{Gz} = D\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Pr}/x\). Separation of variables gives an eigenfunction series. Close to the entrance many terms are needed, and Lévêque's alternative takes over: the thin thermal layer sees only the linear part of the velocity profile near the wall, which gives

$$\mathrm{Nu}_x\approx1.077\,x^{*-1/3}\ (\text{isothermal wall}),\qquad \mathrm{Nu}_x\approx1.302\,x^{*-1/3}\ (\text{uniform flux}),\qquad x^*\lesssim10^{-3}$$

The \(-1/3\) exponent is the Pr ≫ 1 boundary-layer law from Module 2, because near the wall the parabolic profile looks like a shear flow. By \(x^*\approx0.05\) the Nusselt number has settled to its fully developed value. The simulation marches the energy equation down the tube with an implicit finite-volume scheme and reproduces both limits.

For design, the mean Nusselt number over a tube of length \(L\) with isothermal wall is well represented by Hausen's correlation, \(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}_D = 3.66 + 0.0668\,\mathrm{Gz}/(1 + 0.04\,\mathrm{Gz}^{2/3})\) with \(\mathrm{Gz} = D\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Pr}/L\). When velocity and temperature develop together (an unheated entry is absent), the thin velocity layer near the inlet raises \(h\) further. Liquids with strongly temperature-dependent viscosity also need a correction factor \((\mu_m/\mu_w)^{0.14}\).

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_banded

def graetz(bc='T', N=300, nx=500, x0=1e-6, x1=2.0):
    """March (1 - r^2) dθ/dx* = 2 (1/r) d/dr (r dθ/dr), x* = x/(D Re Pr), r = r/r0."""
    dr = 1.0/N; r = np.arange(N + 1)*dr
    rp = np.minimum(1, r + dr/2); rm = np.maximum(0, r - dr/2)
    V = (rp**2 - rm**2)/4 - (rp**4 - rm**4)/8          # ∫(1 - r²) r dr over each control volume
    th = np.ones(N + 1) if bc == 'T' else np.zeros(N + 1)
    if bc == 'T': th[-1] = 0
    xs = x0*(x1/x0)**np.linspace(0, 1, nx); x = 0.0; out = []
    for xn in xs:
        dx = xn - x; x = xn
        ce = np.where(np.arange(N + 1) < N, 2*rp/dr, 0.0); cw = np.where(np.arange(N + 1) > 0, 2*rm/dr, 0.0)
        diag = V/dx + ce + cw; rhs = V/dx*th
        if bc == 'T': diag[-1], cw[-1], rhs[-1] = 1, 0, 0
        else: rhs[-1] += 2.0                             # dθ/dr = 1 at the wall
        ab = np.zeros((3, N + 1)); ab[0, 1:] = -ce[:-1]; ab[1] = diag; ab[2, :-1] = -cw[1:]
        th = solve_banded((1, 1), ab, rhs)
        tm = np.sum(V*th)/np.sum(V)
        nu = 2*abs((3*th[-1] - 4*th[-2] + th[-3])/(2*dr))/tm if bc == 'T' else 2/(th[-1] - tm)
        out.append(nu)
    return xs, np.array(out)

for bc in 'TH':
    xs, nu = graetz(bc)
    pick = lambda v: nu[np.searchsorted(xs, v)]
    print(bc, [f"{pick(v):.3f}" for v in (1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 0.5)])
# expected: T → 3.657 far downstream, H → 4.364
// Same marching scheme, isothermal and uniform-flux walls

  const rp=r.map(x=>Math.min(1,x+dr/2)), rm=r.map(x=>Math.max(0,x-dr/2)), V=r.map((_,i)=>(rp[i]**2-rm[i]**2)/4-(rp[i]**4-rm[i]**4)/8);
  let th=r.map((_,i)=>bc==='T'?(i<N?1:0):0), x=0; const xs=[], nus=[];
  for(let s=0;s<400;s++){ const xn=1e-6*Math.pow(2e6,s/399), dx=xn-x; x=xn;
    const a=[],b=[],c=[],d=[]; for(let i=0;i<=N;i++){ const ce=i<N?2*rp[i]/dr:0, cw=i>0?2*rm[i]/dr:0; a.push(-cw); b.push(V[i]/dx+ce+cw); c.push(-ce); d.push(V[i]/dx*th[i]); }
    if(bc==='T'){ a[N]=0; b[N]=1; c[N]=0; d[N]=0; } else d[N]+=2;
    th=Array.from(M.tdma(a,b,c,d)); const tm=th.reduce((s,t,i)=>s+V[i]*t,0)/V.reduce((s,v)=>s+v,0);
    xs.push(x); nus.push(bc==='T'?2*Math.abs((3*th[N]-4*th[N-1]+th[N-2])/(2*dr))/tm:2/(th[N]-tm)); }
  return [xs,nus]; }

Lecture 18

How close should heated plates be? The intersection of asymptotes

A stack of heat-generating plates (circuit boards, fins, the plates of a compact heat exchanger) fills a fixed volume of height \(H\) and length \(L\). Coolant is pushed through the channels by a fixed pressure difference \(\Delta P\). What plate spacing \(D\) removes the most heat? The two extremes are easy, and the trick is to analyse only them:

  • Very small \(D\): the channels are long and narrow, the flow is fully developed, and the coolant leaves at the plate temperature. The heat removed is \(\dot mc_P\Delta T\), and \(\dot m\) per channel falls like \(D^3\) while the number of channels grows like \(1/D\). Total: \(q'\approx\rho c_PHD^2\Delta P\,\Delta T/(12\mu L)\), which rises with \(D\).
  • Very large \(D\): each plate face carries its own thin boundary layer, the core coolant leaves almost unheated, and there are few plates. Total heat falls as \(D\) grows.

The real curve must pass through a maximum somewhere between. Its location is estimated well by where the two asymptotes cross. Writing \(\mathrm{Be} = \Delta P\,L^2/(\mu\alpha)\) for the dimensionless pressure drop, intersecting the two estimates (with laminar boundary layers driven by the same \(\Delta P\)) gives

Optimal spacing (estimate)$$\frac{D_{opt}}{L}\approx2.7\,\mathrm{Pr}^{-1/8}\,\mathrm{Be}^{-1/4}$$

The intersection method gives the right scaling and a constant of order one without solving the full problem. Numerical and experimental optima confirm the \(\mathrm{Be}^{-1/4}\) law. The idea, that a design is best where two opposing trends balance, returns again and again, for example in Lecture 4's tube that minimises entropy generation.
