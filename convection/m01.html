Lecture 1

What convection is, and the fundamental question it answers

Hold your hand above a candle and then beside it. Conduction alone, through still air, would make the two positions feel almost the same. They don't, because the hot air moves, and as it moves it carries its energy upward. That is convection: heat conduction in a fluid whose every particle is also travelling. The fluid motion does not create a new mechanism of heat transfer. It keeps re-arranging where the temperature gradients are, which makes conduction near the wall far more intense than it would otherwise be.

Engineering wants one number out of all this, the heat transfer coefficient, defined by Newton's law of cooling:

Definition$$q''_w = h\,(T_w - T_\infty),\qquad h = \frac{-k\,(\partial T/\partial y)_{y=0}}{T_w - T_\infty},\qquad \mathrm{Nu} = \frac{hL}{k}$$

The second form is the important one. At the wall the fluid does not slip, so heat crosses the last molecular layer by pure conduction. The heat transfer coefficient is nothing more than the fluid's conductivity times the wall temperature gradient, divided by the overall temperature difference. Every result in this course is a way of finding that wall gradient, \((\partial T/\partial y)_0\), without solving the full flow problem in detail. The Nusselt number is the same gradient made dimensionless: \(\mathrm{Nu} = -\partial\tilde T/\partial\tilde y\) at the wall, a ratio of the actual heat flux to the flux pure conduction across \(L\) would carry.

Physical interpretation

\(h\) is not a property of the fluid. It is a property of the flow: of the geometry, the velocity, the fluid and the temperature difference all together. Air can give \(h = 5\) W/m²K in a quiet room, 100 W/m²K under a fan, and liquid nitrogen boiling on a cold wall can give \(10^4\). The whole subject is about predicting \(h\).

SituationTypical \(h\) (W/m²K)
Free convection, gases2 – 25
Free convection, liquids50 – 1000
Forced convection, gases25 – 250
Forced convection, liquids100 – 20 000
Boiling and condensation2500 – 100 000
Lecture 2

Conservation of mass and momentum in a moving fluid

Follow a small parcel of fluid. Any property \(\phi\) it carries changes for two reasons: \(\phi\) is changing in time at a fixed point, and the parcel moves to places where \(\phi\) is different. The rate seen by the travelling parcel is the material derivative:

$$\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf v\cdot\nabla\phi$$

Mass in a fixed control volume can change only by net inflow, \(\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v) = 0\), or in material form \(D\rho/Dt + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf v = 0\). For the liquids and low-speed gas flows that fill this course, the density of each parcel barely changes, so

Incompressible continuity$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0$$

Newton's second law for the parcel, with pressure and viscous stresses on its faces and gravity acting on its mass, gives the Navier–Stokes equations for a Newtonian fluid with constant viscosity:

Navier–Stokes$$\rho\frac{D\mathbf v}{Dt} = -\nabla P + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf v + \rho\mathbf g$$

Scale every velocity by \(U\), every length by \(L\) and pressure by \(\rho U^2\). The equation becomes \(D\tilde{\mathbf v}/D\tilde t = -\tilde\nabla\tilde P + \mathrm{Re}^{-1}\tilde\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf v} + \ldots\), with the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re} = UL/\nu\) measuring inertia against friction. A large Re does not mean friction is negligible everywhere. It means friction is confined to thin regions where gradients are steep enough to compete with inertia. Those regions are boundary layers, and Module 2 is about them.

Lecture 3

The first law for a moving fluid

Apply the first law to the parcel: its enthalpy rises through conduction into it, through pressure work, and through viscous heating. In terms of temperature,

$$\rho c_P\frac{DT}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + \beta T\frac{DP}{Dt} + \mu\Phi + q''',\qquad \Phi = 2\left[\left(\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)^2\right] + \left(\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \tfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \ldots$$

Here \(\beta\) is the volumetric thermal expansion coefficient (\(\beta = 1/T\) for an ideal gas) and \(\Phi\ge0\) is the viscous dissipation function. For almost every problem in this course the pressure-work and dissipation terms are small, and the energy equation reduces to

Energy equation$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} = \alpha\nabla^2T,\qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c_P}$$

Compare this with the conduction equation: the only new terms are the convective ones on the left. Scaling as before gives the Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe} = UL/\alpha = \mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Pr}\) in front of them, where \(\mathrm{Pr} = \nu/\alpha\) compares how fast momentum and heat diffuse. When is dissipation important? Its ratio to conduction is the Brinkman number, \(\mathrm{Br} = \mu U^2/(k\,\Delta T) = \mathrm{Pr}\,\mathrm{Ec}\). It matters for very viscous oils, lubricating films and high-speed gas flows. It matters in cryogenic pumps too, where a few watts of viscous heating become a few watts of boil-off.

Physical interpretation

The animation shows a warm blob of fluid carried by a uniform stream while it diffuses. At Pe ≪ 1 it spreads almost symmetrically before it has moved far: conduction wins. At Pe ≫ 1 it travels many times its own width almost unchanged: advection wins, and conduction matters only where gradients are steep. The steady profile below it is the same competition between two fixed temperatures. At high Pe, the whole temperature change crowds into a thin layer of thickness \(\sim L/\mathrm{Pe}\) in front of the downstream wall. That crowding is exactly what a thermal boundary layer is.

Lecture 4

The second law: two kinds of irreversibility

Every convective flow destroys useful energy in two ways: heat flowing down a finite temperature gradient, and fluid rubbing against itself. The local rate of entropy generation per unit volume is

Local entropy generation$$S'''_{gen} = \frac{k}{T^2}\left(\nabla T\right)^2 + \frac{\mu}{T}\Phi\ \ge 0$$

Both terms are squares, so both are always positive. An engineer who wants a good heat exchanger faces a tension. Pushing more flow through a narrower passage raises \(h\) and reduces the temperature-gradient term, but raises friction. Opening the passage reduces friction but forces heat across larger temperature differences.

A tube with a fixed duty

Take a tube carrying flow rate \(\dot m\) and receiving heat \(q'\) per unit length. Integrating the local generation over a cross-section gives, per unit length of tube,

$$S'_{gen} = \underbrace{\frac{q'^2}{\pi k T^2\,\mathrm{Nu}}}_{\text{heat transfer}} + \underbrace{\frac{32\,\dot m^3 f}{\pi^2\rho^2T D^5}}_{\text{friction}}$$

With \(\dot m\) fixed, choosing the diameter is the same as choosing \(\mathrm{Re} = 4\dot m/(\pi\mu D)\). In turbulent flow, \(\mathrm{Nu}\propto\mathrm{Re}^{0.8}\) and the Fanning friction factor \(f\propto\mathrm{Re}^{-0.2}\), so the heat-transfer term falls as \(\mathrm{Re}^{-0.8}\) while the friction term climbs as \(\mathrm{Re}^{4.8}\). Setting \(dS'/d\mathrm{Re} = 0\) gives a neat result: at the optimum, the friction term is exactly one sixth of the heat-transfer term. The optimum tube is not frictionless. It deliberately spends a little irreversibility on friction to save more on heat transfer.

Worked example. Water near 300 K (\(\mu = 8.55\times10^{-4}\) Pa s, \(k = 0.613\) W/mK, \(\rho = 996\) kg/m³, \(\mathrm{Pr} = 5.83\)), \(\dot m = 0.1\) kg/s, \(q' = 1000\) W/m. Minimising \(S'_{gen}\) numerically gives \(\mathrm{Re}_{opt}\approx2.35\times10^4\), a tube diameter of about 6.3 mm, and a heat-transfer share of the irreversibility, \(S'_{\Delta T}/S'_{gen} = 6/7\approx0.86\). Change any input in the simulation and the 1 : 6 split stays the same, because it follows from the exponents alone.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

mu, k, rho, cp, T = 8.55e-4, 0.613, 996.0, 4179.0, 300.0   # water near 300 K
Pr = mu*cp/k
mdot, qp = 0.1, 1000.0                                      # kg/s, W/m

def parts(Re):
    D = 4*mdot/(np.pi*mu*Re)
    Nu = 0.023*Re**0.8*Pr**0.4          # Dittus–Boelter (heating)
    f = 0.046*Re**-0.2                   # Fanning friction factor, smooth tube
    S_T = qp**2/(np.pi*k*T**2*Nu)
    S_P = 32*mdot**3*f/(np.pi**2*rho**2*T*D**5)
    return S_T, S_P

res = minimize_scalar(lambda R: sum(parts(R)), bounds=(2e3, 1e6), method='bounded')
S_T, S_P = parts(res.x)
print(f"Re_opt = {res.x:.0f},  D_opt = {4*mdot/(np.pi*mu*res.x)*1e3:.2f} mm")
print(f"S_P / S_T at optimum = {S_P/S_T:.4f}  (theory: 1/6 = {1/6:.4f})")
print(f"heat-transfer share of S_gen = {S_T/(S_T+S_P):.3f}")
const mu=8.55e-4,k=0.613,rho=996,cp=4179,T=300,Pr=mu*cp/k,mdot=0.1,qp=1000;
function parts(Re){ const D=4*mdot/(Math.PI*mu*Re), Nu=0.023*Re**0.8*Pr**0.4, f=0.046*Re**-0.2;
  return [qp*qp/(Math.PI*k*T*T*Nu), 32*mdot**3*f/(Math.PI**2*rho*rho*T*D**5)]; }
const Re=[]; for(let e=3.3;e<=5.3;e+=0.01) Re.push(10**e);
const ST=Re.map(r=>parts(r)[0]), SP=Re.map(r=>parts(r)[1]), S=Re.map((r,i)=>ST[i]+SP[i]);
let i=S.indexOf(Math.min(...S));
print("Re_opt ≈", Re[i].toFixed(0), " S_P/S_T =", (SP[i]/ST[i]).toFixed(3));
plot(Re,[ST,SP,S],{xlabel:'Re',ylabel:"S'gen (W/m K)",labels:['heat transfer','friction','total'],logx:true,logy:true});
Cryogenic angle

At low temperature the \(1/T^2\) and \(1/T\) factors make every irreversibility expensive. A watt of friction or heat transfer loss at 20 K destroys fifteen times more exergy than the same watt at 300 K, and the refrigerator that removes it pays accordingly. Entropy-generation minimisation is the natural design tool for cryogenic heat exchangers, transfer lines and regenerators.

Lecture 5

Scale analysis: the right answer to within a factor of order one

Before solving any equation, ask what sizes each term can have. Scale analysis replaces every derivative by a ratio of characteristic differences and lets the balance of the dominant terms tell you how the unknowns scale. It is a pencil-and-paper method that makes computer results interpretable, and it is used in nearly every lecture that follows. The working rules are:

  1. Define the region you are analysing before you estimate anything. The scales inside a boundary layer are not the scales of the whole flow.
  2. Replace derivatives by differences over that region: \(\partial T/\partial y\sim\Delta T/\delta\), \(\partial^2T/\partial y^2\sim\Delta T/\delta^2\). The symbol ∼ means "same order of magnitude".
  3. In a sum of terms, the largest wins. If \(a\gg b\), then \(a + b\sim a\).
  4. An equation with two dominant terms is a balance. Setting the two scales equal is what determines the unknown.
  5. Check consistency: the terms you discarded must indeed be smaller than the ones you kept, given the result you just found.

Example: a fluid suddenly heated by a wall

A large body of still fluid touches a wall whose temperature jumps by \(\Delta T\). How thick is the heated layer after time \(t\), and what is \(h\)? In the layer of thickness \(\delta\), the energy equation is \(\partial T/\partial t = \alpha\,\partial^2T/\partial y^2\). Scaling both sides gives \(\Delta T/t\sim\alpha\,\Delta T/\delta^2\), so

$$\delta\sim(\alpha t)^{1/2},\qquad h\sim\frac{k\,\Delta T/\delta}{\Delta T} = \frac{k}{(\alpha t)^{1/2}}$$

The exact solution (the error function of conduction theory) gives \(h = k/(\pi\alpha t)^{1/2}\), so the scale estimate is off by only a factor of \(\sqrt\pi\approx1.8\). The method gets the physics and the exponents exactly right and leaves only a constant of order one, which experiments or exact solutions then fix.

Physical interpretation

Scale analysis turns "the heat transfer coefficient depends on everything" into "the heat transfer coefficient is the conductivity divided by the thickness of the thermal layer". In Module 2 the same reasoning, applied to a boundary layer, gives \(\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\) in three lines.

Lecture 6

Heatlines: drawing where the heat goes

Streamlines show where the fluid goes. Isotherms are a poor guide to where the heat goes, because in a moving fluid heat is carried along with the flow as well as conducted across the isotherms. The total energy flux has two parts:

$$\mathbf q = \underbrace{\rho c_P\,\mathbf v\,(T - T_{ref})}_{\text{advected}} - \underbrace{k\nabla T}_{\text{conducted}}$$

In a steady 2D flow without sources, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q = 0\), so we can define a heatfunction \(\mathcal H\) just as the stream function is defined from continuity:

Heatfunction$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial y} = \rho c_Pu\,(T - T_{ref}) - k\frac{\partial T}{\partial x},\qquad -\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial x} = \rho c_Pv\,(T - T_{ref}) - k\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}$$

Lines of constant \(\mathcal H\) are heatlines. Energy flows along them, never across them, and the heat flowing between two heatlines equals the difference in their \(\mathcal H\) values. In still fluid heatlines are perpendicular to isotherms. In fast flow they bend to follow the streamlines.

A heated wire in a uniform stream

A line source releasing \(q'\) W/m sits in a uniform stream \(U\). The steady temperature field has a closed form,

$$T - T_\infty = \frac{q'}{2\pi k}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{Ux}{2\alpha}\right)K_0\!\left(\frac{Ur}{2\alpha}\right)$$

where \(K_0\) is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. The simulation draws this field and traces heatlines from the wire, each pair enclosing the same share of \(q'\). Slide the Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe} = UL/\alpha\) and watch the heatlines swing from a symmetric star (conduction) into a narrow downstream wake (advection).
