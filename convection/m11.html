Convective Mass Transfer

Lecture 57

Mixtures and molecular diffusion

Water evaporating from a lake, a drug dissolving into blood, hydrogen leaking into air, frost growing on a cold pipe: each is the transport of one chemical species through a mixture. The mathematics is almost a copy of heat transfer. Concentration plays the part of temperature, and molecular diffusion plays the part of conduction. Describe the mixture composition with the mass fraction \(\omega_A = \rho_A/\rho\) (or the mole fraction \(x_A\)). Fick's law then states that species \(A\) diffuses down its own concentration gradient:

Fick's law$$\mathbf j_A = -\rho D_{AB}\nabla\omega_A\qquad(\text{compare Fourier: }\mathbf q'' = -k\nabla T)$$

The mass diffusivity \(D_{AB}\) has the same units as \(\alpha\) and \(\nu\), m²/s, and three dimensionless ratios compare the three diffusivities:

$$\mathrm{Sc} = \frac{\nu}{D_{AB}}\ (\text{Schmidt}),\qquad \mathrm{Le} = \frac{\alpha}{D_{AB}}\ (\text{Lewis}),\qquad \mathrm{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha} = \frac{\mathrm{Sc}}{\mathrm{Le}}$$
Pair (1 atm, 298 K unless noted)\(D_{AB}\) (m²/s)Sc
Water vapour – air2.6 × 10⁻⁵0.60
Hydrogen – air7.6 × 10⁻⁵0.21
Helium – air7.2 × 10⁻⁵0.22
Oxygen – nitrogen2.0 × 10⁻⁵0.78
Carbon dioxide – air1.6 × 10⁻⁵0.98
Oxygen dissolved in water2.1 × 10⁻⁹≈ 430

In gases, \(D\) rises roughly as \(T^{3/2}/P\) and Sc is close to one, so momentum, heat and species diffuse at similar rates. In liquids, diffusion is a thousand times slower, Sc runs into the hundreds or thousands, and concentration boundary layers are extremely thin, which is the mass-transfer equivalent of a Pr ≫ 1 fluid.

Lecture 58

Species conservation and boundary conditions

A mass balance on species \(A\) in a moving fluid, with a volumetric production rate \(\dot m'''_A\) from chemical reactions, gives a convection–diffusion equation identical in form to the energy equation:

Species equation$$\frac{\partial\omega_A}{\partial t} + \mathbf v\cdot\nabla\omega_A = D_{AB}\nabla^2\omega_A + \frac{\dot m'''_A}{\rho}$$

The boundary conditions are where mass transfer differs from heat transfer:

  • At a liquid surface, the vapour immediately next to it is saturated: its partial pressure equals the vapour pressure at the surface temperature, \(p_{A,s} = p_{sat}(T_s)\). So the concentration boundary condition depends on the temperature, and evaporation problems couple heat and mass transfer.
  • At a dissolving solid or absorbing liquid, solubility (Henry's law for dilute gases in liquids) fixes the concentration on the fluid side.
  • Net flow through the interface. Evaporation adds mass to the gas, which creates a small outward bulk flow (Stefan flow) that itself carries species. When the transferred species is dilute (\(\omega_{A,s}\lesssim0.1\)), this blowing effect is negligible. This impermeable-surface approximation lets every heat transfer result be borrowed directly.
Lecture 59

The heat and mass transfer analogy

Because the equations and (dilute) boundary conditions have the same form, every convective heat transfer result has a mass transfer twin. Define a mass transfer coefficient by \(j_{A,s} = h_m\rho\,(\omega_{A,s} - \omega_{A,\infty})\) and the Sherwood number \(\mathrm{Sh} = h_mL/D_{AB}\). Then replace

The analogy$$\mathrm{Nu}\to\mathrm{Sh},\qquad \mathrm{Pr}\to\mathrm{Sc},\qquad\text{e.g. laminar plate: }\mathrm{Sh}_x = 0.332\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}\mathrm{Sc}^{1/3}$$

When heat and mass transfer happen together on the same surface, dividing the two results gives the Chilton–Colburn relation between the coefficients:

$$\frac{h}{h_m} = \rho c_P\,\mathrm{Le}^{2/3}$$

This is how evaporation rates are predicted from heat transfer data, and how heat transfer coefficients are measured by mass transfer. In the naphthalene-sublimation technique, a model is cast in naphthalene, exposed to the flow, and the local depth of material lost is converted into local \(h\). The precision is better than most thermal methods can achieve.

The wet-bulb thermometer

A thermometer wrapped in wet cloth, in moving air, settles where the heat convected to it exactly supplies the latent heat of evaporation: \(h(T_\infty - T_s) = h_mh_{fg}(\rho_{v,s} - \rho_{v,\infty})\). With the analogy this becomes an equation in \(T_s\) alone, independent of the air speed. The wet-bulb temperature is therefore a property of the air, which is why it measures humidity. It also sets the coldest temperature an evaporative cooler or cooling tower can reach.

Lecture 60

Concentration-driven buoyancy and turbulent mass transfer

Composition changes density just as temperature does, \(\rho\approx\rho_\infty[1 - \beta(T - T_\infty) - \beta_c(\omega - \omega_\infty)]\). Concentration differences therefore drive natural convection with a mass-transfer Grashof number \(\mathrm{Gr}_m = g\beta_c\Delta\omega L^3/\nu^2\). A wall that releases a light gas such as helium, hydrogen or humid air develops a rising concentration boundary layer exactly like a heated wall. When both temperature and concentration act, the two buoyancy effects add or oppose. A cold, dense wall that releases a light vapour can drive two opposing layers, and in liquids, where heat diffuses about a hundred times faster than salt, opposing gradients produce "salt fingers" and layered convection.

In turbulent flow the analogy holds with an eddy diffusivity for mass, \(\varepsilon_D\), and a turbulent Schmidt number \(\mathrm{Sc}_t\approx0.7\)–\(0.9\). The tube correlations of Module 8 become, for example, \(\mathrm{Sh}_D = 0.023\,\mathrm{Re}_D^{4/5}\mathrm{Sc}^{0.4}\).

Cryogenic angle: frost and cryopumping

A cold surface in humid air is a mass-transfer sink. Water vapour diffuses through the concentration boundary layer and deposits directly as frost below 0 °C. The deposition rate follows from the analogy, \(\dot m''\approx h_m(\rho_{v,\infty} - \rho_{v,sat}(T_s))\), and at cryogenic surface temperatures \(\rho_{v,sat}\approx0\). Ambient-air vaporisers for LNG and liquid nitrogen are designed around the resulting frost layer, which grows thick enough to choke the air passages and must be defrosted periodically. The same mechanism, run deliberately in a vacuum, is the cryopump: surfaces at 10–20 K freeze out every gas except helium, hydrogen and neon.

Lecture 61

Ventilated enclosures: contaminant removal and oxygen deficiency

A room of volume \(V\) is ventilated with fresh air at a volumetric rate \(Q\), and a contaminant is released inside it at a rate \(\dot V_c\). If the room is well mixed, a species balance on the whole room gives an ODE. Each air change replaces part of the contaminated air:

$$V\frac{dc}{dt} = \dot V_c - (Q + \dot V_c)\,c\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad c(t) = c_{ss}\left(1 - e^{-(Q + \dot V_c)t/V}\right),\quad c_{ss} = \frac{\dot V_c}{Q + \dot V_c}$$

The time constant is the inverse of the air-change rate. Real rooms are not well mixed. A ventilation effectiveness, the ratio of the concentration at the exhaust to that at the occupied zone, accounts for short-circuiting between supply and exhaust and for stagnant corners. The heat-and-mass tools of this course, plume entrainment, gravity currents and stratification, decide where the contaminant actually goes.

Oxygen deficiency from cryogens

The most important application in a cryogenic laboratory is asphyxiation. One litre of liquid nitrogen becomes about 0.69 m³ of gas at room temperature. Nitrogen is colourless, odourless and not toxic. It simply displaces oxygen, and a person breathing oxygen-deficient air may lose consciousness without warning. Workplace practice treats air below 19.5% oxygen by volume as oxygen-deficient. Well below that, at around 10% and less, a person can collapse after only a few breaths.

Worked example. 10 litres of liquid nitrogen spill and evaporate quickly in a closed 50 m³ room. The 6.9 m³ of nitrogen gas pushes an equal volume of the room's mixed air out through gaps. If the room stays well mixed, the oxygen fraction falls to \(20.9\%\times\exp(-6.9/50) = 18.2\%\), already below the 19.5% limit. Because cold nitrogen gas is denser than air, the real concentration near the floor will be lower still. Continuous boil-off of 5 L/h into a 30 m³ room with one air change per hour settles at 18.7%. The simulation lets you size the ventilation needed to keep a room safe.
