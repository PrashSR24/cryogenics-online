Lab: Cooling down safely

The cooldown procedure step by step, how much cryogen a cooldown really uses, and why the cold vapour is worth more than the liquid.

Cooling an experiment down is the most expensive and the riskiest routine step in a cryogenic laboratory. Done well, it takes a predictable time and a predictable amount of cryogen. Done badly, it wastes litres of liquid helium, cracks joints and freezes moisture into valves. This lesson is general laboratory practice, not taken from the lectures. Follow your own institution's procedures and safety rules for every cryogen.

The routine, step by step

  1. Check the vacuum. Never cool a cryostat whose insulating vacuum is poor. Confirm the pressure, and log it.
  2. Check the instruments. Confirm that every thermometer reads room temperature, the heater resistance is right, and the logging works. It is much easier to fix a wire now than at 77 K.
  3. Purge the lines. Evacuate or flush the fill and vent lines with dry gas so that no air or moisture is left to freeze. Air in a helium space freezes solid and blocks the path.
  4. Precool with liquid nitrogen. For a system going to 4 K, bring it to about 77 K first, with liquid nitrogen in a jacket or by direct fill. This is vastly cheaper than using helium for the same job, as the calculator shows.
  5. Remove the nitrogen. Liquid nitrogen must be completely removed from the helium space before helium goes in; any left would freeze and block the line.
  6. Transfer the cryogen slowly. Start with a low flow so the cold gas does most of the cooling, and open up as the system cools. Watch the temperatures and the pressure.
  7. Respect the cooling rate. Large temperature differences between parts cause thermal stress. Fragile parts (ceramics, epoxy joints, glass-to-metal seals, superconducting magnets) have rate limits in their manuals, often a few kelvin per minute.
  8. Hold and log. When the temperature is reached, record the time, the cryogen used, and the final temperatures. They are your baseline for next time.

Why the vapour matters

When a liquid cryogen evaporates, it first absorbs its latent heat, then the cold vapour absorbs more heat as it warms up towards room temperature. The vapour's sensible heat is often far bigger than the latent heat. For nitrogen, warming the vapour from 77 K to 300 K absorbs about 230 J/g, against 199 J/g of latent heat. For helium the gap is huge: the vapour absorbs about 1500 J/g warming from 4.2 K to 300 K, against only 20.9 J/g of latent heat, about 70 times more. So the cheapest cooldown is the one that makes the cold gas flow past everything on its way out, and the most expensive is the one that dumps liquid and lets the cold vapour escape.

Cooldown simulator

The simulator treats the cold mass as a single lump. Its heat capacity comes from the Debye model, using Debye temperatures of 343 K for copper, 428 K for aluminium and 470 K for steel. The model is accurate for copper and aluminium to within a few percent; for stainless steel it is rougher (about 15% low), because of magnetic and electronic contributions it leaves out. You choose how hard the cooling source works and the safe cooling-rate limit. The lump cools according to dT/dt = −hA(T − Tbath)/(mc), capped at your rate limit.

What a cooldown costs

The cryogen needed is the heat to be removed divided by what each gram can absorb. With only the latent heat used, that is the enthalpy change divided by the latent heat. Using a share of the vapour's cold as well, the number of grams falls sharply. The simulator reports both figures for liquid nitrogen from 300 K to 77 K, and for liquid helium from 77 K to 4.2 K. It also shows what it would cost to go straight from 300 K to 4.2 K with helium, without a nitrogen precool, so you can see why laboratories nearly always precool.

Common ways a cooldown goes wrong

  • Moisture and air. Water vapour and air freeze in the lines. Purge with dry gas and check for leaks before cooling.
  • Cooling too fast. Parts of different mass cool at different rates, so stress builds at joints. Slow the transfer if the temperature differences grow.
  • Transferring against a warm line. The first liquid evaporates on the warm transfer line and wastes cryogen. Cool the line first and let the flow settle.
  • Pressure. Cold gas and boiling liquid raise the pressure. Keep the vent path open and the relief valve in place.
  • A thermal short. If the system warms or cools unevenly, something is touching that should not. Re-check the supports and wires at the next warm-up.
