Module 11 · Systems and storage

Pressure drop, fittings and two-phase flow

How to add up the losses in a cryogenic line, and what changes when the fluid is half liquid and half vapour.

Where pressure goes

Every component in a cryogenic system (vacuum-jacketed transfer lines, valves, heat exchangers, pumps, expanders) loses pressure, and each loss costs efficiency, because the entropy generated by fluid friction and by heat exchange across a finite temperature difference is the main source of inefficiency. The fluid may be a single-phase liquid, a compressible gas, a supercritical fluid or a two-phase mixture, but most of the fluid mechanics is classical. Pressure is lost to pipe friction, changes of direction, obstructions and changes of cross-section.

Pipe friction

For incompressible single-phase flow, ΔP = 4 fF (L/Dh) (ρu²/2), where fF is the Fanning friction factor (the Darcy factor is four times larger) and the hydraulic diameter is Dh = 4A/S (flow area over wetted perimeter). In a smooth tube: fF = 16/Re for laminar flow (Re < 2000); fF = 0.0791/Re1/4 (Blasius) for turbulent flow up to about 10⁴; and a von Kármán–Nikuradse relation above that. Rough tubes follow Colebrook's formula, which depends on the ratio of roughness to diameter, plotted on the Moody chart. (The previous piping lesson has calculators for these.)

Fittings: the loss coefficient

Bends, valves, tees, entrances and exits are handled with a resistance coefficient K: ΔP = K ρu²/2. For straight pipe K = f L/D, where f is the friction factor at the flow conditions; fittings are tabulated as an equivalent number of pipe diameters (a standard 90° elbow is 30 fT, for example), as in Crane's Technical Paper 410. Control valves are rated with Cv, the number of US gallons per minute of 60 °F water giving a 1 psi drop. Add K for every component in the circuit and use the total. The lecture's worked example is a 1-inch line (internal diameter 1.049 in, completely turbulent friction factor 0.023): entrance 0.78, three standard elbows at 0.69 each, two 24-inch straight runs at 0.53 each and a sharp exit of 1.0, total K = 4.91.

Compressible flow

When the density changes along the line (an isenthalpic expansion of a real gas, or a heated channel in which warming accelerates the fluid) the momentum balance gains a term from the changing density: dp/dx = −4(f/D)(ρu²/2) + u² dρ/dx, and the density depends on pressure and temperature through the equation of state. That gives two unknowns, so a second equation is needed: the energy equation in terms of stagnation enthalpy, which brings in the Joule–Thomson coefficient (∂T/∂p at constant enthalpy).

Two-phase flow

Two-phase flow (liquid and vapour together) is common in cryogenics: the output of a JT valve, boil-off in a heated line, a cooldown. It depends on the fluid, how close the pressure and temperature are to saturation, gravity, heat transfer and the rate of phase change, and it is mostly empirical. The patterns (the Baker map has eight regimes in a horizontal pipe, from bubbly through slug and annular to mist) change with the velocities of the phases. Three definitions:

  • Void fraction α: the vapour volume fraction of the flow area.
  • Quality χ: the vapour fraction of the mass flow.
  • Slip ratio S: vapour speed over liquid speed, S = χ(1 − α)ρl / ((1 − χ)αρv).

The total pressure drop is the sum of gravity (elevation), acceleration (the vapour speeds up as the liquid boils) and friction terms. For friction, a two-phase multiplier φ² relates the loss to that of a single phase flowing alone. Two standard models:

  • Homogeneous (S = 1; the phases move at the same speed and are in equilibrium): works best for helium at high Reynolds number. With the Blasius law, φlo² = [1 + χ(ρl/ρv − 1)] [1 + χ(μl/μv − 1)]−1/4, relative to the same total flow as liquid.
  • Lockhart–Martinelli (separated flow, equal static pressure drop in the two phases): works well for nitrogen and hydrogen at moderate flow. The correlating parameter X compares the pressure drops of the pure liquid and pure vapour, Xtt = ((1 − χ)/χ)0.9 (ρv/ρl)0.5 (μl/μv)0.1 for both phases turbulent, and φl² = 1 + C/X + 1/X² (equivalently (X² + CX + 1)/X²) with C = 20 for turbulent–turbulent flow.

Densities are the saturated values from the lecture (nitrogen 807 and 4.6 kg/m³, helium 125 and 16.7 kg/m³); viscosities are standard handbook values (nitrogen liquid 1.6×10⁻⁴ and vapour 5.4×10⁻⁶ Pa·s; helium liquid 3.3×10⁻⁶ and vapour 1.0×10⁻⁶) and are not from the lecture. Notice how a tiny mass fraction of vapour occupies most of the pipe: 10% quality in nitrogen is about 95% void. The pressure drop of the mixture is a large multiple of the liquid alone, which is why cooldown lines choke and why two-phase lines are sized generously.
