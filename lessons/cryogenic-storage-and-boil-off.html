Module 11 · Systems and storage

Cryogenic storage and boil-off

From a one-litre flask to a 160,000 m³ tank: how big vessels are insulated and why bigger is better.

Why storage matters

Cryogens have almost no shelf life without good containers: an uninsulated vessel can boil dry in minutes. Storage is needed for transport, for buffering inside every liquefaction plant, for rocket propellants on the ground and in flight, and for science. Vessel sizes span one litre to about 42 million US gallons (162,000 m³). A cryostat is a vessel that contains a cryogen, usually to cool a superconducting magnet or experiment; a dewar is the same thing, named for Sir James Dewar; a storage vessel is a dewar for holding and transporting liquid.

Multi-layer insulation (MLI)Many aluminised-Mylar layers approximate a stack of shieldsRecommended loads: 1 to 1.5 W/m² from 300 K to 77 K, and 50 to 100 mW/m² from 77 K to 4 K. Must be in vacuum; wrap in parallel layers, not tightly, or conduction along the layers shorts it

For n shields of emissivity ε the radiation flux is reduced by a factor of about n + 1 when ε is small. With one floating shield between 300 K and 77 K the shield settles at Ts = [(T₁⁴ + T₂⁴)/2]1/4 ≈ 252 K, not halfway, because radiation is not linear in temperature. A refrigerated 77 K shield around an LHe or LH₂ vessel cuts the radiant load by about 250 times, and the boil-off it causes is cheap LN₂. Try it in the shield simulator in the insulation lesson. Here we look at what happens to boil-off as the vessel gets bigger.

Boil-off rate

The heat leak Q turns liquid into vapour at ṁ = Q/hfg. The normal evaporation rate, in percent of the contents per day, is NER = (ṁ × 24 / (V ρ)) × 100. Hold time is how long a closed container keeps its contents before pressure builds up and gas must be vented; it ranges from days for storage to months for transport, and ISO tank containers can cross the globe without losing cargo.

Why size and shape matter

  • Vacuum-insulated vessels of similar shape: heat leak is proportional to area, Q ∝ V2/3, so NER ∝ V−1/3.
  • Powder-insulated vessels: the insulation thickness scales with the vessel, so Q ∝ L ∝ V1/3 and NER ∝ V−2/3. Powder wins decisively for the biggest tanks.
  • Shape: a sphere has the largest volume for its area and the lowest stresses, but is costly to build at scale. A cylinder with dished or elliptical ends is only about 10% worse and is easy to fabricate. Long road and rail tanks have a fixed diameter, so their NER depends on length and radius.

Real examples from the lectures: NASA's launch-pad tanks hold 900,000 gal of liquid oxygen and 1,250,000 gal of liquid hydrogen; a 1,000 m³ LNG tank is rated at an evaporation rate of 0.05% per day. Hydrogen's low density and latent heat make it far harder to store than nitrogen, so large LH₂ tanks often combine powder insulation with an LN₂-cooled shield.
