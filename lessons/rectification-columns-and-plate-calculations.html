Module 5 · Gas separation

Rectification columns and plate calculations

How a distillation column makes pure nitrogen and oxygen, and how to count the plates it needs.

Inside a rectification column

A rectification column is a vertical, well-insulated vessel, often spherical for the 1 to 5 atm used in cryogenic plants. A condenser sits in the top dome and a boiler (reboiler) at the bottom. Inside are horizontal plates with holes, through which vapour rises in small bubbles, and downcomers, through which liquid falls from plate to plate. The two-phase feed enters part way up.

On every plate the rising vapour and the falling liquid meet and exchange heat and matter. The vapour cools slightly and loses some oxygen, the liquid warms slightly and loses some nitrogen. Repeating this up the column enriches the vapour in the low-boiling component (nitrogen) and the liquid in the high-boiling one (oxygen). Heat is removed at the top (the condenser load QD) to make liquid reflux, and added at the bottom (QB) to evaporate it. Infinite plates would give 100% pure products, so size and cost set the achievable purity.

Ideal plates and Murphree efficiency

On an ideal plate the vapour and liquid leaving are in equilibrium, which can be traced on the temperature–composition diagram with isotherms and constant-composition lines. A real plate falls short. The Murphree efficiency compares the real change in vapour composition with the ideal one:

ηM = (yj − yj−1) / (y0,j − yj−1)

Here yj−1 is the vapour entering plate j from below, yj the vapour actually leaving, and y0,j the vapour that would leave at equilibrium. The lecture's examples at 1 atm give ηM = (0.84 − 0.5)/(0.85 − 0.5) = 0.97, and at 2 atm 0.843. High efficiency needs small bubbles, long contact time and high heat and mass transfer coefficients.

The McCabe–Thiele method

Plate counts can be found by the Ponchon–Savarit method (general, uses enthalpy–composition diagrams), by numerical methods, or by the graphical McCabe–Thiele method of 1925. It is less general but easy to follow. It assumes the molar flows of liquid and vapour stay constant along each section, which corresponds to flat enthalpy lines, so the liquid and vapour flow rates L and V are constant in each section.

With D the distillate, B the bottoms, and xD and xB their mole fractions of the light component, mole and energy balances on the enriching (upper) and stripping (lower) sections give two straight operating lines:

Enriching: yn = (Ln+1/Vn) xn+1 + (D/Vn) xD
Stripping: ym = (Lm+1/Vm) xm+1 − (B/Vm) xB

Each passes through the diagonal y = x at its product composition. Ln+1/Vn = 1 − D/Vn is called the internal reflux ratio. The feed condition sets the q line, y = qx/(q − 1) − xF/(q − 1), where q is the liquid fraction of the feed. Saturated vapour feed has q = 0 (horizontal line), saturated liquid q = 1 (vertical), a two-phase feed 0 < q < 1, subcooled liquid q > 1 and superheated vapour q < 0. The operating lines meet on the q line.

To find the plates, start at the top product (xD, xD) on the diagonal and step: horizontally to the equilibrium curve (the liquid leaving a plate is in equilibrium with the vapour leaving it), then vertically to the operating line (that gives the vapour rising from the next plate). Switch to the stripping line once past the feed. Continue until x falls to xB. Each horizontal run is one equilibrium stage.

Reflux: the central trade-off

  • Total reflux. All vapour from the top is condensed and returned (L/V = 1). The operating lines lie on the diagonal and the column needs the fewest plates, but no product comes out.
  • Minimum reflux. As reflux falls the operating lines move toward the equilibrium curve, the plate count rises, and it grows without limit when the operating lines meet the curve. The lecture also notes the lines cannot cross it, since that would violate mass conservation. Condenser load and refrigeration cost are lowest here, and a finite product flows.
  • In practice a compromise is used: 1.15 to 1.35 times the minimum reflux in ordinary distillation, and 1.05 to 1.15 times in cryogenic columns, where refrigeration is expensive.

Asking for higher top purity or lower bottom impurity adds plates, and the feed condition q fixes the slope of the q line and so the approximate feed position. Because a change in D, QD, B or QB alters the others through the mass and energy balances, the design variables cannot be adjusted independently.
