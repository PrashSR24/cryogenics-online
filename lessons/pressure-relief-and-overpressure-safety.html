Module 11 · Systems and storage

Pressure relief and overpressure safety

Why one litre of liquid can burst a steel vessel, and how relief valves, rupture discs and the codes prevent it.

Energy-based hazard thinking

Every injury is the unwanted release of, and contact with, an energy source: mechanical, electrical, pressure or temperature. A cryogenic system holds a lot of the last two. The hazards the lectures list are extreme cold, oxygen deficiency, fire, oxygen enrichment, over-pressurisation or explosion by rapid expansion, high noise and embrittlement of materials. This lesson is about pressure.

Why the pressure is so high

A cryogen warmed in a closed volume expands enormously: nitrogen's liquid density is about 700 to 800 times its gas density at room conditions. Held in a closed volume, that expansion can only turn into pressure. What can go wrong

  • Sudden loss of vacuum. Air floods the insulating space and condenses on cold surfaces, giving a very high heating rate. This is usually the design case for relief sizing.
  • Fire. Extreme external heating of the vessel (sometimes not credible, depending on the installation).
  • Trapped volume. Liquid or cold gas between two closed valves warms slowly from normal heat leak and expands: a relief must be fitted to every such volume.
  • External pressure sources. Compressors or supply valves feeding the vessel; a room-temperature relief on the supply pipe protects it.

Relief devices

A pressure relief valve (PRV or PSV) is a spring-loaded valve that opens at its set pressure and recloses, so the system reseals after an upset. A rupture disk is a sacrificial diaphragm: cheaper at high venting capacity and good for rare events, but it works once. For a normally sub-atmospheric volume such as a 2 K system, the inlet flange must be sealed or guarded against in-leaking air; a rupture disk avoids that problem, or a guard-gas volume can sit between disk and valve. Relief valves are needed on every vessel and pipe that contains cryogen, or could under a failure (including the vacuum vessel of a cryostat).

The procedure: establish the maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) for every vessel and pipe that may hold cryogen; find the worst-case rate at which fluid must leave to keep the pressure under the MAWP; then show that the relief valves as installed, including pressure drop in the inlet and outlet piping, pass that flow. Codes such as the CGA S-1.3 standard give formulas for loss of vacuum and for fire, and rules for the temperature at the valve inlet: for a liquid relieving below the critical pressure use the saturation temperature at relieving pressure; above it (common for helium, which has the lowest critical pressure of any fluid) the code points to the temperature at which a particular fluid property is maximum.

Sizing by heat input

When measured heat fluxes exist, the required vent rate is simply ṁ = Q/hfg with Q = q × area. The lectures quote experimental peak fluxes for air condensing on a liquid-helium cryostat: about 0.6 W/cm² for a super-insulated vessel and 3.8 W/cm² for an uninsulated one.

A lesson learned

A 50-litre laboratory LN₂ dewar was being filled from a 160-litre dewar. The transfer hose would not fit the neck, so a rubber hose was clamped over the outside of the neck with a slot cut for venting. Cooling of the neck during the fill broke the seal to the vacuum jacket through differential thermal contraction; the dewar was nearly full, the vent was inadequate, and it exploded, badly injuring one person. The lesson: a broken insulating vacuum plus restricted venting can destroy even a small dewar, so use the right fill adapter and never improvise a seal.
