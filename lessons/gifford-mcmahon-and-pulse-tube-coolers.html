Module 6 · Cryocoolers

Gifford–McMahon and pulse tube coolers

Valves instead of a direct drive, and a cooler with no moving part at the cold end.

Gifford–McMahon (GM)

W. E. Gifford and H. O. McMahon introduced valves into a regenerative cooler in 1950. A compressor feeds high- and low-pressure lines, and valves open and close in sequence to produce the pressure pulse. A displacer containing the regenerator moves between a warm space and a cold space. The valve timing relative to the displacer position is vital. Commercial machines use rotary valves, but designs with poppet or solenoid valves also exist.

In outline: with the high-pressure valve open the gas fills the system; the displacer then moves so that gas passes through the regenerator and is cooled; the low-pressure valve opens and the gas expands in the cold space, absorbing heat; finally the displacer pushes the cold gas back through the regenerator at constant pressure, cooling it. The regenerator material sets the range: a single stage with stainless steel mesh reaches about 30 K; two stages reach about 10 K, and about 4.2 K with lead in the regenerator.

The pulse tube cooler

Moving displacers wear, vibrate and need rubbing seals at low temperature. The pulse tube cooler removes the cold displacer altogether: an oscillating gas flow in a thin-walled tube does the job. Its parts are the compressor, an aftercooler, the regenerator, a cold-end heat exchanger, the pulse tube, a hot-end heat exchanger and a phase-shift mechanism.

When the compressor pressurises the system, high-pressure gas flows through the regenerator, where it is precooled, into the pulse tube and compresses the gas already at the top, which warms. That heat is rejected at the hot end, held at ambient by water. During depressurisation the gas in the tube expands and cools, and the cold gas returning through the regenerator precools the next incoming gas. The cooling effect is lifted at the cold-end heat exchanger. Because no mechanical drive moves the gas in the tube, vibration is lower than in Stirling and GM machines.

The advantages: no moving parts at the cold end, no low-temperature seals, long life. The drawbacks the lecture lists: orientation effects, and for orifice types, a small orifice that can clog.

Why the phase matters: phasor analysis

The lecture analyses the orifice pulse tube for a monatomic gas, assuming sinusoidal pressure and temperature in the tube and adiabatic gas behaviour. The mass flow at the cold end is the sum of two vectors, and it lies at a phase angle to the pressure. With perfect regeneration, the heat lifted at the cold end is the time-averaged enthalpy flow through the pulse tube, which depends on that phase angle and on the pressure amplitude. A pulse tube cooler therefore needs a phase-shift mechanism to put the mass flow and pressure in step: an orifice, a longer inertance tube, or a double-inlet valve. The lecture later maps the system onto an electrical analogy.
