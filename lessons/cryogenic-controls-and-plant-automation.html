Module 11 · Systems and storage

Cryogenic controls and plant automation

From the 1963 first digital loop to today's PLCs and SCADA, and an interactive loop you can tune yourself.

What a control system is

A cryogenic plant is a physical process: compressors, a cold box, valves, dewars, a distribution system. The control system wraps a second, electronic system around it. Sensors convert physical conditions into (noisy) electrical signals. Actuators (valves, heaters, motor drives) convert signals into (imperfect) physical action. The controller uses what is known about the process to decide what the actuators should do, so that the plant approximates the behaviour you want. Sensors and actuators matter more than they seem: they decide what the controller can see and what it can influence.

Good control design starts with understanding the process, often through a mathematical model. A model quantifies insight and sometimes creates it, but you have to balance its accuracy against the insight it gives, and controller performance against how complicated it is to build and maintain.

A short history

  • 1963: direct digital control at a petrochemical plant.
  • 1970: programmable logic controllers (PLCs) reach the market.
  • 1975: the first distributed control system (DCS), from Honeywell, bringing PID control, flexible software, a networked system with tuning and access from one operator station, and later auto-tuning.

SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) sits on top for operators and data. Today industrial digital control is almost a commodity, so the difficulty is deploying and maintaining the huge number of loops in a big plant, not the algorithms (PID is still the workhorse).

Try a PID loop

A typical cryogenic loop is a thermal mass cooled through a valve: the process responds slowly (a first-order lag) and with a delay (the dead time for the cold stream to arrive). A PID controller adds a proportional term (react to the error now), an integral term (remove steady offset by accumulating past error) and a derivative term (react to how fast the error is changing). This simulation is an illustration, not a model of any particular plant: a process with a 20 s time constant and a 3 s dead time, a valve limited between closed and fully open, and a derivative term applied to the error. Too much gain makes the loop ring and eventually oscillate; too little leaves it sluggish; the integral term removes the lasting offset after the disturbance but slows the response if it is too strong.

How a plant control system is built

A large helium plant such as the cryogenic systems of PIP-II at Fermilab or the LCLS-II linac at SLAC shows the usual layers:

  • I/O modules interface with the hardware (resistance temperature inputs, analogue in and out, digital in and out) and convert raw readings into engineering units.
  • PLCs are the main controllers: they run the limits and alarms, interlocks and bypasses, PID loops, a local display and some integrated functions (heater control, cold compressor pump-down). They are built for harsh industrial use, are compact, can be redundant, and have low failure rates. Control loops inside the PLC keep running even if the operator interface or the network goes down.
  • Input/output controllers (IOCs) on a server publish every sensor value, setpoint, limit, alarm, interlock, bypass and PID parameter as named process variables, and can run automated sequences.
  • User interfaces and high-level applications on operator machines show status, allow setpoint changes and automate processes through the same variables.
  • Networks join PLCs to each other (for soft interlocks between subsystems, alarms and coordination) and to the supervisory layer, with field buses (Profibus DP, Profibus PA, EtherNet/IP) reaching valve positioners and temperature monitors.

Positioners in a particle accelerator tunnel must tolerate radiation: for example a radiation-resistant pneumatic positioner design originally developed for the LHC is used inside the tunnel, with a commercial positioner outside the radiation area.

From requirements to operation

The lecture's development chain runs from process requirements (process calculations, operating modes) through the process flow diagram and the piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) to a functional analysis, then to control loops, alarm, trip and interlock definitions, logic diagrams, PLC programs and operator displays (synoptics), and finally to an operating manual and procedures, then transfer to the operations team. Process engineers, process control engineers and controls engineers each own a stretch of the chain. Operating modes must be defined up front: commissioning modes (leak test, pressure test, controls checkout, purge and fill), normal modes such as plant start-up and cool-down, closed-loop purification, and outage modes for power, cooling water, instrument air, network or controls failure, plus an emergency shutdown. Each piece of equipment is marked against each mode so that nothing is forgotten.
