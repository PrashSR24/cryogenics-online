Transient conduction and cooldown

How a solid cools down in time: the Biot number, the Fourier number, and when you can treat a whole object as one lump.

Steady state tells you what a cryostat does after days of running. But much of an experiment is spent in the transient: the hours when a magnet, a sample holder or a heavy copper plate is cooling down. This lesson is general physics, not taken from the lectures. The model uses constant material properties at their room-temperature values, so it gives a first estimate only: the heat capacity of a solid falls steeply as it gets colder, so the last tens of kelvin go much faster than this model shows.

The equation that governs it

Heat flows in, heat is stored, and the temperature changes. For one dimension, the heat equation is

∂T/∂t = α ∂²T/∂x², with α = k / (ρ c)

The thermal diffusivity α, in m²/s, measures how fast a temperature change spreads through a material: a large k carries the heat quickly, a large heat capacity ρc slows it down. Diffusion takes a time of about d²/α to cross a thickness d. Copper has α about 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s, so a 1 cm thick plate equilibrates in about a second; stainless steel (α about 4 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s) takes about 25 seconds; G-10 (about 3 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s) takes about 5 minutes.

Two numbers that tell you what will happen

  • The Biot number, Bi = h d / k, compares the resistance to heat flow inside the solid (d/k) with the resistance at its surface (1/h). Here h is the surface heat transfer coefficient and d the thickness (for a plate cooled on one face and insulated on the other, the full thickness).
  • The Fourier number, Fo = α t / d², is the time measured in units of the diffusion time.

If Bi is small (below about 0.1), the inside of the solid stays almost uniform while it cools, because the heat passes easily through the solid and struggles to get across the surface. Then you can treat the whole object as one lump, and its temperature simply decays exponentially, T − Tbath = (T0 − Tbath) exp(−t/τ), with τ = ρ c d / h for a plate of thickness d cooled on one side. If Bi is large, the surface is quickly at the bath temperature and the inside lags behind; the cooling is controlled by diffusion, with time scale about 4d²/(π²α) for a plate cooled on one face.

See it happen

The animation cools a plate that starts at 300 K, with its left face in contact with a 77 K bath and its right face insulated. The coloured bar shows the temperature through the thickness; the left graph shows the profiles at different times; the right graph shows the left (surface) and right (far face) temperatures against time, together with the lumped estimate as a dashed line. The solver is a standard implicit finite-difference scheme with 40 cells.

Try copper at h = 150 W/m²·K, which is a typical order of magnitude for film boiling in liquid nitrogen (a later lesson covers boiling): the Biot number is tiny, the plate cools as one lump, and the dashed line follows the solid curves. Now switch to stainless steel or G-10: the Biot number rises, and the far face lags far behind the surface. Raise h to a few thousand, as in good nucleate boiling, and even copper starts to show gradients. The practical lessons:

  • For a metal plate in a poor-heat-transfer environment, the surface limits the cooldown. Spend your effort on better contact with the coolant, not on the thickness.
  • For a poor conductor (G-10, stainless steel, a thick epoxy layer), the inside lags, and cooling too fast creates large temperature differences and thermal stress. This is why parts of different material and mass are cooled slowly.
  • A part's cooldown time scales with its heat content: a heavy copper block takes longer to cool than a thin one by the ratio of masses, not because copper conducts slowly, but because there is a lot of heat to take out.
Key points
  • Diffusivity α = k/ρc sets how fast temperature changes spread; time to cross d is about d²/α
  • Biot number Bi = h d / k: below about 0.1, treat the object as one lump
  • Lumped time constant τ = ρ c d / h; diffusion-limited time about 4d²/(π²α)
  • Constant-property estimates are rough: heat capacity falls steeply on cooling
