Module 10 · Safety

Safety in cryogenics

The six basic hazards and how engineers and users protect against them.

Cryogens are liquefied gases stored at very low temperature: helium, nitrogen, argon, hydrogen and oxygen are the commonly used ones. Cold alone changes materials: rubber and organic matter turn brittle, and materials shrink, which can break parts or open leaks. Boil-off in a closed container can raise pressure suddenly. Handling cryogens therefore needs training and precautions.

The six basic hazards

HazardWhat happens
1. Extreme coldBare skin or eyes exposed to cryogen or cold vapour can be burnt in as little as about 5 seconds; inhaling very cold vapour damages the lungs; prolonged contact with cold surfaces causes frostbite; skin can stick to a cold metal pipe and tear when pulled away
2. Oxygen deficiencyBreathing air needs about 19.5% oxygen at minimum; below that, a few minutes can bring choking, unconsciousness or death (asphyxiation). It is the most dangerous hazard because oxygen is odourless, colourless and gives no warning
3. Oxygen enrichmentCold boil-off or cold pipes can liquefy air. Nitrogen has a much lower latent heat than oxygen, so it evaporates first, leaving an oxygen-rich liquid; with a flammable material this is a fire risk
4. FireHydrogen and oxygen are flammable; hydrogen is colourless, odourless and explosive in air in the right proportions
5. ExplosionVessels are closed and boil-off is continuous; without venting the pressure rises. Nitrogen and helium have low latent heats and big expansion ratios, so the rise can be sudden
6. EmbrittlementFace-centred cubic metals stay ductile at low temperature; body-centred cubic ones become brittle. Thermal stresses plus embrittlement can break pipes or supports

Oxygen deficiency in a room

The expansion ratio of a cryogen warming from its boiling point to ambient is of the order of 1:1000, and one litre of liquid nitrogen displaces about 700 litres of air. Nitrogen and argon vapour are heavier than air, so they pool and do not disperse; hydrogen and helium mix and stratify but still deplete oxygen. Protection

  • Personal protective equipment: a face shield and safety goggles for any transfer, loose-fitting insulated gloves that cryogen cannot flow into, high-top safety shoes, and overalls or lab coats covering all skin. No rings, watches or jewellery, because metal can freeze to the skin.
  • Procedure: only trained and qualified personnel handle, transport or store liquefied gases; follow standard operating procedures; never overfill; pour or transfer slowly to limit boiling and splashing; direct all vents away from people and work areas. Placards and, for hydrogen, a flashing blue light warn of the risk.
  • Oxygen detectors in risk areas read 0 to 25%. A first-stage warning sounds when oxygen falls below 19%, and a second, danger alarm at 17%.
  • Pressure relief valves: a spring-loaded valve with an adjustable set pressure that opens to vent excess pressure and recloses. They need periodic checks, and electronic versions also monitor the system.
  • Bursting (rupture) discs: a sacrificial metal diaphragm that bursts at a set overpressure or excess vacuum, used as a backup to relief valves. It works once and must be replaced.
