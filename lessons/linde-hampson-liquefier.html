Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Linde–Hampson liquefier

From the energy balance to liquid yield and work, with worked cases.

Why an ideal liquefier is impractical

An ideal cycle compresses all the gas isothermally and then expands it reversibly until it liquefies, so every kilogram compressed becomes liquid. For nitrogen starting at 300 K and 1 bar, the lectures show the pressure needed would exceed 70,000 bar. Real liquefiers instead use a heat exchanger to recover cold and a Joule–Thomson valve to make the liquid.

The system

Animation: gas is compressed, precooled by the cold return stream, expanded through the valve, and the liquid collects in the receiver.

The compressor is assumed isothermal, the exchanger 100% effective with no pressure drops, and the valve isenthalpic. Only part of the gas liquefies, so this is an open cycle: makeup gas replaces what leaves as liquid.

Liquid yield

An energy balance on the exchanger, valve and receiver gives the fraction liquefied:

y = mf / m = (h1 − h2) / (h1 − hf)

Here state 1 is the low-pressure inlet gas, state 2 is the compressed gas at the same temperature, and f is saturated liquid at the low pressure.

Work

Combining the first law with the second-law heat rejection of an isothermal compressor gives the work input per unit mass of gas compressed:

Wc/m = T1(s1 − s2) − (h1 − h2)

Dividing by y gives the work per unit mass of liquid, the number that really counts.

Choosing the compression pressure

To maximise y you want h2 as small as possible. Along the isothermal line, h2 is lowest where (∂h/∂p)T = −μJT·Cp = 0. Since Cp is positive, this needs μJT = 0, so state 2 should sit on the inversion curve.

Try the worked cases

Key points
  • y = (h1 − h2)/(h1 − hf)
  • Yield rises with higher compression pressure and lower inlet temperature
  • Best yield needs state 2 on the inversion curve
