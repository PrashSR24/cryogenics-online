Module 5 · Gas separation

Air separation and distillation in motion

See how a double column turns compressed air into nitrogen, oxygen and argon, and step off the plates of a binary column to see how many are needed.

Almost all the nitrogen, oxygen and argon in the world is made by cryogenic distillation of liquid air. This lesson animates the two ideas behind it: the double column that links two columns at different pressures, and the plate-by-plate stepping that tells you how tall a column must be. This lesson is general process engineering, not taken from the lectures; the pressures and the layout are the common textbook arrangement, simplified.

The double-column air separation unit

Air is compressed to about 6 bar, cleaned of water vapour and carbon dioxide (which would freeze), and cooled in a large counterflow heat exchanger by the cold products leaving the plant. It enters the bottom of the high-pressure (HP) column. Rising vapour meets falling liquid on the plates: the vapour grows richer in nitrogen, the liquid richer in oxygen. At the top of the HP column, nearly pure nitrogen vapour reaches the condenser–reboiler, where it is condensed against boiling oxygen from the low-pressure (LP) column above, which works at about 1.3 bar. This is the trick: nitrogen at the higher pressure condenses at a higher temperature than oxygen boils at the lower pressure, so the condensing nitrogen provides the heat to boil the oxygen, and the same unit serves as reflux source for one column and as reboiler for the other. Part of the condensed nitrogen returns to the HP column as reflux, part goes to the top of the LP column. The oxygen-rich liquid from the bottom of the HP column is fed to the middle of the LP column. Gaseous nitrogen leaves the top of the LP column and oxygen the bottom; argon, whose boiling point (87.3 K) lies between those of nitrogen (77.4 K) and oxygen (90.2 K) at 1 atm, builds up in the lower middle of the LP column, and a side draw feeds a smaller column that produces argon.

How tall must a column be? The McCabe–Thiele method

On every ideal plate the vapour leaving is in equilibrium with the liquid leaving. For a binary mixture of a light component (nitrogen) and a heavy one (oxygen), the equilibrium curve for a constant relative volatility α is y* = α x / (1 + (α − 1) x), where x and y are the mole fractions of the light component in the liquid and the vapour. Between plates, the compositions of passing streams lie on operating lines: above the feed, y = R/(R+1) x + xD/(R+1), where R is the reflux ratio and xD the top product; below the feed the line runs from the point (xB, xB) at the bottom to its meeting with the first line at x = xF. Stepping between the operating lines and the equilibrium curve counts the plates. The animation does it for you, one plate at a time, and the column on the right colours each plate by the liquid composition on it. The feed is taken as saturated liquid, and the model assumes constant molar flow (a standard simplification). It treats air as a binary mixture of 79 per cent nitrogen and 21 per cent oxygen.

Try it. Raise the purity of the top product and the plate count grows quickly. Lower the reflux towards the minimum, the least that could ever work, and the staircase squeezes against the pinch point where the operating line meets the equilibrium curve: the number of plates rises steeply. Lower α (components that are harder to separate) and the staircase gets shorter steps, so more plates are needed. This is the trade between the capital cost of a taller column and the running cost of more reflux, and it is the central design choice. The minimum number of plates, reached at total reflux, is given by the Fenske equation, Nmin = ln[ (xD/(1 − xD)) ((1 − xB)/xB) ] / ln α.

Key points
  • A double column couples a high-pressure and a low-pressure column through one condenser–reboiler
  • Nitrogen condensing at 6 bar boils oxygen at 1.3 bar: the same exchanger is condenser and reboiler
  • The McCabe–Thiele staircase counts ideal plates between the operating lines and the equilibrium curve
  • More reflux means fewer plates but more energy; the minimum reflux needs infinitely many plates
