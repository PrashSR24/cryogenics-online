Boiling in motion: bubbles, burnout and hysteresis

Watch what happens to a heated surface in liquid nitrogen as the heat flux rises past the critical value, and why the way back is not the way up.

A magnet, a sensor or a heater immersed in a bath of cryogen is cooled by boiling. This is a superb way to take away heat, until it suddenly is not. The earlier lesson on convection and boiling gave the correlations; here you can drive a heated surface up and down the boiling curve and see the consequences. This lesson is general heat transfer physics, not taken from the lectures. The curve below is an illustrative model built from the usual relations, with rounded round-number parameters, not measured data for any particular surface.

Three ways a pool boils

  • Convection and nucleate boiling. At small temperature differences, the surface warms the liquid next to it, which rises. When the surface is a few kelvin warmer than the liquid, bubbles form at tiny pits in the surface, rise, and are replaced by liquid. This is very efficient: the heat flux rises steeply with the wall superheat ΔT, roughly as ΔT2.5 in this model, and each bubble carries away latent heat.
  • The critical heat flux. As the flux rises, bubbles crowd together until the vapour leaving the surface blocks the liquid from returning. The surface can no longer be wetted. The peak value is estimated by the Zuber formula, q* = 0.16 hfg ρv1/2 [σ g (ρl − ρv)]1/4. For liquid nitrogen at atmospheric pressure it gives about 20 W/cm² (197 kW/m²), using the empirical constant 0.16.
  • Film boiling. Beyond the peak a continuous vapour film covers the surface. Vapour conducts heat poorly, so the heat transfer coefficient falls to a small value (taken as 150 W/m²·K here, an illustrative figure). To carry the same flux the surface must run far hotter.

Burnout, and why you cannot just turn the power back

When the heat flux is imposed (an electrical heater, a magnet quenching, a sensor dissipating power), the surface cannot choose: the flux is fixed by the source. If it is raised past the critical value, the surface has no nucleate-boiling state that can carry the heat, so it flips to film boiling and its temperature jumps by hundreds of kelvin: burnout. Now lower the flux again. The film is stable, and it stays until the flux falls well below the peak, to the minimum film-boiling flux (the Leidenfrost point, estimated with a similar formula with a constant of 0.09, giving about 0.8 W/cm² for liquid nitrogen). Only then does the liquid re-wet the surface and the temperature crashes back. The path up is not the path down: this is hysteresis.

Press the button and watch the ramp. As the flux rises, the dot moves up the nucleate branch, with more and more bubbles. At about 20 W/cm² the dot jumps to the right, onto the film branch, and the wall temperature leaps. On the way back the dot stays on the film branch right down to about 0.8 W/cm², then falls back to nucleate boiling. You can also drag the slider yourself: it remembers where you came from.

What to do about it

  • Keep the design flux well below the critical heat flux. A safe margin of a factor of two or more is typical, because the real peak depends on the surface, its orientation and the depth of liquid.
  • A magnet or sensor that is well cooled in the nucleate regime may be quite uncooled in film boiling, even at the same power. Never assume that because it worked a minute ago, it still works.
  • For heat flux above the pool-boiling limit, use forced flow, which raises the critical flux, or a different cooling method.
Key points
  • Nucleate boiling is very efficient, with flux rising roughly as ΔT2.5
  • The critical heat flux (about 20 W/cm² for liquid nitrogen by the Zuber estimate) ends it
  • With an imposed flux, passing the peak causes a jump to film boiling and a large temperature rise: burnout
  • Recovery needs the flux to fall to the much lower minimum film-boiling flux: hysteresis
