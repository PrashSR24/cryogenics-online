Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Liquefier cycles in motion

Follow the gas around the loop in the Linde–Hampson, precooled and Claude cycles, and see where the liquid comes from.

Read the loop

Every liquefier does the same six things in the same order. Compress the gas, cool it back to room temperature, cool it further against the cold stream returning from the receiver, expand it through a valve so that a fraction condenses, separate the liquid from the vapour, and send the cold vapour back to chill the next batch of gas. Make-up gas replaces whatever leaves as liquid. The animation below shows these six steps in a plain Linde–Hampson loop. The tabs add the two big improvements: a pre-cooling bath and an expansion engine. Pipe colour runs from red (warm) to blue (cold), and the moving dots show the direction of flow.

What each tab adds

  • Linde–Hampson. The only cooling is the J–T valve, helped by the counterflow exchanger. Only about 2 to 4% of the gas becomes liquid per pass for nitrogen, so the compressor does a great deal of work for little liquid. The yield is y = (h₁ − h₂)/(h₁ − hf).
  • Precooled. A separate refrigerator, such as an R134a loop, chills the high-pressure gas before it enters the exchanger. A lower starting temperature means a larger enthalpy difference across the valve and a higher yield. The yield rises linearly with r until the refrigerant stream reaches its limit.
  • Claude. A fraction x of the compressed gas is diverted to an expansion engine, where it does work and comes out much colder. It then joins the cold return stream. The engine adds a second source of cooling, and the yield rises linearly with x until x + y approaches 1.

The numbers under the figure use the lecture's enthalpies for nitrogen at 300 K: the Linde–Hampson and Claude cases use 1.013 to 40.52 bar or 101.3 bar, the precooled case uses 1.013 to 101.3 bar with R134a. The figure of merit is the ideal work of liquefying nitrogen (767 J/g) divided by the real work per gram liquefied. Values are rounded from the lecture tables, so results are approximate, and the ideal expander and exchanger are assumed.

Why the exchanger is the heart of the machine

The J–T valve only cools the gas by a few kelvin per pass. The counterflow heat exchanger is what lets the cold build up: each trip around the loop starts a little colder than the one before, until the gas reaching the valve is cold enough to condense. Look at the colour gradient in the high-pressure line as you step through the tabs: the pipe is red at the top and deep blue at the bottom because the returning vapour has cooled it. An exchanger that is not effective enough lets the warm end leak cold away and the loop never reaches liquid.
