Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Liquefaction cycles on the T–s diagram

Follow one parcel of nitrogen round the Carnot, ideal, Linde–Hampson, precooled and Claude cycles on a real temperature–entropy diagram, and see where the work goes and where the cold comes from.

The earlier lessons give the energy balances of each liquefier. To see what the gas does, engineers draw the cycle on a temperature–entropy (T–s) diagram. This lesson draws it for nitrogen, using the accurate reference equation of state for nitrogen, so the lines are real, not sketches. The entropy is measured from saturated liquid at 1 atm. This lesson is not taken from the lectures, and the figures it reports can differ a little from the lecture's table values because they come from a different property source.

How to read a T–s diagram

  • The dome is the two-phase region. Inside it, liquid and vapour coexist. Left of the dome is liquid, right of it is gas, and above it, supercritical fluid. The top of the dome is the critical point, 126 K for nitrogen.
  • A horizontal line is isothermal; a vertical line is isentropic (reversible and adiabatic, as in an ideal compressor or expander).
  • The area under a path, ∫T ds, is the heat exchanged in that step. A heat leak into a reversible step moves the path to the right; a heat rejection moves it to the left.
  • Thin curves are isobars (constant pressure). The ones drawn are 1 bar (the low-pressure side) and the cycle's high pressure.
  • Curves of constant enthalpy, isenthalps, can be switched on. A Joule–Thomson expansion follows one of them, always towards higher entropy because it is irreversible.

What to notice

  • Carnot refrigerator. A rectangle running counter-clockwise. The cooling effect is the area under the cold isothermal at 77 K; the work is the enclosed area. The ratio is the Carnot coefficient of performance, Tc/(Th − Tc) = 0.345. It is the limit nothing can beat, but it needs a perfect compressor and expander.
  • Ideal liquefier. Compress isothermally until the entropy has fallen to that of the saturated liquid, then expand isentropically to the liquid state at 77 K. The work is T1(s1 − sf) − (h1 − hf), about 769 kJ per kg of nitrogen liquefied.
  • Linde–Hampson. The isothermal compression removes entropy at 300 K; the counterflow exchanger cools the high-pressure gas along the isobar; the valve then throttles it across a slanting isenthalp into the dome, so only a fraction y comes out as liquid and the rest returns cold. The cold vapour warms along the 1 bar isobar back to the compressor. Raise the pressure and the throttling step becomes longer: more liquid.
  • Precooled. An external refrigerator takes the high-pressure gas from the warm exchanger temperature down to 230 K, so the gas arrives at the valve colder and more of it condenses. Compare y.
  • Claude (schematic). A fraction of the flow does work in an expander at 180 K, which is a nearly vertical, nearly isentropic path to a much lower temperature: far more cooling than throttling. The remainder follows the Joule–Thomson path. The numbers here are illustrative; the yield of a real Claude plant is worked out in its own lesson.

For the Linde–Hampson and precooled cycles the numbers come from the standard energy balance with an ideal exchanger (no temperature difference at the warm end, no pressure drop) and an isothermal compressor: y = (h1 − h2) / (h1 − hf), with the extra precooler heat added for the precooled case. The figure of merit is the ideal work divided by the actual work per kilogram of liquid. At 200 bar the Linde–Hampson cycle reaches about 12 per cent of the ideal: the rest is lost in throttling, an irreversible step.

Key points
  • On a T–s diagram, area under a path is heat; the enclosed area of a cycle is work
  • A throttle (Joule–Thomson valve) follows an isenthalp and always creates entropy; an expander follows a vertical isentrope
  • Higher pressure and precooling both lengthen the cooling step and raise the liquid yield
  • The Linde–Hampson cycle is simple but far from ideal; the figure of merit shows how far
