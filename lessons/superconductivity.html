Module 3 · Materials

Superconductivity

Zero resistance, flux expulsion, critical limits, and the race to higher transition temperatures.

Resistance that vanishes

The electrical resistance of a metal falls as it is cooled, because the scattering of electrons by lattice vibrations dies away, while scattering by impurities and dislocations does not depend on temperature. A few materials go further: their resistance drops suddenly to zero at a critical temperature. Onnes saw this in 1911 in mercury, whose resistance fell to zero at about 4.2 K.

Superconductivity exists only inside a region bounded by three critical values: temperature Tc, current density Jc and magnetic field Hc. Exceed any one and the material turns normal again.

Why electrons stop scattering: BCS theory

In the superconducting state electrons attract each other weakly through the lattice (an interaction of about 0.1 eV) and form pairs, called Cooper pairs. The second electron follows the first, so the pair meets much less resistance. This was explained by Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer in 1957, and they received the Nobel Prize for it in 1972.

The Meissner effect

Animation: drag the temperature below 7.2 K. A superconductor expels magnetic flux, which is a separate property from having zero resistance.

A normal material lets magnetic field lines pass through. The moment it turns superconducting it pushes the field out, which was first observed in 1933. This is also what lets a magnet levitate above a superconductor: the lecture shows YBCO, cooled below about 90 K, turning diamagnetic and floating a vessel above a magnetic track, and the same principle supports maglev trains with no contact and no friction.

The critical field

The threshold field falls as the temperature rises: HT = H0 [1 − (T/T0)²], where H0 is the critical field at zero kelvin and T0 is the critical temperature. Choose a metal, then set a temperature and applied field.

Low-Tc and high-Tc materials

Early on, materials with transition temperatures above 30 K were called high-Tc. Today the term usually means materials that superconduct at liquid-nitrogen temperature, which is far cheaper to reach than liquid helium. The chart shows how the record rose, with a line at 77 K.

Applications

  • NMR and MRI: superconducting magnets from about 10 T to 25 T cooled by liquid helium; measurement accuracy improves with field strength.
  • Maglev trains: superconducting magnets on the vehicle and coils on the guideway levitate the train.
  • Transformers and generators: windings kept cold to remove I²R losses.
  • Particle accelerators and fusion: the 27 km CERN ring at 1.9 K, and the ITER magnets cooled by helium gas.
Key points
  • Resistance drops to zero below Tc, and current density and field set further limits
  • Cooper pairs explain the zero resistance (BCS theory)
  • The Meissner effect, flux expulsion, is what makes levitation possible
