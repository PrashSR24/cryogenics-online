Lab: Vacuum and leak checking in practice

How to pump down a cryostat, find a leak with a helium detector, and tell a real leak from outgassing by watching the pressure.

A cryostat's insulating vacuum is the first thing to get right. A poor vacuum adds heat load, and a leak can freeze gas onto cold parts, block a fill line or ruin a measurement. The good news is that vacuum problems are diagnosed with a simple routine, and most of it is patience and good habits. This lesson is general laboratory practice, not taken from the lectures.

Four kinds of gas load

  • Real leak. A path from outside into the vacuum: a poor seal, a cracked weld, a pinhole. It adds gas at a constant rate for ever.
  • Virtual leak. A trapped pocket of gas inside, such as a blind tapped hole, a double seal or a gap between two welded plates. It bleeds out slowly and looks like a leak until the pocket has emptied.
  • Outgassing. Gas and water vapour released from the surfaces. It is large at first and falls roughly as 1/time, so it gets better the longer you pump.
  • Permeation. Gas slowly diffusing through elastomer seals and polymers. It is small, but it sets the limit for O-ring-sealed systems.

The unit of a gas load is the throughput, pressure times volume per second, in mbar·l/s. At steady state, the pressure you reach is p = Q/S, where Q is the total gas load and S the pumping speed at the chamber. So a leak of 10-5 mbar·l/s into a 50 l/s pump leaves you at 2×10-7 mbar, and the same leak into a sealed chamber raises the pressure at a rate Q/V.

The pump-down routine

  1. Clean the seal faces and fit the seals. Use as little grease as possible on O-rings, none on indium.
  2. Start the roughing pump and open the valve to the chamber. The pressure falls from atmosphere to about 10-2 mbar.
  3. When the pressure is low enough for the turbomolecular pump, as stated in its manual, start the turbo pump and open to it. Do not start a turbo against atmospheric pressure.
  4. Leave it pumping while you check for leaks. For cryostats a few hours of pumping before cooling is common, and overnight is better.

Find the leak with helium

A helium leak detector is a mass spectrometer tuned to helium (mass 4), connected to the pumping port. Helium is chosen because it is inert, light enough to pass through very small holes, and rare in air, so a trace is easy to see. The routine:

  • Pump until the detector reaches its background level and the pressure is stable.
  • Spray helium on one joint at a time in short, gentle bursts. Helium is lighter than air, so start at the top and work down.
  • Watch the detector's signal. A rise that follows your spray on a particular joint points to the leak, but the signal lags by several seconds, so move slowly. Mark the suspect joint, then check again.
  • Repair, re-pump and re-test. When the total leak rate is below your target, the system is leak-tight.

Try it below. Choose which joint leaks and how large the leak is, then move the gun along the joints, or press Auto-sweep. The signal responds slowly after you pass the leak, which is exactly why real leak hunting needs patience.

Rate-of-rise: real leak or outgassing?

The helium detector tells you where. The rate-of-rise test tells you how much, and which kind of load it is. Pump to a good vacuum, close the valve to the pump, and record the pressure against time. The slope gives the throughput: Q = V × dp/dt. A straight line means a real leak, because it keeps adding gas at a constant rate. A line that bends over and flattens means the load is dying away, so it is outgassing or a virtual leak. Set the leak and the outgassing yourself and see how the curves differ.

How long to pump down?

The pump-down is governed by the volume, the pumping speed and the gas loads. For a chamber of volume V and a pump of speed S, the pressure obeys V dp/dt = −S p + Q, where Q is the sum of outgassing and leaks. The roughing stage falls exponentially, with a time constant V/S. Then outgassing takes over and sets the pace, falling slowly as the surfaces clean up. The calculator uses a roughing pump up to 0.1 mbar and a turbo pump after that, and an outgassing rate that follows 1/time. The outgassing rate for a clean unbaked stainless-steel surface varies widely, from 10-9 to 10-7 mbar·l/s per cm² after an hour of pumping, depending on cleanliness and history, so treat the result as a guide.

Tips from experience

  • Cold leaks. A joint can be tight at room temperature and leak when cold, because metals contract by different amounts. If the vacuum worsens on cooling, find it with the detector while the cryostat is cold, or with a quick helium test as it warms.
  • Seals. A hair, a scratch or a dry O-ring causes most seal leaks. Wipe, inspect, replace. Indium needs clean, flat, parallel faces.
  • Vent screws. A screw in a blind hole is a virtual leak. Use vented screws, or leave a small slot.
  • Gauges lie. An ion gauge reads differently for different gases, and a hot filament outgasses. Be careful about comparing readings of two different gauges.
  • Write it down. Log the pressure and the time of every pump-down. A change from the usual curve warns you before a problem becomes serious.
