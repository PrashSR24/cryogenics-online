Heat transfer and insulation

Every cryogenic machine is a fight against heat. Here are the three ways heat gets in, and how to block each one.

Heat always flows from hot to cold, and a cryogenic system is a very cold object in a very warm room. Between a 300 K laboratory and liquid nitrogen at 77 K there is a temperature difference of 223 K, and between the room and liquid helium it is nearly 296 K. Every watt of heat that leaks in boils away liquid, or has to be pumped out by a refrigerator at a high cost. So the whole art of cryogenic design is to keep heat out. Heat travels in three ways. Watch them below, and use the slider to change the temperature difference.

1. Conduction: heat passing through a solid

In a solid, atoms vibrate. The hotter end vibrates harder, and the vibration is handed on from atom to atom until the energy reaches the cold end. In a metal, free electrons carry heat too, which is why metals conduct so well. The rate of heat flow is given by Fourier's law:

Q = k × A × ΔT / L

Here Q is the heat flow in watts, k is the thermal conductivity of the material, A is the cross-section area, ΔT is the temperature difference and L is the length. To keep heat out you want a small A, a long L and a low k. That is why a cryostat hangs from a thin-walled stainless-steel tube rather than a copper rod: stainless steel conducts roughly 25 times less than copper at room temperature. A glass-fibre composite such as G-10 conducts even less.

2. Convection: heat carried by moving fluid

When a gas or liquid is warmed, it expands and becomes lighter, so it rises; when cooled, it sinks. This sets up a circulation that carries heat from the hot surface to the cold one far faster than conduction alone. The rate is Q = h × A × ΔT, where h is the heat transfer coefficient. The cure is simple: take the fluid away. The space between the inner and outer walls of a cryogenic vessel is pumped to a high vacuum, so there is no gas left to carry heat. Convection also matters inside the liquid itself, and in the gas flowing out of a vent, which we study later.

3. Radiation: heat as light you cannot see

Every surface sends out infrared radiation, and the amount rises steeply with the temperature, as the fourth power of the absolute temperature. For two surfaces, Q = σ × ε × A × (T₁⁴ − T₂⁴), where σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²·K⁴ and ε is the emissivity of the surface, between 0 and 1. Radiation needs no matter at all, so a vacuum does not stop it. Two things reduce it. A bright polished metal has a low emissivity, so it radiates and absorbs little. And shields placed between the warm and cold surfaces absorb the radiation and re-emit part of it: each extra shield cuts the flow roughly in proportion. Many thin, shiny layers together make multilayer insulation (MLI).

How much heat reaches the liquid?

Try your own vessel. The vessel is cooled by liquid nitrogen at 77 K and sits in a 300 K room. Choose the vacuum quality, the radiation shielding and the supporting neck, and read off the heat load and how much liquid it boils away. The numbers are simple estimates (a stainless-steel neck with an average conductivity of about 12 W/m·K between 77 K and 300 K), good enough to compare designs.

Experiment with it. With no vacuum, the gas dominates and the vessel loses liquid in hours. With a good vacuum, radiation takes over; adding shield layers cuts it by about the number of layers plus one. In the end the neck, which you cannot make infinitely long or thin, sets the floor. This three-way balance is the basis of every cryostat and storage tank, and later lessons explore each route in detail.

Key points
  • Heat gets in by conduction, convection and radiation; all three matter
  • Conduction: small area, long length, low conductivity (Q = kAΔT/L)
  • Convection: take the gas away with a vacuum
  • Radiation: low emissivity and many shields (σεA(T₁⁴ − T₂⁴))
  • Heat leak shows up as boil-off: every watt boils off liquid
