Hydrogen and helium

Ortho and para hydrogen, the phase diagram of helium, and the strange behaviour of superfluid He II.

Hydrogen has three isotopes: ordinary hydrogen, deuterium (one proton and one neutron) and tritium, which is very rare and radioactive with a half-life of 12.5 years.

Ortho and para hydrogen

Arrows show the direction of the nuclear spin of each proton in the H2 molecule.

If the two proton spins point the same way the molecule is ortho hydrogen; if they point opposite ways it is para hydrogen. Para is the low-energy form. At 300 K the mix is 75% ortho and 25% para (normal hydrogen), but at 20 K the equilibrium is almost entirely para, 99.8%.

The conversion from ortho to para releases heat and is very slow, so catalysts are used to speed it up. Without conversion during liquefaction, the heat released later in storage would evaporate about 70% of the liquid originally made. That makes complete conversion an essential part of making and storing liquid hydrogen.

Equilibrium composition calculated from the rotational energy levels and nuclear-spin weights of H2 and D2. Hollow circles are the lecture's values at 300 K and 20 K. Deuterium behaves the opposite way: it becomes mostly ortho when cold.

Helium-3 is rare and hard to separate from helium-4; it was first liquefied in 1948. Helium-4 is a monatomic inert gas, first noticed as a spectral line in sunlight in 1868 and liquefied by Onnes in 1908.

The phase diagram of helium-4

Helium has no triple point: it has no pressure and temperature at which solid, liquid and vapour coexist, and the liquid has to be compressed to about 25.3 bar to solidify. When liquid helium is cooled below 2.17 K (the lambda point) a second liquid appears. The boundary is the lambda line, the normal liquid above it is He I, and the superfluid below it is He II.

Superfluid helium: the Rollin film

Animation: a cup of liquid helium lifted out of the bath. As He II it empties itself through a thin creeping film; as He I it stays put.

He II shows zero viscosity when flowing through narrow channels, very high thermal conductivity, and it flows through slits and powders that stop ordinary liquids. It also clings to walls as a film about 30 nanometres thick, which creeps up and over container walls. The flow rate does not depend on the height of the barrier, it is zero at the lambda point and becomes constant below about 1.5 K. A He II container must therefore be designed so the film cannot creep into warm regions and evaporate.

EffectWhat happens
Rollin film (1937)A thin film creeps over walls against gravity, filling or emptying vessels
Thermomechanical effect (1938)Heating liquid inside a container joined by a narrow channel raises its pressure head, because superfluid flows in towards the heat but normal fluid cannot flow out
Mechanocaloric effect (1939)Superfluid flows easily through fine powder, leaving normal fluid behind, so the flask warms up
Fountain effectHeating a powder-filled tube in a He II bath draws superfluid in and squirts it from a capillary at the top

Three kinds of sound

  • First sound: ordinary pressure and density waves, above and below the lambda point.
  • Second sound: below the lambda point the normal and superfluid components can oscillate against each other, giving a temperature wave. Its speed is zero at the lambda point.
  • Third sound: in thin films only the superfluid moves, seen as waves in the film thickness, with a speed of about 0.5 m/s according to the lecture.

The lecture quotes up to 239 m/s for second sound near 0 K. That is close to the speed of ordinary first sound in helium, and references usually give second sound as much slower, so check this value in a standard source before relying on it.

Key points
  • Hydrogen must be converted from ortho to para during liquefaction, or storage losses are huge
  • Helium has no triple point; below 2.17 K liquid helium becomes superfluid He II
  • He II creeps, conducts heat extremely well and flows without viscosity through fine channels
