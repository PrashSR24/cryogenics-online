Heat transfer track

Convection and boiling heat transfer

Dimensionless numbers, the boiling curve and the peak heat flux that decides whether your magnet stays cold.

Three modes, with a convective twist

Heat moves by conduction (Fourier, Q = −kA dT/dx, with the conductivity integral doing the work in cryogenics), convection (Newton, Q = hA ΔT) and radiation (Stefan–Boltzmann). The convection coefficient h is not a property: it depends on the fluid, the speed and the geometry, so engineers correlate it with dimensionless numbers.

Forced convection in a tube

For laminar flow (Re below about 2000) the Nusselt number is constant: 3.66 for a constant wall temperature and 4.36 for a constant wall heat flux. For turbulent flow (Re above 10⁴) the Dittus–Boelter correlation, good to about ±15%, is Nu = 0.023 Re0.8 Prn, with n = 0.4 when the fluid is being heated and 0.3 when cooled, properties evaluated at the film temperature, valid for 0.6 < Pr < 160 and L/D > 10. Sieder–Tate adds a viscosity ratio for strongly varying properties, and Johannes (1972) gives a helium-specific version (within ±8.3%) with a temperature-ratio factor, because helium's properties change a lot between wall and bulk.

Properties are typical handbook values (liquid nitrogen k = 0.145 W/m·K, Pr = 2.3; helium gas at 20 K k = 0.0098 W/m·K, Pr = 0.68), not values from the lecture. The fluid is taken as being heated (n = 0.4).

Free convection

When there is no forced flow, buoyancy drives the motion near a warm wall. The key number is the Rayleigh number, Ra = gβΔT L³ / (ν Dth), where Dth is the thermal diffusivity. For a vertical plate: at very low Ra the result is pure conduction (Nu about 1); for Ra below about 10⁹ the flow is laminar, Nu ≈ 0.59 Ra1/4; above that it is turbulent, Nu ≈ 0.1 Ra1/3. Pool boiling

An object immersed in a large pool of cryogen goes through the same stages as a pot on a stove: convective cooling, nucleate boiling, a peak, and film boiling. Nucleate boiling needs a layer of superheated liquid next to the wall (thickness δ = kΔT/q) and surface imperfections as nucleation sites, which set the size of the smallest bubble that can grow (a balance between surface tension and superheat: the larger the superheat, the smaller the critical bubble). It is the principal mechanism for static liquid below the critical heat flux q*; for liquid helium the heat flux rises with the superheat to the power 2.5, so h is not constant. Theory (Kutateladze) predicts the measured curves for nitrogen and helium well. At the critical heat flux the vapour stream becomes unstable and a vapour film covers the surface; because the vapour conducts so poorly the coefficient drops sharply, and the surface temperature jumps.

The critical (peak) heat flux comes from a balance between surface energy and kinetic energy of the liquid and vapour streams: the Zuber correlation, with the empirical constant 0.16, is

q* ≈ 0.16 hfg ρv1/2 [σ g (ρl − ρv)]1/4

Peak flux is the safety limit for any boiling-cooled magnet or sensor: exceed it and the temperature of the surface can run away, even if the flux is small compared with what the wall could carry in the nucleate regime. In film boiling the Breen–Westwater correlation describes the (low) heat transfer coefficient, which is why recovery needs a flux well below the peak (hysteresis). The curve in the figure is a dimensionless schematic: nucleate q ∝ ΔT2.5, the peak, a transition, and a film regime with a smaller coefficient. The peak heat fluxes it uses come from the Zuber formula and standard property values for each fluid (hydrogen 446 kJ/kg, neon 86, argon 162, oxygen 213, nitrogen 199, helium 20.9; surface tensions from handbook values), so they are estimates for flat plates facing upward, not design data.

Flow boiling in channels

In a heated tube carrying both liquid and vapour the heat transfer depends on mass flow, orientation, flow regime, quality and void fraction, and is the sum of a convective part and a boiling part. For horizontal flow at moderate Reynolds number the enhanced Nusselt number is correlated as Nu2φ/NuL = A χtt−m, with A about 5.4 and m about 0.385 (de la Harpe), where χtt is the Lockhart–Martinelli parameter of the next lesson on two-phase flow. In a vertical channel the peak heat flux depends on the channel width w and length z: it scales with w for narrow channels, with z−1/2 when w/z is below about 0.1, and becomes independent of the channel at large w/z, where it approaches pool-boiling behaviour.
