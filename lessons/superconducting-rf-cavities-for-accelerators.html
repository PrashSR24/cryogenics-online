Module 12 · Science applications

Superconducting RF cavities for accelerators

How a niobium cavity at 2 K loses a hundred-millionth of the power of a copper one, and what that costs in refrigeration.

A refresher on superconductivity

Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908 and found the superconductivity of mercury in 1911. Below a critical temperature Tc the DC resistance drops to zero, and the material expels magnetic fields (the Meissner effect) because screening currents flow in a thin layer, the London penetration depth. The material stays superconducting up to a critical magnetic field Hc(T) = Hc(0)[1 − (T/Tc)²]. Type II superconductors let field in as vortices between a lower critical field Hc1 and an upper critical field Hc2; each vortex carries one flux quantum, and moving vortices dissipate power. For RF fields a perfect surface can stay in the Meissner state up to a superheating field above Hc1, the fundamental limit on the gradient of a cavity.

How a cavity accelerates

A particle of charge q crossing a voltage V gains energy qV; with an accelerating gradient Eacc across a gap of length L the gain is qEaccL. An RF cavity stores electromagnetic energy in a resonant mode, oscillating between the electric and magnetic fields. To give maximum acceleration, a particle must cross a cell in half an RF period, so cell length is βλ/2. In the π-mode of a multi-cell cavity, neighbouring cells have opposite field; as the bunch moves one cell, the field has reversed, so it always sees an accelerating field. Why superconducting?

Power is dissipated by surface currents driven by the magnetic field: P = ½ Rs ∮ H² dS. The quality factor Q₀ = G/Rs is 2π times the stored energy over the energy lost per cycle; G is a geometry factor and the typical Q₀ is about 10⁴ for a copper cavity and about 10¹⁰ for a niobium one at 2 K. The cavity voltage and power are linked by the shunt impedance R/Q (set by the shape alone): Pc = Vc² / ((R/Q) Q₀). So power falls in proportion to Rs, and shape optimisation tries to maximise G·(R/Q).

The surface resistance of a superconductor is not zero in an RF field. It has a temperature-dependent part from thermally excited quasiparticles (BCS theory) and a residual part from impurities, trapped flux and hydrides. For niobium below about Tc/2 an approximate formula is Rs ≈ 2×10⁻⁴ (f/1500)² (1/T) exp(−17.67/T) + Rres, in ohm with f in MHz, and Rres is typically 1 to 10 nΩ. Only about 100 nm of the surface matters, which is why final surface preparation, electropolishing, baking and nitrogen doping decide the performance. Assumptions: f = 1300 MHz, G = 270 Ω and R/Q = 1036 Ω (typical values for a nine-cell TESLA cavity, quoted from standard references, not the lectures), length 1.038 m; cryoplant efficiency 25% of Carnot, similar to the 4×10⁻³ coefficient of performance quoted for 4.5 K in the lectures.

Niobium has the highest critical temperature of any element, high Hc1 and Hc, a theoretical Meissner breakdown field near 240 mT, low residual resistance, and is easy to form. Nb₃Sn reaches Q₀ above 10¹⁰ at 4.2 K but has so far been limited to about 20 MV/m; alloys like NbTi are too lossy for cavities, and high-temperature superconductors show very high surface resistance. For high Q at 2 K or the highest gradient (above 45 MV/m) nothing yet beats niobium.

Making and cooling a cavity

Cavities are made from niobium sheets several millimetres thick, deep-drawn or spun into half-cells, electron-beam welded into cells and then into a full structure, then cleaned and etched; at least 150 µm is removed by electropolishing for state-of-the-art performance. A recipe such as a 120 °C bake, nitrogen doping or low-temperature baking tunes the surface, and the performance test is a measurement of Q against Eacc, which shows three regions: a low-field slope, a medium-field slope and a high-field slope. The finished cavity is welded into a titanium or stainless-steel helium vessel. Elliptical cells serve particles with β near 1; quarter-wave, half-wave and spoke resonators serve slower ones. The helium-filled cavities sit inside a cryomodule: a cryostat with thermal shield, MLI, magnetic shield, power coupler, tuner and higher-order-mode dampers. Long linacs such as the European XFEL and LCLS-II use a continuous string of cryomodules with an internal cryogenic transfer line; others use separate modules fed from outside.

The cost of cold: normal against superconducting

Copper cavity losses grow as the square of the voltage, so for continuous-wave operation at high gradient a superconducting cavity wins even though cold power is expensive. The lectures compare two 500 MHz RF systems for a storage ring that must give 300 kW to the beam:

Normal-conductingSuperconducting
Cavity wall power278 kW65 W dynamic + 30 W static
Total RF power needed578 kW300 kW
Cryoplant COP at 4.5 Knone4×10⁻³
Total wall-plug (AC) power1,156 kW624 kW

The superconducting system needs fewer cavities and less power; its larger beam aperture also lowers the beam impedance, which reduces disruption of the beam, and its design is free from the pressure to minimise wall losses.
