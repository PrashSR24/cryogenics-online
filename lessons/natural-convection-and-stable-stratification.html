Natural convection and stable stratification

When a fluid heated from below starts to churn, how much extra heat that carries, and why warm gas on top of cold gas keeps a cryostat neck quiet.

In natural convection nobody pumps the fluid: warm fluid is lighter and rises, cold fluid is heavier and sinks, and the buoyancy drives a circulation. Whether it starts, and how much heat it moves, is decided by one number. This lesson is general fluid and heat transfer physics, not taken from the lectures; the correlation is the standard one for a horizontal layer and is accurate only to about 10 to 20 per cent. (The earlier lesson on convection and boiling gives the vertical-plate case.)

The Rayleigh number

Consider a horizontal layer of fluid of height H between a warm plate below and a cold plate above. Buoyancy tries to start a circulation; viscosity and heat diffusion try to stop it. The ratio is the Rayleigh number:

Ra = g β ΔT H³ / (ν α)

Here g is the acceleration of gravity, β the thermal expansion coefficient of the fluid (the fractional change of density per kelvin), ΔT the temperature difference across the layer, ν the kinematic viscosity and α the thermal diffusivity. Below a critical value, about Ra = 1708 between two rigid plates, the fluid stays still and the heat just conducts across. Above it, the layer breaks into rolls of rising and falling fluid, and the heat flow rises above the conduction value.

The Nusselt number

The Nusselt number Nu is the actual heat flux divided by what conduction alone would carry, k ΔT / H. So Nu = 1 means no convection at all, and Nu = 10 means convection carries ten times as much heat as conduction. The effective heat transfer coefficient is h = Nu × k / H. A widely used correlation for a horizontal layer heated from below (Hollands) is

Nu = 1 + 1.44 [1 − 1708/Ra]⁺ + [(Ra/5830)^(1/3) − 1]⁺

where [x]⁺ means x if positive and zero otherwise. For large Ra the last term dominates and Nu grows as Ra^(1/3), so that, in the turbulent regime, the heat flux does not depend on the layer height. Watch the rolls form in the animation, and use the controls to choose the fluid, the height and the temperature difference.

Try the settings. In liquid nitrogen, a 2 cm layer with only 1 K across it is already well above the critical value, while a layer of a millimetre needs only about half a kelvin. Air at room temperature in a 2 mm gap does not convect at all, for any temperature difference you will meet in a cryostat. Then switch the heating to above: whatever the numbers, the layer is stable, and the heat moves by conduction alone.

Stable stratification: why necks are quiet

If the warm fluid sits above the cold fluid, the density decreases upward and nothing wants to move. This stable stratification is the normal state of a gas column in the neck of a dewar: warm at the top, cold at the bottom. The gas conducts and the neck wall conducts, but the gas does not overturn. It is one reason helium storage dewars are built with long, narrow necks. The same idea acts in a liquid tank: heat entering through the side wall warms the liquid next to it, the warm liquid rises along the wall and forms a warm layer at the top, and the bulk below stays cold. The surface layer is warmer than the bulk, so the vapour pressure at the surface, and hence the tank pressure, can rise faster than the boil-off of the whole liquid would suggest. (The next lessons look at thermoacoustic oscillations, which are the one thing that can set a stable gas column in motion.)

Key points
  • Ra = gβΔT H³/(να) decides whether buoyancy starts a flow; the layer between rigid plates is stable below Ra ≈ 1708
  • Nu is the heat flow relative to pure conduction; h = Nu k / H
  • In turbulent natural convection Nu grows as Ra^(1/3) and the heat flux no longer depends on the layer height
  • Warm fluid above cold fluid is stable: gas in a dewar neck conducts but does not overturn
