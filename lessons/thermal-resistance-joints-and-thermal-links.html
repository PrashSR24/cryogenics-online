Thermal resistance, joints and thermal links

How to treat a heat path as a chain of resistances, why a bolted joint can cost more temperature than a metre of copper, and how to build a good cold link.

A real cold stage is joined to its load by a chain of parts: a sample holder, a clamp, a copper strap, another clamp, a cold plate. The heat has to cross each one in turn. This lesson is general engineering, not taken from the lectures. The contact figures are illustrative: real joint conductance varies by orders of magnitude with surface finish, force, plating and temperature, so measure the joints that matter.

Thermal resistance

Fourier's law can be written like Ohm's law. For a bar of constant conductivity the thermal resistance is R = L / (k A), in kelvin per watt, and Q = ΔT / R. The temperature difference plays the role of voltage, the heat flow that of current. Resistances in series (the heat passes through each in turn) add: R = R₁ + R₂ + …. Resistances in parallel (the heat has several paths) combine as 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …, so the easiest path carries most of the heat. When k varies with temperature, the resistance depends on the temperatures, and you use the conductivity integral from the previous lesson for each part.

Joints: where the heat has to squeeze through

Two solid surfaces pressed together do not touch over their whole area. At the microscopic scale, each surface is rough, and contact occurs only at the tips of the high points, the asperities. The heat must squeeze through these small contact spots, so the joint has a resistance of its own, an extra temperature drop at the interface. Pressing harder flattens the asperities and enlarges the spots, so the conductance of the joint rises with the force. Adding a soft layer that fills the gaps (a thin foil of indium, or a thin film of vacuum grease) makes the contact far better at a given force. Gold plating prevents oxide layers on copper, which otherwise act as barriers. In a metal joint at low temperature, the heat is carried by the electrons, so the conductance of the joint falls roughly in proportion to the temperature, which is why joints become the weak point at the coldest stage.

The model in the animation uses these trends in an illustrative form: the conductance of a joint is G = G0 × (F / 100 N)0.8 × (T / 4 K) × (surface factor), with G0 = 0.05 W/K for a dry bare joint at 100 N and 4 K. The surface factor is 1 for a dry joint, 5 for a greased joint and 20 for an indium foil joint. These numbers show the trends; they are not measurements.

Experiment. At 4 K with a dry joint and a light force, the two joints take most of the temperature rise, far more than the copper strap. Add grease or indium, or raise the force, and the joints shrink; then the strap dominates, and making it shorter or thicker helps. Raise the plate to 77 K: the joint conductance is larger there, so everything is easier. The lesson is to spend your effort on the weakest link of the chain, and it is very often a joint.

Good habits for cold links

  • Use high-purity, annealed copper (high RRR) for the strap, with good gold-plated, flat contact faces.
  • Use a bolt, a spring or a Belleville washer to keep the force steady as the parts contract in the cold.
  • Use indium foil or a thin film of grease for joints that must carry the most heat.
  • Fewer joints are better than many good joints; each one adds resistance.
  • Do not forget that the thermometer sits at the end of its own thermal path: its reading tells you the temperature at the sensor, not of the sample.
Key points
  • Series resistances add; parallel paths combine like conductances
  • A joint has its own resistance, because heat passes through small contact spots
  • Joint conductance rises with force, with a soft interface layer, and with temperature
  • At 4 K the joints, not the copper, usually limit a cold link
