Module 5 · Gas separation

Gas separation and ideal work

Why unmixing gases costs work, and how much, for air and for any binary mixture.

Why separate gases at all

Air, roughly 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 1% argon with traces of helium, neon, krypton and xenon, is the raw material for most industrial gases. Liquid nitrogen precools most cryogenic systems, argon is used in welding and chemistry, and liquid oxygen and hydrogen fuel rockets. Ammonia plants must also separate nitrogen, argon and other inert purge gases at low temperature. Gas separation is the process of splitting a mixture into its components and purifying them.

Four common methods

MethodPrincipleExample from the lecture
Synthetic membraneA porous medium passes only certain moleculesA thin palladium sheet passes hydrogen
AdsorptionCertain molecules stick to a surfaceFinely divided nickel adsorbs hydrogen
AbsorptionA substance is taken into another phase, often chemicallySodium hydroxide solution absorbs CO2 from a stream
DistillationComponents differ in volatility (boiling point)Cryogenic distillation of liquid air gives O2, N2, Ar, Ne, Kr and Xe

For air, one can liquefy first and separate the liquid (cryogenic separation) or separate the gas at room temperature. The lecture lists the advantages of the cryogenic route: the separation work is lower at lower temperature (see below), the boiling points of the components are further apart in the relevant sense, large quantities can be handled, and very high purity is possible.

Mixing is irreversible

Remove the wall between two gases and they mix on their own. Putting the wall back does not unmix them. Unmixing needs work, so the ideal separator is a thought experiment: a chamber with two frictionless opposing pistons made of semi-permeable membranes, one passing only gas A and the other only gas B. Pushing them reversibly and isothermally at the mixture temperature Tm separates the gases. The work for a mixture of N constituents with mole fractions yj is

Wi/nm = ℜ Tm Σ yj ln(1/yj)

per mole of mixture, with ℜ = 8.314 J/mol·K. Dividing by a constituent's mole fraction gives the work per mole of that constituent, and dividing by its molar mass gives work per unit mass.

Composition first

The formulas use mole fractions, so percentages must be converted. For a 60% A, 40% B mixture by weight with molar masses 28 and 32 g/mol, the lecture finds yA = 0.631 and yB = 0.369. By volume, an ideal gas mixture has volume fraction equal to mole fraction, so yA = 0.6 and yB = 0.4.

Try it

Ideal work per mole of mixture against yA

The lecture's nitrogen–oxygen case (80% N2, 20% O2, 300 K) gives 1248.1 J per mole of mixture, 1560.1 J per mole of N2 (55.71 J/g) and 6240.5 J per mole of O2 (195.01 J/g). The work per mole of mixture is always smaller than the work per mole of any one constituent, because the mixture contains all of them.

Three features follow from the formula. The work is proportional to Tm, so a cooler separation costs less. For two gases, the work per mole of mixture is greatest for an equal mixture, where it equals ℜT ln 2. And for three gases the maximum comes at y = 1/3 each.

Air as a three-gas mixture

With 78% N2, 21% O2 and 1% Ar at 300 K, the lecture gets 1415.6 J per mole of air. Spread over each constituent that becomes:

ConstituentMole fractionWork per mole of that gas (J/mol)
Oxygen0.216740.9
Argon0.01141,560

Argon costs about twenty times more per mole than oxygen simply because it is so scarce: the whole separation effort is divided among very few argon molecules. The lecture also separates the cases where all three gases are isolated and where only one gas is pulled out, leaving the other two mixed.
