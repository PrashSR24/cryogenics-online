Module 5 · Gas separation

Mixtures, phase diagrams and K-values

The Gibbs phase rule, dew and bubble lines, Raoult's law and how to read a vapour–liquid equilibrium.

How many properties fix the state

The Gibbs phase rule, formulated by J. Willard Gibbs in 1878, gives the number of independent intensive properties F needed to fix a mixture in equilibrium, from the number of components C and phases P:

F = C − P + 2

Single-phase nitrogen has C = 1, P = 1 and so F = 2: pressure and temperature fix everything else. Two-phase nitrogen has F = 1, so the saturation pressure is set by the temperature alone. A single-phase two-gas mixture has F = 3 (pressure, temperature and composition). A boiling mixture of liquid air is C = 2, P = 2 and so F = 2: at a given pressure, temperature and composition are tied together.

The temperature–composition diagram

At constant pressure, plot temperature against the mole fraction of one component. Two curves appear. The upper one is the dew line, where vapour starts to condense. The lower is the bubble line, where liquid starts to boil. Between them lies a two-phase region. For nitrogen and oxygen at 1 atm, above about 90 K the mixture is all vapour, and below about 77 K it is all liquid.

Cool a vapour of composition 1 down to the dew line and the first drop forms. Its composition is read along the horizontal tie line to the bubble line, and it is richer in oxygen, the higher-boiling component. Cool further and more liquid forms, while the remaining vapour grows richer in nitrogen. Condensation or boiling of a mixture is therefore not isothermal. Repeating this enrichment is the idea behind rectification. The further apart the boiling points, the more complete the split in one step. Mixtures that behave like a single substance at some composition, called azeotropes, cannot be separated this way, though cryogens rarely show them.

Three laws

  • Dalton's law (1801): in a mixture of non-reacting ideal gases, the partial pressure is pj = ptot yj, and the partial pressures sum to the total.
  • Raoult's law (1882): over an ideal liquid mixture, the partial pressure of component j is pj = πj xj, where πj is its pure-component vapour pressure and xj its liquid mole fraction.
  • Gibbs–Dalton law: apply Dalton's law to the vapour above the liquid, so pj = yj ptot.

Combine the last two and the distribution coefficient appears:

Kj = yj / xj = πj / ptot

It is defined only where two phases coexist. K is above 1 for the more volatile component (nitrogen here) and below 1 for the less volatile one, and it approaches 1 at that component's own boiling point. For a two-component mixture, requiring both y's and both x's to sum to 1 gives the liquid composition directly:

xA = (1 − KB) / (KA − KB), yA = KA xA

For real mixtures K is tabulated from experiment as ln(K pmix/p0) with p0 = 1 atm. Worked case from the lecture: nitrogen–oxygen at 5 atm and 100 K. The table gives 2.004 for nitrogen and 1.042 for oxygen, so KN2 = e2.004/5 = 1.483 and KO2 = e1.042/5 = 0.567. Then xN2 = (1 − 0.567)/(1.483 − 0.567) = 0.472, and the vapour holds yN2 = 1.483 × 0.472 = 0.70.
