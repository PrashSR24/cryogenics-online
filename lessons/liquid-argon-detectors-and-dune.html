Module 12 · Science applications

Liquid argon detectors and DUNE

Hunting neutrinos and dark matter with thousands of tonnes of argon purified to parts per trillion.

Why liquid argon?

Argon is 0.93% of the atmosphere by volume (1.2% by mass), so it is abundant and cheap: the lectures quote about $1.15 per kg for liquid argon, against about $3,393 per kg for xenon gas, roughly three thousand times more. It boils at 87.3 K at 1 atm, so inexpensive LN₂ (77.4 K) can keep it liquid. It is an excellent scintillator that is transparent to its own light, and ionisation electrons can drift metres through it under an electric field with millimetre-scale spreading. And mass matters, because the targets are rare events: neutrino interactions, dark-matter collisions and double-beta decay.

Liquid argon packs matter densely. A saturated liquid at 87.6 K has a density of 1,393 kg/m³, against 5.965 kg/m³ for the saturated gas and 1.723 kg/m³ for gas at 293 K. Try the volumes:

How a liquid-argon time projection chamber works

A charged particle crossing the liquid leaves a trail of ionised atoms and excited atoms. The ionisation electrons drift in an electric field to wire planes at the anode; because the arrival times and wire positions give the track in three dimensions, the detector is a time projection chamber (TPC), a concept proposed by Rubbia in 1977. The scintillation light is flashed at the moment of the interaction and seen by photon detectors, which give the time stamp. The trouble is that electrons drift for about a millisecond. In that time an electron makes about 10¹³ collisions per second, so any electronegative impurity (oxygen, water) will capture it; the resulting negative ion moves so slowly that its signal is lost in noise. Light is spoiled differently: nitrogen quenches the scintillation, and methane or CO₂ absorbs it.

The purity budget: parts per trillion

The charge that survives a drift of time t is Q = Q₀ e−t/τ, where τ is the electron lifetime. For electric fields below a few hundred V/cm the lectures give τ(µs) ≈ 300 / [O₂ in ppb], so 1 ppb of oxygen means a 0.3 ms lifetime, and a 3 ms lifetime needs just 0.1 ppb, i.e. 100 parts per trillion. What was achieved

  • The ICARUS detector at Gran Sasso (two 600-tonne modules) reached lifetimes above 12 ms.
  • ProtoDUNE-SP at CERN (740 tonnes) consistently exceeded 30 ms, about 10 ppt of oxygen-equivalent. Pump stoppages degraded purity every time, and it recovered when circulation resumed.
  • The DUNE requirement is under 100 ppt, about a 3 ms lifetime.

Keeping it pure

  • Start with good argon. Purchased liquid argon contains about 1 ppm of oxygen, nitrogen and water (specifications allow 5 ppm O₂ and 10 ppm N₂ and water), often ten times better. Nitrogen is left alone; water and oxygen are removed.
  • Remove water with 4A molecular sieve, about 22 g of water per kg of sieve (bulk density 705 kg/m³); regenerate above 450 K but below 500 K.
  • Remove oxygen with activated copper pellets, 0.5 to 0.7 g of O₂ per kg (about 200 m²/g surface). The copper oxidises (2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO) and is regenerated at 450 to 500 K in 1 to 2% hydrogen in argon (CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, ΔH = −84.5 kJ/mol). Too much heat from that reaction is a risk, and regeneration temperature profiles in practice must be watched.
  • Circulate the liquid at first about one volume per ten days, then slow down once the argon is pure. Never stop circulating the gas: contaminants outgas into the warm argon vapour at the top and dissolve straight back into the liquid, so the gas is recondensed outside the cryostat and purified, not vented.

Size a purifier with the lecture's method: oxygen mass equals the argon gas volume at STP times the impurity fraction times oxygen's density, and media mass equals oxygen mass divided by the per-kilogram capacity.

Materials, cooldown and watching it happen

  • Materials. In the cold liquid almost any material that survives the temperature is acceptable, because the vapour pressure of everything is tiny at 87 K; what matters is floating (PTFE at 2,200 kg/m³ sinks, PEEK at 1,260 kg/m³ floats), peeling and cracking from contraction (metals shrink about 2.5 to 4 mm/m, polymers 25 to 30 mm/m). In the warm gas, avoid materials with high water content such as G-10 and nylon. The Fermilab Materials Test Stand measured the outgassing and lifetime effect of over seventy components.
  • Temperature uniformity. The electron drift speed changes by about −2% per kelvin, so the argon must stay within about 0.1 K of uniform. Hydrostatic head matters: 20 cm of argon adds about 26 mbar, enough to superheat the liquid by about 230 mK, so a thermometer coming out of the liquid reads higher. ProtoDUNE kept the temperature gradient to a few millikelvin when circulation was on.
  • Cooldown no faster than 50 K/hour and under 50 K between any two points, because the detector mixes stainless steel, G-10 and fine copper-beryllium wires (150 µm, held at 5 N against 25.6 N breaking tension) with different contraction. Liquid is sprayed in at the top; computational fluid dynamics predicted ProtoDUNE's cooldown closely.
  • Cameras inside the liquid, in evacuated polycarbonate or stainless housings with heated windows and LED lighting, verify alignment during cooldown and fill and check for bubbling near ground planes.

DUNE: the largest argon plant

The Long-Baseline Neutrino Facility will send a beam from Fermilab to the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment, a mile underground at the Sanford Underground Research Facility in South Dakota. The far detector is four modules of 17,450 tonnes of liquid argon each (the project builds two first). The lectures give these numbers per cryostat:

For scale: 87.1 kW divided by argon's latent heat of about 161 kJ/kg is roughly 0.54 kg/s of boil-off, about 47 tonnes a day or 0.27% of the inventory a day, all of which must be recondensed and purified. The design deliberately has no cryogens in the shaft: argon is vaporised and moved as gas, then recondensed underground. The cryostat pressure is protected by an inline safety valve whose seat sits inside the cryostat, below the liquid level.
