Module 13 · Sub-kelvin cryogenics

Below 1 kelvin and pumped helium refrigerators

Why one kelvin is the line between industrial and laboratory cryogenics, and how a pump on a pot of helium gets you to 0.3 K.

Why one kelvin is a boundary

Helium-4 has the lowest boiling point of the common cryogens, 4.21 K at one atmosphere, and the only practical way to go colder with a liquid is to lower its vapour pressure. The large superfluid plants met earlier run at about 10 mbar, roughly 2 K, and can pump hard enough to lift around 100 W. A laboratory cryostat with its own pump reaches about 1 K, but the pump is small, so the cooling power is only about 10 mW. One kelvin is a soft boundary, but below it you are no longer doing industrial-scale cryogenics: you are doing small, delicate laboratory work where every microwatt matters.

RangeNameHow you get there
0.1 K to 1 KSub-kelvinPumped helium-4 or helium-3 cryostats, magnetic cooling
1 mK to 100 mKMillikelvinDilution refrigerators, magnetic cooling
1 µK to 1 mKMicrokelvinNuclear magnetic cooling
Below 1 µKNanokelvinAtom traps and laser cooling (these cool the atoms themselves, not a physical object)

A short history

  • 1908: helium is first liquefied, but sub-kelvin temperatures had to wait for pumps. In 1922 Kamerlingh Onnes reached 830 mK with an enormous battery of pumps.
  • 1926 to 1933: Debye and Giauque propose magnetic cooling; it is demonstrated in 1933 at 530 mK, then 270 mK.
  • 1951 to 1966: London proposes the dilution refrigerator; it first works in 1965 to 1966.
  • 1972: nuclear demagnetisation goes below 1 mK; by the late 1990s equilibrium temperatures of 1 µK are routine.
  • 1995: trap cooling below 1 µK reveals the Bose–Einstein condensate.

Why bother? Cold means quiet. Sensors for faint far-infrared and millimetre light, dark-matter crystals, and superconducting quantum bits all need thermal noise pushed far below anything a 4 K machine can offer.

Helium-3 keeps a higher vapour pressure than helium-4 over the whole range, because it does not turn superfluid until a couple of millikelvin. That is exactly why it can reach lower temperatures when you pump on it: about 0.3 K, against about 1 K for helium-4. The price is steep: helium-4 costs roughly $1 per standard litre of gas, helium-3 about $1000 per standard litre from the US Department of Energy isotope programme and up to $3000 on the open market, so helium-3 machines are always sealed and recirculating.

The pumped helium-4 pot

The simplest design connects a bath of liquid helium-4 to a pump and lets the liquid cool as it boils. It works, but about half of the liquid evaporates during the pump-down from 4 K to 1 K, and a big pump is needed. The better design uses a small pot fed from an atmospheric helium bath through a narrow flow impedance. The pump only has to handle the pot, and the cooling power is the balance between the heat that reaches the pot (mainly down the pumping tube) and the experiment's own load. The pot is also self-regulating: if the load rises, the liquid level falls, and less heat creeps in down the wetted tube.

The evaporation rate follows from the latent heat: V̇ = Q̇ / (ρ L). For 5 mW at 1.5 K the lecture finds about 5.5 cm³ of liquid per hour, or about 5 standard litres of gas per hour; this calculator uses L = 22.5 J/g and ρ = 0.145 g/cm³, which are the values implied by that example. Note how small the gas flow is. That is why a modest pump will do: at 1.5 K the vapour pressure is only about 5 mbar, easy for a mechanical pump.

Choosing the impedance

The impedance Z is the pressure drop divided by the volume flow rate and the viscosity. If Z is too large the pot runs dry; if it is too small, too much warm liquid arrives and the pot never gets cold. A long capillary does the job, and for laminar flow in a round tube Z = 8 l / (π R⁴). Because of the fourth power, small errors in radius matter enormously, and measured values typically agree with the formula only to within a factor of two.

The lecture quotes a typical impedance of about 10¹¹ cm⁻³, equivalent to a few metres of capillary. Taking 0.1 mm as the radius reproduces that. Double the radius and Z drops sixteen-fold.

Pumps that live in the cold: sorption coolers

You do not need a room-temperature pump. A cryogenic getter pump, usually activated charcoal, adsorbs helium at low temperature and releases it when warmed. Heat the charcoal to 20 to 40 K and it drives the gas off, which condenses in the cooler; cool it below about 10 K and it pumps the pot. At the pressure of a 1 K refrigerator (about 0.1 torr) one gram of charcoal at 4 K holds about 5 litres of helium gas. The example calculator above uses that figure to tell you how much charcoal your pot needs.

  • Pros: compact and self-contained, mechanically simple, and no heat from a warm pump line, so the base temperature can be lower.
  • Cons: usually one-shot (a continuous sorption cooler needs several pumps and valves and becomes very complicated), and low pumping speed limits the cooling power.
  • Where they shine: places where nobody can top up a pump, such as stratospheric balloons, sounding rockets and space telescopes. Multi-stage versions put one or more helium-3 stages after a helium-4 stage.

Helium-3 refrigerators

A helium-3 refrigerator has the same idea, one temperature lower. Since helium-3 is too precious to vent, the gas must be condensed from a pumped helium-4 pot at 1 to 2 K. Three layouts are common:

  • Single-shot: a pumped helium-4 bath plus a one-shot helium-3 pot. Simple, but wasteful of helium-4 and not continuous.
  • Atmospheric bath plus pumped pot: better use of helium-4, but the helium-3 stage is still discontinuous.
  • Fully recirculating: a helium-3 pot that is continuously condensed and pumped. Continuous cooling, at the cost of a complicated condenser and a warm pump system.

Typical performance for a helium-4 and helium-3 pair in a pulse-tube-cooled (cryogen-free) machine is about 10 mW at 1.5 K and about 1 mW at 0.4 K. Each of these fridges shows an overload state (the liquid is being used up and the pot runs for a while above its rating) and a stationary state (when the liquid has gone the pot can still hold a little load, but at a higher temperature). Below roughly 0.3 K, a different physical process is needed, and the next lessons describe it.
