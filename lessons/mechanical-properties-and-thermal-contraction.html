Module 3 · Materials

Mechanical properties and thermal contraction

Why some metals stay tough in the cold, and why parts shrink by different amounts.

Why properties change

The lecture opens with two examples. Rubber dipped in liquid nitrogen turns hard and shatters like a brittle solid, while Nb–Ti wire loses all electrical resistance in liquid helium. Design therefore needs strength, ductility, thermal and electrical data at the working temperature, not just at room temperature.

Crystal structure decides ductility

LatticeSlip planesBehaviour when coldTypical examples
Face-centred cubic (FCC)MostStays ductileCopper, aluminium, nickel, austenitic stainless steel
Hexagonal close-packed (HCP)In betweenIntermediateTitanium, magnesium, zinc
Body-centred cubic (BCC)FewestProne to brittle failureCarbon steel, ferritic steels

The slip-plane ranking comes from the lecture. The examples are standard ones added for illustration.

Slip planes are the directions in which layers of atoms slide over one another. Real crystals contain dislocations, and the number of slip planes governs how easily dislocations move, and with it ductility and impact strength.

Strength rises, toughness can collapse

PropertyAs temperature fallsWhy
Yield strengthRisesAtoms vibrate less, so dislocations are harder to move
Ultimate strengthRisesThe same reason
Fatigue strengthRisesA micro-crack needs a larger stress to grow
Impact strengthGenerally falls; some materials show a sharp ductile-to-brittle transitionLittle energy is absorbed before fracture

Carbon steels undergo this ductile-to-brittle transition at roughly 80 to 100 K, so they cannot be used for cryogenic service. In the lecture's impact data, 304 stainless steel, 9% nickel steel and 2024-T4 aluminium keep their toughness. For parts under cyclic load, such as the flexure bearings made of beryllium–copper, the working stress is kept below the endurance limit.

Illustrative curves built from the trends described in the lecture, not measured data. The shaded band marks the 80 to 100 K range where carbon steels change from tough to brittle.

Thermal contraction

The linear coefficient of thermal expansion is λt = (δL/L)/δT, and for an isotropic material the volumetric coefficient is β = 3λt. Materials contract on cooling, and the coefficient itself shrinks as temperature falls, so most of the total contraction has already happened by 80 K. For 304 stainless steel the lecture gives 2.91 mm per metre from 300 K to 80 K, and only 0.13 mm per metre from 80 K to 20 K. Copper contracts 3.11 mm per metre between 300 K and 80 K.

Key points
  • Slip planes: FCC has the most and stays ductile, BCC the fewest and turns brittle
  • Strength rises as temperature falls, but impact strength can fall sharply
  • Dissimilar metals contract by different amounts, so joints and long runs need allowance
