Counterflow heat exchangers in motion

Watch two streams exchange heat along an exchanger, see why counterflow beats parallel flow, and what each lost percent of effectiveness costs.

The heat exchanger is the workhorse of cryogenic plants: it lets the cold gas leaving the cold end cool the warm gas entering. The earlier lesson on heat exchanger effectiveness gave the definitions and the lecture's results; here you see the temperature profiles in motion and compare the arrangements. This lesson is general heat transfer theory, not taken from the lectures, and uses constant specific heats.

Two streams, one wall

In a counterflow exchanger the two streams run in opposite directions. In a parallel-flow exchanger they run the same way. Each stream has a capacity rate C = ṁ cp (mass flow times specific heat), in W/K. A stream with a small C changes temperature a lot for a given heat; one with a large C changes little. Call the smaller capacity rate Cmin, and the ratio Cr = Cmin/Cmax. The size of the exchanger enters as the number of transfer units, NTU = U A / Cmin, where U is the overall heat transfer coefficient and A the area. The effectiveness ε is the actual heat exchanged divided by the most that could be exchanged, Cmin × (Thot,in − Tcold,in).

  • Counterflow: ε = [1 − exp(−NTU(1 − Cr))] / [1 − Cr exp(−NTU(1 − Cr))], and ε = NTU / (1 + NTU) for Cr = 1.
  • Parallel flow: ε = [1 − exp(−NTU(1 + Cr))] / (1 + Cr).

In the animation the warm stream enters on the left and the cold stream, which in a liquefier is the returning low-pressure gas, enters at the other end (counterflow) or the same end (parallel flow). Dots carry the streams, coloured by their local temperature. The graph below shows both temperatures along the exchanger.

Things to try:

  • Set Cr = 1 and counterflow: the two temperature lines are parallel, the temperature difference is the same everywhere, and ε = NTU/(1+NTU). To reach ε = 0.95 you need NTU = 19.
  • Switch to parallel flow with Cr = 1: the outlet temperatures can never cross, so ε can not exceed 0.5, however large the exchanger. That is why cryogenic plants use counterflow.
  • Reduce Cr below 1 in counterflow, as happens in a liquefier where part of the flow leaves as liquid and the return stream is smaller: the exchanger can then do better at the same NTU.

What lost effectiveness costs

The heat that the exchanger fails to recover, (1 − ε) Cmin (Thot,in − Tcold,in), arrives at the cold end as an extra load that the refrigerator or the liquefier must remove. For a flow of 1 g/s of nitrogen gas (C ≈ 1.04 W/K per g/s), a drop in effectiveness from 0.98 to 0.95 between 300 K and 80 K adds about 7 W at the cold end for every gram per second. This is why cryogenic exchangers are built with very high effectiveness, and why the Linde–Hampson cycle needs a very good one.

Key points
  • ε and NTU describe an exchanger; ε depends on NTU, Cr and the arrangement
  • Counterflow can approach ε = 1; parallel flow cannot go beyond about 0.5 when Cr = 1
  • Unrecovered heat, (1 − ε) Cmin ΔT, lands on the cold end as extra load
  • High NTU means large area, which means more cost and pressure drop: a design trade-off
