Heat transfer track

Gas conduction and thermal radiation

Why a good vacuum is enough, why radiation depends on the warm wall, and how floating shields cut it by n + 1.

Conduction through a gas

A gas conducts heat in one of two regimes, set by how the molecules' mean free path λ compares with the gap L between the surfaces. The mean free path in SI units (M in kg/mol) is λ = 3.62 (μ/P) √(T/M), where μ is the viscosity, P the pressure and T the mean temperature.

  • Viscous (continuum) regime, λ ≪ L. Molecules collide with each other many times crossing the gap. The gas obeys Fourier's law with a conductivity k(T) that does not depend on pressure.
  • Molecular (Knudsen) regime, λ ≫ L. Molecules fly wall to wall. Heat is carried by individual molecules in Kennard's law: Q/A = α (γ+1)/(γ−1) √(R/(8πMT)) P (Tw − Tc), with α the accommodation coefficient (between 0 and 1), γ = Cp/Cv and T the mean temperature. The heat flow is linear in pressure, which is why a better vacuum helps, and why thermal-conductivity (Pirani) gauges can read pressure.

Viscosity and conductivity here use simple power-law fits (nitrogen μ = 1.78×10⁻⁵ (T/300)^0.7 Pa·s, k = 0.026 (T/300)^0.8 W/m·K; helium μ = 1.99×10⁻⁵ (T/300)^0.68, k = 0.152 (T/300)^0.7), which are handbook-style approximations and not from the lecture. The regimes are blended as two resistances in series; this is an engineering approximation, not a rigorous transition model. With nitrogen between 290 K and 80 K at 10⁻⁴ torr the Kennard heat flux is about 4 W/m², comparable with a layer of poor insulation, and at 10⁻⁶ torr it falls a hundredfold.

Thermal radiation

Every surface above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation. A black body at temperature T radiates σT⁴ (σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴), peaking at a wavelength λmax = 2898/T µm (Wien): about 10 µm for a 300 K wall, 36 µm at 80 K, 0.5 µm for the Sun. Real surfaces emit a fraction ε < 1 (the emissivity); an opaque surface reflects what it does not absorb (ρ + α = 1). Between two surfaces the exchange is Q = σ A₁ ε₁₂ (T₁⁴ − T₂⁴), where the effective emissivity ε₁₂ for closely spaced parallel plates is 1/(1/ε₁ + 1/ε₂ − 1), and for a small body inside a large enclosure it is set essentially by the small body's emissivity.

Two things follow for cryogenics. First, because of the fourth power the heat load is a strong function of the warm temperature and almost independent of the cold one: with black surfaces, 290 K to 80 K gives about 399 W/m², and 290 K to 4.2 K about 401 W/m², while 80 K to 4.2 K gives only 2.3 W/m². That is why thermal intercepts at 80 K are so valuable. Second, you can intercept radiation with floating thermal screens: n screens with no contact between them reduce the radiative flux to σε/(n+1) × (Tw⁴ − Tc⁴)/(2 − ε), and each screen settles at a temperature given by Ti⁴ = Tc⁴ + (i/(n+1))(Tw⁴ − Tc⁴).

Multi-layer insulation

MLI stacks many low-emittance reflectors (polyester film with a 300 to 400 Å aluminium coating) separated by insulating spacers (paper, silk, polyester net). It behaves like a stack of floating shields: n shields cut the radiation by about n + 1. Heat flow along the layers is about 1000 times larger than through them, so the blanket must be well overlapped and not compressed. An insulating spacer is needed because any metal contact short-circuits a shield.
