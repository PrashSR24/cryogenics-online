Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

The Joule–Thomson effect in motion

Watch gas molecules slow down as they are pulled apart, and see why hydrogen and helium warm up when throttled at room temperature.

What a throttle does

Force a gas through a valve, a porous plug or a narrow orifice and its pressure drops sharply. No work is done on the surroundings, and with good insulation no heat flows in or out, so the enthalpy stays constant: h₁ = h₂. This is the Joule–Thomson (J–T) expansion, and it is the cooling step of every simple liquefier. The temperature change per unit pressure drop is the Joule–Thomson coefficient, μ = (∂T/∂P) at constant enthalpy. Positive μ means the gas cools as the pressure falls; negative μ means it warms.

Why a real gas cools

For an ideal gas the molecules ignore one another, enthalpy depends on temperature alone, and a throttle changes nothing. Real molecules do two things:

  • They attract each other at moderate distances. When the gas expands the molecules are pulled apart, which raises their potential energy. With total energy fixed, the kinetic energy falls, so the gas cools.
  • They have a finite size, so they also repel at close range. In a hot, dense gas this repulsion dominates, and expansion releases energy that heats the gas.

Which effect wins depends on temperature. Below the inversion temperature attraction wins and the gas cools; above it the gas warms. The simplest model that captures both effects is the van der Waals gas, with attraction constant a and molecular volume b. At low pressure it gives μ ≈ (2a/RT − b)/Cp, so the inversion temperature is Ti = 2a/(Rb). Below, drag the sliders and watch the molecules. Inversion temperatures: a model and the real thing

The van der Waals model gets the pattern right, and it explains why nitrogen and oxygen cool at room temperature while hydrogen and helium do not. It is not accurate in the numbers: it over-estimates the inversion temperature of the heavier gases badly. The handbook values below are standard figures from outside the lectures.

What this means for liquefiers

  • Nitrogen, oxygen, air and argon start at room temperature far below their inversion temperatures, so a simple Linde–Hampson cycle works from the outset.
  • Hydrogen must be pre-cooled below about 200 K, usually with liquid nitrogen, before a J–T valve helps.
  • Helium must first be cooled below about 40 K, using an expansion engine or a liquid-hydrogen bath, before a J–T valve can finish the job. That is why every helium liquefier has expanders ahead of the valve.

J–T valve versus expansion engine

A throttle is irreversible: it wastes the pressure energy of the gas and cools only as a side effect. An expansion engine makes the gas do work and expands it nearly isentropically. It cools every gas, whatever its inversion temperature, and the work can be recovered. The price is moving parts at low temperature, so most plants combine both, with engines for the bulk cooling and a valve for the last step into the liquid.
