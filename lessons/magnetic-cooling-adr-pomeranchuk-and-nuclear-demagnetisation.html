Module 13 · Sub-kelvin cryogenics

Magnetic cooling: ADR, Pomeranchuk and nuclear demagnetisation

Cooling with no pumps and no fluids: cleverly ordering and disordering tiny magnets, and freezing helium-3 to cool it.

Cooling without any fluid

Adiabatic demagnetisation refrigeration (ADR) cools entirely electronically: no pumps, no circulating fluid. It is therefore well suited to space instruments and small experiments where a dilution refrigerator would be impractical. It was proposed independently by Debye (1926) and Giauque (1927) and first shown in 1933 (530 mK, then 270 mK), long before helium-3 was available. It dominated sub-kelvin research until the 1950s and 1960s, fell from favour when helium-3 and dilution machines arrived, and returned in the 2000s as helium-3 became scarce and costly and as space and suborbital missions needed compact cold sources. It is most commonly used around 100 mK because that is reachable from a 4 K start, though it can go as low as 2 mK.

Entropy in a magnet

A paramagnetic ion with total angular momentum J has 2J + 1 possible orientations in a field, so its disordered state carries an entropy S = R ln(2J + 1) per mole. Two things lower that entropy:

  • Lower temperature: the moments interact with each other and eventually order spontaneously, and by the third law S goes to zero.
  • An applied field: the moments partly line up with it, creating order. The ratio of magnetic energy to thermal energy B/T decides how much.

Here is the cycle, drawn on an entropy–temperature diagram:

  1. A to B, magnetise isothermally. A heat switch connects the salt pill to the precooling bath (at Ti). The field is raised to Bi. The entropy falls, and the heat of magnetisation, Q = n Ti [S(0, Ti) − S(Bi, Ti)], flows into the bath: several joules per mole.
  2. B to C, isolate. Open the heat switch.
  3. C to D, demagnetise adiabatically. Ramp the field down. The entropy is constant, and for free moments it depends only on B/T, so Tf/Bf = Ti/Bi. The pill cools.

The temperature cannot be pushed to zero by lowering Bf to zero, because the moments then order spontaneously. After demagnetisation the refrigerant absorbs heat as it warms along the low-field curve; that cooling power is far smaller than the heat of magnetisation, because it is taken in at temperatures below Ti. Low final temperature and large cooling power are always a compromise.

This calculator uses the textbook spin-½ formula for free moments, S/R = ln(2 cosh x) − x tanh x with x = μBB/kBT. The lecture shows measured entropy curves for real salts rather than this formula, so the numbers are a model of the trend. It reproduces the order of magnitude the lecture states (a few joules per mole of heat of magnetisation).

Choosing and building the pill

The salt sets the limit. Water of crystallisation spaces the magnetic ions apart, which lowers the ordering temperature. Each salt has a low-temperature or high-temperature range:

Chromium methylammonium alumCMA16 mKHigh-temperature range
Ferric ammonium alumFAA26 mKHigh-temperature range

Gadolinium sulphateGS182 mKHigh-temperature range
  • Thermal contact. These salts are poor conductors. Crystals are grown around a bus of copper or gold wires, or the canister is filled with a little helium-3 exchange gas.
  • Hermetic can. The salts contain a lot of water; a welded stainless-steel canister stops them drying out in vacuum.
  • Suspension. The pill is hung on tensioned Kevlar thread, rigid but a poor conductor. Kevlar expands on cooling, so springs maintain the tension. Space designs use two different supports at each end for a kinematic mount.
  • Heat switch. Either a mechanical switch (a motor pressing contact surfaces together) or a helium-3 gas-gap switch with no moving parts.

Real machines

  • Laboratory ADR: typically two stages (1 K and 100 mK) on a pulse-tube cooler, with about 1 J of capacity at 1 K and about 120 mJ at 100 mK.
  • HAWC+ (the far-infrared camera on the SOFIA airborne telescope): holds 100 mK from a 1.5 K bath with a very large pill of nearly 2 mol of FAA.
  • ASTRO-H (a space X-ray telescope): a three-stage continuous ADR, with two gadolinium-gallium-garnet stages and one CPA stage, cooling a superconducting X-ray detector array to 50 mK. Helium-3 gas-gap switches join the stages.

An ADR is inherently one-shot, because it must be recycled when the pill warms up. A continuous ADR uses several independent stages with separate magnets, in series or in parallel (one pill is recycled while another is cold). It works but is very complex.

Pomeranchuk cooling: freeze helium-3 to cool it

Normally freezing a liquid gives off heat, because the solid is more ordered than the liquid. Pomeranchuk predicted in 1950 that helium-3 below about 0.3 K does the opposite: its melting curve has a minimum (at 29.3 bar and 315 mK) and below that the solid has more entropy than the liquid, because the solid's nuclear spins are still disordered. By the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, the negative slope of the melting line means that compressing liquid helium-3 so that part of it solidifies absorbs heat. The cooling power is Q̇ = ṅsol T (Ssolid − Sliquid) with Ssolid = R ln 2 and, for the liquid below about 30 mK, Sliquid = 36 T J/mol·K. Anufriev first did it in 1965, cooling from 50 mK to below 18 mK; Wheatley and co-workers reached 2 mK. It was the only route to a few millikelvin until strong dilution refrigerators appeared in the late 1970s.

Nuclear demagnetisation: into the microkelvin range

Nuclei carry magnetic moments too, so the same trick can be used with nuclear spins. The advantage is that nuclear moments order only at extremely low temperatures, so the refrigerant can be cooled into microkelvin territory. The catch is that nuclear moments are weak: the nuclear magneton is 5.1 × 10⁻²⁷ J/T against 9.3 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T for the Bohr magneton, about 1800 times smaller. A pure metal such as copper is used, because it conducts heat well and has many moments. Starting from 8 T and 6 mK, demagnetising copper removes only about 9% of the nuclear spin entropy. The cooling power Q = n ∫ T dS is many orders of magnitude smaller than in the salt, and the nuclear spins must then cool the conduction electrons and the lattice. Nuclear stages are launched from below 1 K, usually from a dilution refrigerator, and are used to study materials and gases, not to cool real apparatus. Summing up, the useful ranges are: pumped helium refrigerators 1 K to 0.3 K; salt demagnetisation 1 K to 30 mK; dilution refrigerators 1 K to 2 mK; Pomeranchuk cooling 0.3 K to 2 mK; nuclear demagnetisation 1 mK to 1 µK.
