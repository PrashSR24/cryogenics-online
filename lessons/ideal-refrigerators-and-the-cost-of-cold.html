Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Ideal refrigerators and the cost of cold

Why a watt of cooling at 4 K costs about 75 W from the wall even in a perfect machine, and why ideal cycles cannot be built.

The cheapest possible cold

A cryogenic cycle lifts heat from a cold reservoir and dumps it into the warm surroundings. The second law says that costs work, and the ideal cycle shows the minimum. The ideal isothermal-load refrigerator is a Carnot refrigerator: isothermal compression at the ambient temperature, isentropic expansion, isothermal heat absorption at the cold temperature (the refrigeration load), isentropic compression. The refrigeration power is Qref = Tcold(s₄ − s₃) and the net work is (Tamb − Tcold)(s₄ − s₃), so

COP = Tcold / (Tamb − Tcold)

for an ideal gas it depends only on the two temperatures, not on the fluid or its pressure. For a 4 K load and 300 K surroundings the COP is about 0.013: at least 75 W of mechanical power is needed to remove 1 W at 4 K. Real machines manage a fraction of this (often 10 to 30%), so a 4 K watt often costs 250 to 1000 W at the wall.

When the load is not at one temperature

Most real loads are not isothermal: a stream is cooled from a warm temperature T₂ down to T₁. The ideal cycle then has one isothermal process, two isentropic ones and an isobaric heat absorption. The refrigeration power is the enthalpy change, Qref = h₂ − h₁, and for an ideal gas the COP becomes

COP = (r − 1) / [ (Tamb/T₁) ln r − (r − 1) ], with r = T₂/T₁.

Cooling a stream down is far cheaper per watt than holding the same coldest temperature isothermally, because most of the heat is lifted at warmer temperatures.

The ideal liquefier

An ideal liquefier starts with gas at ambient temperature and pressure, compresses it isothermally, and expands it isentropically to a saturated liquid, with all the gas liquefied (yield one) and make-up gas added at room temperature. The net work per unit mass is W/ṁ = Tamb(s₁ − sf) − (h₁ − hf). It is the sum of a series of small Carnot refrigerators between the ambient temperature and the gas temperature as it cools, plus a Carnot refrigerator at the boiling point that removes the latent heat. At 300 K this reproduces the lecture: about 26,400 J/mol, which is 6600 J/g or about 0.23 kWh per litre of liquid helium, as the minimum.

Why ideal cycles cannot be built

  • The compressor would have to be absurd. An isothermal compressor must discharge at pideal = patm exp(ΔS/R). The lecture finds: nitrogen about 7.4 × 10⁵ atm (74 GPa), argon about 1.7 × 10⁵ atm (17 GPa), helium-4 about 7.2 × 10⁵ atm (72 GPa), hydrogen about 9.5 × 10⁵ atm, neon about 9.8 × 10⁵ atm. No compressor can do that.
  • Isentropic expansion ends in liquid. A turbine or piston expander cannot handle a stream that finishes as saturated liquid.
  • The fluid is a real gas. Enthalpy depends on pressure as well as temperature, which is exactly what a throttle exploits.

The practical cycle

Real cycles stop compression at an achievable pressure, add a counterflow heat exchanger that pre-cools the forward stream with the cold return stream (so that the expansion starts below the inversion temperature), and replace the isentropic expander with an isenthalpic Joule–Thomson valve near the two-phase dome. The price is that the cycle ends inside the dome: only part of the stream liquefies (yield y < 1) and the isothermal refrigeration available falls from T(sv − sf) to T(sv − s₄). The heat exchange and throttling generate entropy, so the work per unit liquid is larger. Real compressors are described by an isothermal efficiency and a mechanical efficiency (overall is their product): compression is adiabatic followed by constant-pressure heat removal in an aftercooler. Expanders have an adiabatic efficiency, η = (h₁ − h₂)/(h₁ − h₂s), and a mechanical efficiency. Heat exchangers are recuperative (continuous steady flows) or regenerative (warm and cold streams use the same channel at different times, with the matrix storing heat).
