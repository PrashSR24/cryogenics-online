Module 6 · Cryocoolers

Cryocooler fundamentals and the Stirling cycle

Closed-cycle machines that replace liquid cryogens, and the ideal and real Stirling refrigerator.

What a cryocooler is

A cryocooler is a mechanical refrigerator that makes low temperature by compressing and expanding a gas in a closed cycle: the mass of working gas stays constant. It has a compressor, a heat exchanger and an expander, and the cold made in the expander is passed to the object to be cooled through a cold-end heat exchanger (an evaporator). Machines that reach 77 K or 4.2 K can replace liquid nitrogen or liquid helium, which is attractive because they need no cryogen supply, run reliably with little maintenance, and cryogen prices are rising while cryocooler prices are falling.

Practical requirements depend on the use but usually include low weight and volume, quick cool-down, low vibration, long mean time between failures, little sensitivity to orientation and noise, and cost-effectiveness. The numbers that decide the choice are the cooling power at a given temperature, the compressor power, the cooling water and servicing needs, vibration level and cost.

Classification

Cryogenic refrigeration can be open cycle (stored gas, stored cryogen) or closed cycle. The closed-cycle machines split by how they exchange heat:

Heat exchanger typeHow it worksCryocoolers
RecuperativeTwo streams flow steadily and at the same time, separated by a solid wall (counter, cross or parallel flow)Joule–Thomson, Brayton, Claude
RegenerativeOne matrix stores heat from the warm gas, then gives it to the cold gas in alternate periods, so the flow is oscillating and the heat transfer is indirectStirling, Gifford–McMahon, pulse tube

In the J–T cryocooler a reciprocating compressor feeds a counterflow exchanger and an isenthalpic valve, so the gas must start below its inversion temperature. The Brayton cooler is similar but expands the gas in a work-producing expander. Regenerative machines need only a periodic pressure oscillation and a regenerator, which makes them compact.

The ideal Stirling cycle

Robert Stirling conceived the cycle in 1815 as an engine; John Herschel suggested using it as a cooler in 1834, Alexander Kirk built one in 1861, and Jan Koehler made the first commercial machine in 1956 and liquefied nitrogen with it in 1965. The ideal refrigeration cycle has four processes on a p–V diagram:

  • 1 → 2 isothermal compression at the warm temperature TC; heat is rejected.
  • 2 → 3 constant-volume cooling; the regenerator absorbs heat from the gas.
  • 3 → 4 isothermal expansion at the cold temperature TE; the gas absorbs heat QE, the useful cooling.
  • 4 → 1 constant-volume heating; the regenerator gives its heat back.

With a perfect regenerator the two constant-volume steps cancel, and the COP equals that of a Carnot refrigerator, COP = TE / (TC − TE). Scrub or play the cycle:

Real machines

An actual Stirling cooler has continuous (harmonic) piston motion instead of the discontinuous ideal one, dead volume, an imperfect regenerator, and non-isothermal compression and expansion. The geometry can be α-type (two pistons, a compression piston and an expansion piston, whose motion is out of phase by an angle α), β-type (a displacer and power piston in one cylinder) or γ-type (a displacer in one cylinder and a piston in another, with a variable compression space). Typical features of Stirling coolers: high efficiency, small size and weight, dry or no lubrication, and high reliability, but vibration from the moving displacer. Uses include infrared sensors, satellites, high-temperature superconductors and the liquefaction of nitrogen, air and hydrogen.

Schmidt's analysis and sizing

In 1861 Gustav Schmidt gave an analysis of a more realistic cycle that assumes perfect isothermal compression and expansion, harmonic piston motion and perfect regeneration. It is a first guess of dimensions. Its non-dimensional design parameters are the swept volume ratio k = VC/VE, the temperature ratio τ = TC/TE, the dead volume ratio X = VD/VE, and the phase angle α; the operating parameters are speed N and maximum pressure pmax. Results are non-dimensionalised as Qmax = QE/(pmaxVT), with VT the total swept volume. In 1962 Graham Walker combined the effects into optimisation charts. Real losses (regenerator ineffectiveness, pressure drop, solid and shuttle conduction, drive losses) mean Qnet = QE − Σ losses and Wtotal = WT + Σ losses. The lecture's rule is to design for three times the required cooling, QE,design = 3 QE,req.

The lecture sizes an α-type helium Stirling nitrogen liquefier (TE = 77.2 K, TC = 300 K) and reads k = 2.85, α = 0.575 (quoted as 32.9°) and Qmax = 0.07 off Walker's chart for X = 1. The watch-it-run figure above suggests the optimum phase angle for this k and X is near 90° to 100°, where the model also gives Qmax ≈ 0.07, so 0.575 may be in units of π radians (about 103°) rather than radians. Treat the angle with caution; the volumes below do not depend on it. Change the capacity, pressure and speed and see the resulting dimensions. The chart values are held fixed, so they only hold near these conditions.

With the lecture's inputs the result is about 965 W required, 2895 W design, VT ≈ 430 cm³, compression cylinder diameter 81.4 mm and stroke 60.8 mm, expansion cylinder 57.5 mm by 43.1 mm, using a stroke-to-bore ratio of 0.75. For a very large design the lecture suggests using two or more cylinders, or working near the maximum, with phase angles of roughly 60° to 120°.
