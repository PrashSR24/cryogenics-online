Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Claude system

Adding an expansion engine to recover work and cold.

Why add an expansion engine

A J–T valve expands gas at constant enthalpy. That is irreversible and wastes the pressure energy of the gas. An expansion engine instead lets the gas do work while it expands, which is close to a reversible adiabatic (isentropic) process. It cools any gas whatever its inversion temperature, and the work can help drive the compressor. The system is named after its inventor, who built an air liquefier on this idea and established l'Air Liquide in 1902.

The system

Animation: a fraction x of the compressed gas is diverted at state 3, expanded in the engine to state e, and rejoins the cold return stream ahead of the first exchanger.

The cycle has a compressor, three counterflow exchangers, an expansion engine working across the second exchanger, a J–T valve and a makeup connection. The rest of the gas, a fraction 1 − x, carries on to the valve.

Liquid yield

An energy balance on the exchangers, valve and receiver gives:

y = (h1 − h2)/(h1 − hf) + x (h3 − he)/(h1 − hf)

The first term is the yield of a simple Linde–Hampson cycle. The second is the extra yield from the expander, where x = me/m. For a fixed expander inlet state 3, y rises in a straight line with x.

The balance only holds while some gas is left in the return stream, m − mf − me > 0, which means x + y < 1. Past that point the lecture uses a limiting yield y = 0.99 − x, and notes that the real yield may be lower.

Work

−Wnet/m = T1(s1 − s2) − (h1 − h2) − x (h3 − he)

The first part is the compressor work of the simple Linde–Hampson cycle. The last term is the work recovered by the expander, assuming it helps drive the compressor.

Effect of expander inlet temperature

The lecture repeats the calculation for four expander inlet temperatures. Its tabulated results at the best point of each case are:

Expander inlet T3x at best pointMax yield yMin work (J/g liquefied)
300 K0.670.32608.8
275 K0.690.30659.3

225 K0.730.26866.8

In every case yield peaks, and work per unit of liquid is lowest, where x + y reaches the limit. The peak moves to higher x as T3 falls. The best work here, roughly 600 to 870 J/g, is far below the 5214 J/g of the simple Linde–Hampson air case in the previous lesson, although those two cases use different gases and pressures.

Key points
  • The expander removes energy reversibly and returns work
  • y = simple L–H yield + x (h3 − he)/(h1 − hf), valid for x + y < 1
  • Yield has a maximum and work per unit liquid a minimum as x increases
