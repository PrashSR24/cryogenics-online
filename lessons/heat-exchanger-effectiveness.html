Module 4 · Liquefaction and refrigeration

Heat exchanger effectiveness

Why a Linde–Hampson liquefier lives or dies by its counterflow exchanger.

What effectiveness means

A heat exchanger passes cold from the returning low-pressure stream to the incoming high-pressure stream, at constant pressure. Its effectiveness ε is the heat actually transferred divided by the most that could be transferred in theory:

ε = Qactual / Qmax, with Qmax = (ṁCp)min (Thot,in − Tcold,in)

It is a number between 0 and 1. In the Linde–Hampson cycle it can be written with enthalpies: the returning gas leaves at h1′ instead of the ideal h1, so

ε = (h1′ − hg) / (h1 − hg)

Effect on liquid yield

An energy balance with the imperfect exchanger gives

y = [(h1 − h2) − (1 − ε)(h1 − hg)] / [(h1 − hf) − (1 − ε)(h1 − hg)]

The term (1 − ε)(h1 − hg) is the cold that leaks away at the warm end. It is subtracted from the small cooling effect of the valve, so a modest loss of effectiveness removes most of the yield. Try it with the lecture's air case (1.013 to 202.6 bar, 300 K).

Liquid yield against effectiveness

The lecture tabulates 1.00 → 0.085, 0.95 → 0.060, 0.90 → 0.034 and 0.85 → 0.006. The curve above reproduces those and shows that yield reaches zero near ε = 0.84. That is why the lecture says effectiveness must be better than about 85% before the cycle makes any liquid at all, and why real liquefiers use very long, carefully designed exchangers.

Figure of merit

To compare liquefiers fairly, the lecture defines the figure of merit: the ideal work per unit mass liquefied divided by the actual work per unit mass liquefied.

FOM = (Wideal/mf) / (Wactual/mf)

Worked case from the lecture: nitrogen, 1.013 to 202.6 bar, 300 K, ε = 1.

Ideal work, T1(s1 − sf) − (h1 − hf)761 J/g liquefied

So even a perfect-exchanger Linde–Hampson liquefier uses roughly eight times the ideal work. Later lessons show how precooling, a second pressure level and expansion engines close that gap.
