Module 11 · Systems and storage

Cryostat design

The eight parts of a cryostat, the heat-load budget that sizes the cooler, and thirteen rules from people who have built them.

The main elements

  1. Vacuum vessel. The outermost shell: contains the insulating vacuum (10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁶ torr, the lower the better), is the main structure, and contains the pressure if a cold line leaks. Designed to the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section VIII.
  2. Thermal shield and insulation. A shield at 50 to 80 K, sometimes a second at 5 K or 20 K, intercepts radiation and is also the heat sink for supports, current leads and power couplers. Cooled by LN₂, cold helium gas or a cryocooler. Because it is a passive component, keep its cooldown time short with a high-conductivity, low-heat-capacity material such as aluminium. Covered with MLI: 30 to 60 layers on an 80 K shield, 10 to 15 on a colder one, about 50 to 60 layers per inch, and a realistic load of about 1.5 W/m² at 80 K and 0.15 W/m² at 4.5 K.
  3. Cryogenic process piping and cryocoolers. Supply and return lines compatible with the other materials and with codes such as ASME B31.3. Cryocoolers (Stirling, Gifford–McMahon, pulse tube) give 0.5 to 5 W at 4.2 K and a few hundred watts at 80 K, with a 50 K stage for the shield and a 4 K stage for conduction cooling.
  4. Supports for the cold mass. They must hold alignment through shipping and operation, yet insulate heat from 300 K. Strength and low conductivity pull in opposite directions; use materials with low thermal-conductivity integrals, such as stainless steel, titanium alloy and G-10 composites, and intercept the heat at the shield.
  5. Flexibility and vibration isolation. Everything shrinks on cooling. Bellows give axial flexibility, flex hoses lateral flexibility, and flexible thermal straps give flexibility, vibration isolation and a conduction path together.
  6. Magnetic shield. A high-permeability alloy (mu-metal at 300 K, Cryoperm when cold) 1.5 to 2 mm thick keeps the field at a cavity or sensor low, for example under 15 mG. It must not be bent or stressed after annealing.
  7. Instrumentation and wiring. Mount sensors at room temperature outside the vacuum when possible and heat-sink every lead wire.
  8. Over-pressure protection. Relief valves and rupture discs; see the pressure-relief lesson.

A worked example: a cryocooler-cooled SRF test cryostat

The lectures describe a cryostat built to test an SRF cavity with no liquid helium. Pulse-tube coolers (a Cryomech PT420 gives 2 W at 4.2 K and 55 W at 45 K) cool a 45 K shield and a 4 K cavity; the vessel has four cooler ports. The shield hangs on four stainless-steel threaded rods and is wrapped in MLI; heat estimates are about 7 W from rod conduction and 7 W from radiation through MLI, under 15 W in total against 55 W of capacity. The cavity hangs from four titanium Ti-6Al-4V rods and is joined to the cooler by 5N aluminium flexible links; conduction from 300 K to the cold mass is only 70 mW, the rods are stressed to about 510 psi against a yield strength of 120,000 psi, and the cavity dissipates 2 W at about 4.8 K. Rebuild that heat budget yourself. The conduction integrals use the lecture's tabulated values (4 to 76 K and 76 to 300 K), interpolated to a 45 K shield.

Thirteen design tips from the lectures

  1. Define and prioritise requirements first.
  2. Design safety features in from the start.
  3. Use only materials shown to be suitable at cryogenic temperature.
  4. Study the literature and use existing codes and standards.
  5. Use tested commercial solutions where you can.
  6. Intercept heat at higher temperatures; remember the second law, since removing a watt at 77 K costs far less work than at 4 K.
  7. Allow for thermal contraction; do not over-constrain parts as they cool.
  8. Avoid feedthroughs and demountable seals at cryogenic temperature.
  9. Heat-sink sensor wires so that leak and reading are both right.
  10. Install pressure transducers and flow meters at room temperature when possible.
  11. Analyse the design for thermoacoustic oscillations.
  12. Hold independent design reviews at conceptual and detailed stages.
  13. Test a prototype, and when you make many cryostats, series-test the production units too.
