Module 6 · Cryocoolers

Cryocoolers in motion

Watch the displacer, the valves and the gas parcels of the Gifford–McMahon, pulse tube and recuperative coolers, and see why each one makes cold.

One pressure wave, four strokes

A regenerative cooler needs only two things: a gas whose pressure rises and falls, and a regenerator that stores heat between strokes. In the Gifford–McMahon (GM) machine the pressure wave is made by two valves connecting the cold head to a compressor's high- and low-pressure lines, and a displacer that holds the regenerator moves the gas between a warm space at the top and a cold space at the bottom. One turn of the cycle has four steps:

  1. Pressurise. The displacer sits at the cold end. The high-pressure valve opens and gas flows into the warm space. It heats as it is compressed and rejects that heat at the warm flange.
  2. Cool at high pressure. The displacer rises. The gas flows down through the regenerator into the cold space, giving its heat to the regenerator material on the way.
  3. Expand. The high-pressure valve closes and the low-pressure valve opens. The gas in the cold space expands and drops in temperature, and it absorbs heat from the load at the cold tip. This is the useful cooling.
  4. Return at low pressure. The displacer moves down and pushes the cold gas back up through the regenerator, which it cools for the next cycle, before the gas returns to the compressor.

Use the tabs below to move between the three machines. Press Pause and drag the cycle slider to freeze any moment, and watch the valves, the displacer, the gas colour and the pressure marker together. The compressor's work heats and cools the gas through the pressure ratio; the regenerator carries the cold forward from stroke to stroke.

Why the regenerator is the heart of it

In the GM cooler the regenerator is a porous matrix of stainless-steel mesh, or lead at the lowest temperatures, that absorbs heat from the downward-moving gas and gives it back to the upward-moving gas. It must have a large surface and a heat capacity far above that of the gas. The material sets the range: a single stage with stainless-steel mesh reaches about 30 K, two stages reach about 10 K, and a lead regenerator gets down to about 4.2 K. Regenerator performance is a major limit on how cold and how powerful the cooler can be.

A cooler with no cold moving part

The pulse tube cooler removes the displacer entirely. The gas itself acts as the piston. The pulse tube is a thin empty tube between a cold-end heat exchanger and a hot-end one; gas parcels in the middle of it never leave it. When the compressor raises the pressure, high-pressure gas flows through the regenerator, is pre-cooled, and pushes into the tube, compressing the gas already there. That gas warms, and the heat is rejected at the hot end, which is held at ambient by water. When the pressure falls the gas in the tube expands and cools, and it flows back toward the cold end, where it absorbs heat. The pulse-tube animation draws each gas parcel in a colour that follows its temperature: parcels near the hot end are red at high pressure, and parcels near the cold end are blue at low pressure.

What makes this work is the phase shift between pressure and flow in the tube. In the simplest design an orifice and a reservoir at the hot end make the gas in the tube lag behind the pressure, so the gas arrives at the cold end at the right moment to absorb heat. Without it, no net cooling is produced. The advantages are no moving parts at the cold end, no low-temperature seals and lower vibration.

Steady flow: J–T and Brayton coolers

The third tab shows a different family: recuperative coolers, in which two streams flow steadily in opposite directions through a counterflow heat exchanger separated by a solid wall. There is no pressure wave. A compressor feeds the high-pressure stream through the exchanger, and an expansion device drops its pressure and temperature. The returning cold low-pressure stream cools the incoming gas, so the cold builds up from the compressor end to the cold end. If the expansion device is a throttle valve, the machine is a Joule–Thomson cooler, and the gas must start below its inversion temperature. If it is a work-producing turbine, the machine is a Brayton cooler, which cools any gas and recovers some work. Unlike the closed liquefier loops earlier in the course, a cryocooler of this kind has no liquid product: the cold stage absorbs a heat load and the gas goes back to the compressor.

Pressure ratio and the temperature swing

The pressure ratio sets how much the gas warms and cools. For an ideal adiabatic change in helium, T varies as p0.4, so at a ratio of 2.5 the gas swings from about 208 K to 432 K starting from 300 K. In a real cooler the regenerator pre-cools the gas before it expands, so the cold end reaches far below what a single expansion from room temperature could. Frequency matters as well. From the lecture table, Stirling coolers run at 20 to 150 Hz, compact and with a dry compressor, while GM coolers run at 1 to 5 Hz with an oil-lubricated compressor and valves.
