Module 14 · Experimental cryogenics

Lab: Calibrating thermometers

How to turn a resistance or a voltage into a trustworthy temperature: the comparison method, fixed points, how many points you need, and the errors that creep in.

A cryogenic thermometer does not tell you the temperature; it gives you a resistance or a voltage. A calibration is the table or curve that turns that signal into a temperature, built by comparing the sensor with something you trust. Everything you later conclude about your experiment rests on it. This lesson is general laboratory practice, not taken from the lectures.

What to compare against

  • A calibrated reference thermometer. This is the usual method in a laboratory. A reference sensor with a certified calibration is put in the same isothermal block as the sensor under test, and the two are compared at a series of temperatures.
  • Fixed points. Pure substances have temperatures that are the same everywhere in the world. At the triple point a pure substance holds solid, liquid and vapour in equilibrium at one temperature. Some defining fixed points of the international temperature scale are hydrogen at 13.8033 K, neon at 24.5561 K, oxygen at 54.3584 K, argon at 83.8058 K, mercury at 234.3156 K and water at 273.16 K. These are used in standards laboratories.
  • Boiling baths. Liquid nitrogen boils at 77.35 K and liquid helium at about 4.2 K at atmospheric pressure. They are handy checks, but the temperature depends on the pressure, so read the barometer. Liquid nitrogen that has taken up oxygen from the air boils warmer, which can shift a 77 K check by a degree or more.

The comparison method in practice

The set-up is a small, solid copper block, thermally anchored to the cold stage through a controlled thermal link, with a heater on it. The reference thermometer and the sensors under test are fitted into drilled holes or clamped on, with a thin film of vacuum grease for good thermal contact. A radiation shield surrounds the block. A temperature controller holds the block at each set point in turn. The routine has five parts:

  1. Step to a set point and wait for equilibrium. Look at the reference reading; it should stop drifting.
  2. Take the readings of the reference and every sensor, with four-wire measurements and a low excitation current.
  3. Move to the next set point. Cover the range with more points where the sensor changes fastest or most unevenly.
  4. Repeat some points on the way back, going up as well as down, to check for hysteresis and drift.
  5. Fit a smooth curve through the points. It is the curve, not the table, that you will use between the points.

Watch the animation. The block steps through a series of set points. At each one it waits until the temperature has settled, and only then does the system record a point.

How many points do you need?

The curve you fit is an interpolation, and it can only be as good as the points that define it. Too few points and the curve wanders between them; too many for the degree of the fit and it chases the noise. The simulator calibrates a generic negative-temperature-coefficient resistor, of the kind used from room temperature down to a few kelvin. Its resistance follows R = R0 exp(√(T0/T)), with R0 = 40 Ω and T0 = 78 K, giving about 67 Ω at 300 K and about 3 kΩ at 4.2 K. This is an illustrative model, not a specific product. The fit is a Chebyshev polynomial of log T in log R, by least squares. Try changing the number of points, the degree and the noise on the reference readings.

The residual is the difference between the fitted curve and the true curve at 300 test temperatures. Note how bad an even spacing in temperature is for this sensor: it changes by orders of magnitude at low temperature, so it needs points that are dense there. If the degree is too low, the curve cannot follow the shape. If the degree is close to the number of points, the curve passes through the noise and can swing between the points. A good calibration has a few more points than the degree, spread where the sensor is most sensitive and where it changes most rapidly.

Errors that creep in

Self-heating. The measuring current dissipates power I²R in the sensor. At low temperature the thermal contact to the surroundings is poor, so the sensor warms above the thing it is measuring. Lead resistance. A two-wire measurement adds the resistance of the leads to the sensor's; a four-wire measurement avoids it. The calculator below shows both errors for the same generic sensor.

Good habits

  • Use the lowest excitation that gives a clean signal. Lower the current until the reading stops changing.
  • Anchor the leads thermally at each stage, so heat does not flow along them into the sensor.
  • Keep the sensor in good thermal contact with the thing you are measuring: a bolted copper block, not a free-hanging bead.
  • Wait for equilibrium. A thermometer that reads 2 K off during a fast cooldown is not wrong, just late.
  • Check the calibration regularly against a fixed point: liquid nitrogen at 77 K, and liquid helium at 4.2 K. Record the date and the result.
  • Be careful in magnetic fields: many sensors change their reading in a field.
