Module 3 · Materials

Specific heat and thermal conduction

Why solids need so little energy to cool further, and how to calculate heat flow when conductivity changes with temperature.

Specific heat of solids

At room temperature each lattice point of a solid stores the same energy, which gives the Dulong–Petit result cv = 3R, independent of temperature. That fails in the cold. Einstein modelled the solid as identical oscillators. Debye did better by counting every standing wave in the solid, which gives the lattice heat capacity per mole:

cv = 9R (T/θD)³ ∫0θD/T x⁴eˣ / (eˣ − 1)² dx

Only the Debye temperature θD changes from one material to another. When T is above 2θD, cv approaches 3R. Below θD/12 it follows a cubic law, cv = (12π⁴/5) R (T/θD)³, so it collapses very quickly as the temperature drops.

Debye temperatures are from the lecture's table. This is the lattice contribution only; in metals the electrons add a small extra at very low temperature, and Cp and Cv are nearly equal for solids at these temperatures. Worked checks from the lecture: copper at 100 K gives cv/R of about 1.93, roughly 253 J/kg·K, and aluminium at 25 K gives about 19 J/kg·K.

Thermal conductivity in solids

Supports and struts inside a cryostat should have low conductivity to cut heat leak, while a cold link that must carry cooling to a load should have high conductivity, which is why copper is used. In general conductivity falls as temperature falls, but pure metals behave differently from alloys.

  • Pure metals: both electrons and lattice vibrations (phonons) carry heat. Above liquid-nitrogen temperature electrons dominate and conductivity stays roughly constant. Below it phonons take over, conductivity rises (varying about as 1/T²) to a peak, and then falls when the electrons' mean free path becomes as large as the specimen.
  • Impure metals and alloys: electrons and phonons contribute about equally, and imperfections such as grain boundaries and impurities scatter them, so there is no large peak.

Conductivity integrals

Fourier's law is Q = −k(T) A dT/dx, but k changes strongly with temperature. Define the thermal conductivity integral relative to a datum, θ(T) = ∫k dT from Td (0 or 4.2 K) to T. For a constant cross-section A and length L, the heat flow is

Q = (A/L) [θ(Thot) − θ(Tcold)]

Only the end temperatures are needed, not the temperature profile. Averaging k at the two ends is tempting but can be wrong when k is not linear in T. This demo uses a made-up power law, k = k₃₀₀ (T/300)ⁿ, to show the size of the mistake.

Illustrative model, not a real material: k at 300 K = 10 W/m·K, area 1 cm², length 0.1 m.
Key points
  • Debye: cv tends to 3R above 2θD and falls as T³ below θD/12
  • Pure metals have a conductivity peak at low temperature; alloys do not
  • Use the conductivity integral, not an average of k, when k varies strongly
