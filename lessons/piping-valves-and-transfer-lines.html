Module 11 · Systems and storage

Piping, valves and transfer lines

Moving cryogens without freezing the plant, vapour-locking the pipe or losing half the liquid on the way.

Transfer lines

A cryogenic transfer line is a pipe inside a vacuum jacket, usually wrapped in multi-layer insulation and often with a nitrogen-cooled shield for helium. Design rules from the lectures:

  • Slope the line up toward the warm end so thermal stratification can establish itself, or if it must fall, make it fall after a short rise. Avoid low points and siphons in liquid-carrying lines, where vapour or trapped liquid causes surges.
  • Allow for thermal contraction with bellows or loops; a cold line shortens by millimetres per metre.
  • Put a phase separator at the delivery end of a long siphon. Two-phase flow arrives as gas and liquid; a sintered-bronze phase separator vents the gas and delivers liquid. Place such filters where they can be reached, because they clog.
  • Shields are built as concentric cylinders, brazed cooling pipes, extruded finned elements, or quilted panels; the spacing of cooling pipes is set by the shield's conduction (the greater the sheet conductivity, the further apart the pipes may go).

Valves

Cryogenic valves must keep the cold end cold and the stem seal warm. The helium plant specification quoted in the lectures is typical: extended-spindle valves with body and stem in line; a welded metal-bellows dynamic seal, designed for 10,000 full-stroke cycles, backed by a safety stuffing box; a static O-ring seal at the warm end (a double O-ring with guard gas in sub-atmospheric service); body of austenitic stainless steel such as 316L; stem and seat arranged so the trim can be changed without breaking the insulating vacuum. Leak-tightness targets are 10⁻⁶ Pa·m³/s to the atmosphere across the seal, 10⁻⁵ across the seat and 10⁻⁹ Pa·m³/s into the insulating vacuum.

Sizing a valve: Kv

The flow coefficient Kv is the flow of water in m³/h that gives a 1 bar pressure drop. For a liquid it is Kv = ṁ / √(1000 ρ Δp), with ṁ in kg/h, density ρ in kg/m³ and Δp in bar. There are separate formulas for gases, in subcritical and critical (choked) flow, because for gas the pressure and temperature at the inlet enter as well.

Pipe pressure drop

Δp = λ (l/d) (ρ v²/2), with friction factor λ from the Reynolds number Re = ρ v d / μ: for Re below 2300 the flow is laminar and λ = 64/Re; for 2300 < Re < 10⁵ in smooth pipe, Blasius gives λ = 0.3164 / Re0.25; for 10⁵ < Re < 10⁸ Nikuradse gives λ = 0.0032 + 0.221 / Re0.237.
