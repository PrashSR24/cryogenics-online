Module 13 · Sub-kelvin cryogenics

Materials and thermometry below 1 kelvin

At millikelvin temperatures the cooling power is tiny, so even aged stress, hydrogen in copper and a thermometer's own heating matter.

When cooling power is measured in microwatts

Sub-kelvin refrigerators deliver many orders of magnitude less power than machines at 4 K, so four things you might ignore at higher temperature become critical: thermal conduction, thermal isolation, thermal contact and time-dependent heat sources inside the materials themselves.

Conduction

The conductivity integral from earlier lessons applies again, but the physics changes. Phonons freeze out, strongly cutting the conductivity of insulators and leaving electrons as the main carriers in metals. Superconductors become good insulators well below their critical temperature, because the Cooper pairs carry no heat; the drop is gentler than for electrical resistance and mostly complete by Tc/10. Pure copper has a linear conductivity at low temperature and is excellent, while high-purity (high residual resistivity ratio) grades such as RRR 150 conduct better still.

For notional supports with the same stiffness, the heat conducted from 1 K to 0.1 K was:

Support materialHeat conducted, 1 K to 0.1 K

Titanium 15-3-3-3 (superconducting, Tc about 3 K)2.8 mW

The carbon-fibre composite wins because it is very disordered and scatters phonons; the titanium alloy wins because it is superconducting, and therefore insulates.

Joints

Joints are demountable (bolted or compression fits) or permanent (welds and braze). Beware solders and filler metals that are superconductors, which insulate the joint. Electron-beam welding of copper is very effective. Contact conductance depends on the tiny asperities that touch, so tricks such as thermal grease or indium foil that help at room temperature often stop working below 1 K, and can even degrade the joint. Gold (or silver or platinum) plating is effective. For bolted copper joints a design curve from the literature (Schmitt et al., 2015) is of the form K(T) = 0.1161 T^1.11 for a 10-32 screw, scaled up by tensile area for bigger screws and multiplied by the number of screws; check the units in the original paper before using it, because the slide does not state them.

Heat that appears on its own

Cold metal and plastic are not inert. Three processes release energy slowly, over days:

  • Ortho–para hydrogen conversion. Commercial copper contains 10 to 100 ppm of hydrogen, trapped as small gas pockets. On cooling it liquefies and slowly converts from ortho to para, releasing 1.063 kJ per mole of ortho hydrogen converted. The conversion follows x(t) = x₀ / (1 + x₀ k t) with k = 0.019 per hour, so the heat rate falls as 1/t². Annealing the metal first lets the hydrogen escape.
  • Tunnelling in amorphous solids and crystals. Atoms hop between nearly equal energy states by quantum tunnelling and release energy at a rate that falls as 1/t. Typical amorphous materials release about 100 pW/g after 24 hours; the rate is strongly material dependent.
  • Stress relaxation. Thermoelastic stresses built up when the grains in a metal contract at different rates relax with a time constant of about 100 hours, Q̇ = W₀ e^(−t/τ), with W₀ about 500 pW/g for copper (a model gives about 700 pW/g, in agreement with experiment). It starts the moment stress develops, so cooling slowly helps.

The curves use the time dependences from the lecture, scaled to the lecture's after one day values (20 pW/g for hydrogen conversion and for tunnelling) and to W₀ = 500 pW/g for stress relaxation. They are order-of-magnitude guides, since each real sample differs. The practical defences are the same: use less mass, anneal before cooling and cool slowly.

Measuring temperature

Temperature is defined by the Carnot cycle, which gives only ratios, so a scale needs fixed points. The Kelvin is 1/273.16 of the triple point of water. The standard scale, ITS-90, is defined by the triple points of hydrogen (13.8033 K), neon (24.5561 K), oxygen (54.3584 K), argon (83.8058 K), mercury (234.3156 K) and water (273.16 K), plus melting and freezing points up to copper (1357.77 K) and, in the helium range, the vapour pressure of helium between 3 K and 5 K. Platinum resistance interpolates between the fixed points.

  • Primary thermometers need no calibration because the theory is well known: a gas thermometer (PV = nRT, with corrections for dead volume and non-ideal gas), or the thermal (Johnson) noise in a resistor. They are slow and fiddly, so they are mainly used at the very lowest temperatures and to calibrate others.
  • Secondary thermometers must be calibrated, but are easy to use. A good one is sensitive, quick to reach equilibrium, easy to read, insensitive to magnetic fields, with a simple temperature law and minimal self-heating.

Not heating the thermometer

A resistance thermometer dissipates P = I²R. At 4 K you can ignore this; at 20 mK the sensor's own power can warm it above its surroundings. Recommended maximum powers for a ruthenium oxide sensor are about 10⁻¹⁴ W at 20 mK, 10⁻¹⁰ W at 0.1 K, 10⁻⁹ W at 1 K and 10⁻⁸ W at 10 K. Use the highest excitation that does not cause self-heating; lower than that and the signal vanishes in noise.

  • Four-wire measurement separates the current leads from the voltage leads so lead resistance does not matter, at the price of twice as many wires and twice the conducted heat.
  • Shielding and filtering. Use twisted pairs, make the cryostat a Faraday cage with an explicit ground, and filter every line (RC, RL or RLC with a cutoff above the excitation frequency) in a grounded box that is well heat-sunk to a cold plate. Radio-frequency pickup shows up as heating in the sensor and gives a false reading.
  • Choose sensors by the temperature, accuracy and resolution you need and by whether there is a magnetic field or radiation. Often two sensors are used: platinum for the cooldown and ruthenium oxide at base.
