Module 7 · Insulation

Cryogenic insulation

Foams, powders, vacuum and multilayer insulation, with the heat leak of a real LN2 vessel.

Why insulation

A cryogen in a vessel boils off continuously because heat leaks in. The vessel cannot be sealed, since the vapour-to-liquid volume ratio of a typical cryogen is about 175 (and the pressure would rise). Heat reaches the cryogen by conduction through supports and any gas, by convection of gas in the space, and by radiation from the warm wall at 300 K to the cold one at 77 K. Insulation is chosen for its thermal conductivity, working temperature, effectiveness, cost, ease of application, weight and reliability, and often combined.

Heat transfer through a layer is described by the apparent thermal conductivity kA, defined by Q = kA A (T1 − T2)/L so that all modes are lumped together.

Foams and powders

Expanded foam is a low-density cellular solid with gas (such as CO2) in the voids, so heat moves by conduction in solid and gas. For 77 to 300 K the lecture gives:

MaterialDensity (kg/m³)kA (mW/m·K)
Polyurethane foam1133
Polystyrene foam3933
Rubber foam8036
Silica foam16055
Glass foam14035

Foam is cheap and can be blown directly onto the surface. At LN2 temperature, however, the CO2 in the cells condenses, and over time air, hydrogen or helium diffuse in, so kA rises. Foams also contract a lot: the linear expansion coefficient is 7.2 × 10−5 per °C for polystyrene foam against 1.15 × 10−5 for carbon steel, so a foam fitted snugly can crack or pull away.

Gas-filled powders and fibres (77 to 300 K) break up the gas into tiny gaps so gas conduction and convection fall. Perlite (50 kg/m³, 26 mW/m·K), silica aerogel (80, 19), glass fibre (10, 25) and rock wool (160, 35). The powders settle and pack, which raises solid conduction, and moisture and air must be kept out with a vapour barrier or nitrogen purge. As the solid fraction tends to zero, kA tends to the conductivity of the gas, the lowest possible for this type.

Radiation and shields

Radiation needs no matter. Between surfaces at T1 and T2, Q = Fe F1→2 σ A (T14 − T24), where Fe is the effective emissivity factor. Placing N thin, low-emissivity radiation shields in the gap cuts Fe:

1/FN = (1/e1 + 1/es − 1) + (N − 1)(2/es − 1) + (1/es + 1/e2 − 1)

For N = 0 it reduces to 1/F = 1/e1 + 1/e2 − 1. With e1 = e2 = 0.8 and shield emissivity es = 0.05, the lecture finds F = 0.667 for no shields and F10 = 0.00255 with ten. See the effect on a 300 K to 77 K wall:

Vacuum and residual gas

Even in vacuum a few gas molecules remain. When their mean free path λ exceeds the gap, they carry heat directly between the surfaces in free molecular conduction. A molecule that hits a surface leaves at a temperature between its own and the surface's; the accommodation coefficient a measures how fully it comes to the surface temperature. For two surfaces, Fa = 1/(1/a1 + 1/a2 − 1), and kinetic theory gives Q = G p A (T2 − T1), so the heat flow is proportional to pressure and the temperature difference, independent of the gap. Insulating vacuum is normally held between about 10−3 and 10−5 torr. In an evacuated powder, kA is directly proportional to pressure between about 15 torr and 10−3 torr; multilayer insulation reaches its lowest kA only once the pressure is down to about 10−5 torr.

Multilayer insulation (MLI)

MLI was first developed in 1951 and alternates highly reflecting foils (typically 6 µm aluminium) with low-conductivity spacers (glass fibre, nylon net) in a good vacuum. For an optimum job the vacuum should be about 7.5 × 10−5 torr, and performance depends on compressive load, number of shields, gas type and pressure, perforations and operating temperature. Layer bulk density follows from the foil and spacer masses per layer, and apparent conductivities of tens of µW/m·K are reported, far below any powder. Between layers heat crosses by radiation and by solid conduction through the spacers.

Comparison on a real vessel

The lecture compares insulations on a spherical LN2 vessel (inner 1.2 m, outer 1.6 m, 77 K to 300 K, emissivity 0.8):

InsulationHeat leak
Perlite at atmospheric pressure349.7 W
Vacuum alone (1.5 mPa)2648 W radiation + 0.356 W gas
Vacuum + 10 shields11.02 W
Evacuated fine perlite12.7 W
50/50 copper–Santocel (opacified)4.41 W

Opacified powder is best. With latent heat 200 kJ/kg and liquid density 807 kg/m³, 4.41 W boils off 0.098 L/h of nitrogen, 2.36 L per day. The same 4.41 W would vaporise about 151 L of helium per day (20.2 kJ/kg, 124.8 kg/m³; 1 L/h of helium is about 0.7 W). That is why the lecture says insulations for 77 K down to 4 K need to be better.
