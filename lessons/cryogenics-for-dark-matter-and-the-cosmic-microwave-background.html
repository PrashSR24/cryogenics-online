Module 12 · Science applications

Cryogenics for dark matter and the cosmic microwave background

Thermometers that sit on the edge of a superconducting transition, nested cans at 15 millikelvin, and detectors counting heat from single particles.

Why cryogenics reaches the edge of physics

The sky and the lab need the same trick: heat from a single particle or a few photons is tiny, so make the detector so cold that its own thermal noise is lower still. Two science cases from the lectures show the engineering.

Dark matter: waiting for a few events a year

Galaxy rotation curves stay flat far beyond the visible disc; colliding galaxy clusters (the Bullet Cluster) show mass, inferred from gravitational lensing, passing straight through the hot gas; and simulations show that structure forms in the right way only if most matter is invisible. A favoured candidate is a weakly interacting massive particle (WIMP) that scatters off a nucleus like a billiard ball, depositing energy as charge, light or heat. Expected rates are a few events per year in current experiments, so the design must: detect very small energies, reject backgrounds, and have large mass and patience.

  • Go deep. Cosmic-ray muon flux falls by orders of magnitude with depth: the Soudan mine is about half a mile down, SNOLAB in Ontario 1.5 miles. The whole SNOLAB laboratory is a clean room, because dust carries radioactivity.
  • Layered shielding. The CDMS shield used, from outside in: plastic-scintillator muon veto, 40 cm polyethylene, 18 cm lead, 4.5 cm ancient lead, and 10 cm polyethylene; the cryostat adds about 1.9 cm of copper.
  • Cryogenic detectors. Germanium and silicon crystals (76 mm diameter, 25 mm thick) are cooled so that a particle recoil releases both electric charge and crystal vibrations (phonons, i.e. heat). Aluminium fins 300 nm thick absorb the phonons; the energy passes as quasiparticles into tungsten transition-edge sensors (TES), read out by SQUIDs. CDMS ran at about 50 mK at Soudan from 2003 to 2015; SuperCDMS SNOLAB, planned for 2020 to 2030, runs at about 15 mK.

Other cryogenic dark-matter experiments use liquid xenon (162 K), liquid argon (85 K), bubble chambers, silicon CCDs (140 K), scintillating crystals and an RF-cavity axion search at 130 mK.

Getting to 15 millikelvin

A home refrigerator condenses a gas and expands it to carry heat away. A dilution refrigerator works on the same principle, but with a mixture of the two helium isotopes, and reaches about 0.01 K. In the experiments here, large copper cans, each colder than the last, nest one inside another and hang from the next outer can on Kevlar ropes, which have little conduction. Look at the stack:

The 50 K and 4 K stages are cooled by Gifford–McMahon cryocoolers; the 1 K still, 250 mK cold plate and 15 to 30 mK mixing chamber belong to the dilution refrigerator. Every layer is suspended from the next outer layer on Kevlar ropes; all materials must be radio-pure.

The cryogenic challenges named in the lectures: wet fridges need daily helium and nitrogen refills underground, which led to recapture and recondensation; impurities in the circulating mixture must be trapped in cold traps that need regular cleaning; long stems and many joints make it hard to hold detectors below 50 mK, and joint conduction degrades with air exposure unless gold-plated; thermal contraction can make cans touch during cooldown; and pumps and mechanical coolers shake the detectors. CDMS at Soudan ran for 12 years, once for 1.5 years continuously at base temperature.

The cosmic microwave background

The microwave background is light from about 400,000 years after the Big Bang, and holds more than 90% of the energy in light in the universe; COBE found it smooth to one part in 100,000 and a near-perfect 3 K blackbody. Mapping its faint temperature and polarisation patterns tells us about inflation, neutrinos and dark energy. The South Pole Telescope sits at the South Pole because its winter water vapour is about four times lower than at Chile's Atacama plateau (and six times lower than at Mauna Kea), and the sky is roughly 30 times more stable than in Chile during the six-month night. Progress has come from detector technology: 16 detectors in 2001, 960 in 2007, 1,600 in 2012 and about 16,200 in the SPT-3G camera of 2017, with the proposed CMB-S4 reaching 500,000.

The detector is a bolometer: an absorber of heat capacity C heated by incoming power Popt, joined to a cold bath by a weak thermal link G, with a thermistor reading its temperature. The temperature rise is Popt/G, and the time constant is C/G. The design goal is thermal noise below photon noise. The first bolometers used a hand-made bismuth absorber on sapphire, cooled to 300 mK; modern ones use a superconducting transition-edge sensor, a metal bilayer (for example Al/Ti or Mo/Au) 30 to 100 nm thick whose superconducting transition, near 500 mK, is only about 10 mK wide. Biased in the middle of the transition, a tiny temperature rise makes a big change in resistance. Try it:

Loop gain is the key advantage over germanium thermistors (loop gain about 1 to 5, a resistance of megaohms and a high sensitivity to microphonics): a TES has loop gain of about 20 to 1000, a resistance near an ohm (so low microphonics), and can be fabricated directly on the bolometer. Currents are read by SQUIDs, with 0.2 nV/√Hz of voltage noise, and many detectors share one line by frequency-domain multiplexing: each detector sits at the resonance of its own LC filter in the 300 to 1000 kHz range, and the LC quality factor keeps crosstalk below 1%. In SPT-3G, each pixel has an antenna in the centre that sends power to six TES bolometers, measuring three colours (95, 150, 220 GHz) and two polarisations, all cooled to about 0.3 K inside a camera of roughly one tonne.
