Thermoacoustic oscillations in cryogenic systems

Why a quiet instrumentation tube can sing, shake an accelerator cavity and leak 1000 times more heat than conduction.

Heat and sound talk to each other

Thermoacoustics is the interaction of heat and sound in a fluid. Temperature gradients in a compressible gas can make sound waves, and sound waves can make temperature gradients. This is always going on, but we rarely notice: a normal conversation (about 60 dB) produces temperature swings of only about 10⁻⁴ K. In a cryogenic system, however, a narrow gas-filled tube running from room temperature to 4 K can turn this tiny effect into large, self-sustaining pressure oscillations: thermoacoustic oscillations (TAOs).

Three ingredients are needed: a compressible gas in a narrow tube with a closed end, a large temperature difference along it, and a gas whose thermal diffusivity is much larger than its viscous diffusivity. Helium between 300 K and 4 K ticks all the boxes. They turn up in instrumentation lines, control valves and valve stems, wherever a stagnant gas column bridges warm and cold.

The mechanism, in two pictures

Simplified: gas at the cold end warms as it moves toward the warm end, expands and its pressure rises; that pressure pushes gas back toward the cold end, which drops the pressure at the warm end; gas then flows back into the lower-pressure space, and the cycle repeats. Under the right tube size and temperature gradient the oscillation grows and sustains itself.

As a mass and a spring: at low temperature the gas is dense but has little viscosity, so the cold gas behaves like a heavy slug of mass. The gas in the warm region acts as a spring (compressible). A mass on a spring that is pumped in step with its motion will oscillate; here the heat does the pumping. The heat flow is the nasty part: the oscillating gas shuttles heat from the warm wall to the cold end, much more than steady conduction through the tube wall would carry.

The frequency estimate treats the tube as a quarter-wave resonator (closed at the warm end, open at the cold end) and integrates the sound travel time along the linear temperature profile. It uses the ideal-gas sound speed of helium and is only an estimate, but it lands in the tens to hundreds of hertz range that is seen in practice.

A short history of the theory

  • 1863: Helmholtz calculates the damping of sound in long tubes from wall friction.
  • 1868: Kirchhoff adds heat conduction to the wall.
  • 1969 to 1973: Nikolaus Rott builds the linear theory of thermoacoustics for gas in a channel with an axial temperature gradient. His analysis applies when the channel width is of the order of the gas's thermal penetration depth (about 1 mm for helium), which is much shorter than the sound wavelength (about 1 m). Time-averaged products of the oscillating quantities give the engineering results: heat flux goes with the product of temperature and velocity, work with pressure and velocity, mass flux with density and velocity. Swift's Thermoacoustic Engines (1988) gives the rigorous derivation.

When do TAOs appear?

Not always. There are two stable regions, and TAOs live between them:

  • If the tube is too narrow, viscous friction on the walls damps the oscillation before it can build.
  • If the tube is too wide, heat transfer from the wall into the bulk of the gas is too weak to drive it.

Stability maps use the temperature ratio α = Thot/Tcold, a length ratio ξ = (L − l)/l (L the total length and l the distance from the cold end to where the temperature equals the average of hot and cold) and a dimensionless diameter group that compares the tube radius with the viscous penetration depth in the cold gas (d/2 times the square root of the cold sound speed over the product of the cold length and kinematic viscosity). The slide equations did not extract cleanly, so they are described here in words. Findings from the maps: a longer tube needs a larger minimum diameter to avoid TAOs, and a colder warm end lowers the minimum diameter, so an 80 K thermal intercept helps. Rott's theory says TAOs cannot exist when the warm-to-cold temperature ratio is below about 6.

Signs of trouble

  • Heat loads up to about 1000 times conduction.
  • Ice balls at the warm end of an oscillating line or valve stem.
  • Noisy or disturbed temperature and pressure readings.
  • Mechanical vibration.

A case study: a superconducting cavity cryomodule

An accelerator cryomodule had to hold the 1.3 GHz resonance of its cavities to within 10 Hz of peak detuning (equivalent to about 3 nm of cavity compression), and a static heat load at 2 K under 9.5 W. When first cold, it showed about 102 Hz average peak detuning and a 42 W static load. The detuning spectrum was dynamic and wandered, which suggested a cryogenic source. Ice balls were found on the valves and instrumentation lines. Varying the cryogenic conditions revealed that at a subcritical supply pressure the vibration improved by about a factor of 10 and the flow fell from 4.7 to 1.75 g/s, pointing to TAOs in the valve stems. A theory for why subcritical pressure stops them is not settled; one idea is that the stem changes from a dense supercritical fluid (128 kg/m³ at 5 K) to a low-density vapour (39 kg/m³) that cannot sustain the oscillation.

Cures

  • Reduce the driving force: reduce the temperature gradient, make the warm end colder, change the length ratio.
  • Add damping: enlarge the cross-section at the cold end and shrink it at the warm end.
  • Convection brakes (wipers): a barrier that closes the annular space in a valve stem, placed where the local temperature ratio is about 2 to 3 so Rott's criterion is satisfied. A single wiper confined the TAOs above it, so the heat load fell from 42 W to 11 W but vibration remained; several wipers or reversing the valve were needed.
  • A second oscillator: a damping volume (300 to 1000 mL) connected through a metering valve; adjust it while watching a pressure trace until the oscillation vanishes. If its impedance matches the tube's acoustic impedance, the wave is absorbed. It cannot be used where there is no warm connection.
  • Design rules: avoid closed lines, remove the helium fill line after transfer, use check valves and buffer volumes with restrictive elements (porous fillers, orifices), fill the tube cross-section with wires, keep a small gas flow, or add a vent hole partway up.
