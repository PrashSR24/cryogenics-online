Module 9 · Instrumentation

Measuring flow, pressure and liquid level

How to put a number on cold flow and level with orifices, Coriolis meters, superconducting wires and a simple pressure difference.

Pressure: measure it warm if you can

Three kinds of reading are common: gauge (relative to the atmosphere), absolute and differential. Most use a thin diaphragm whose flex is sensed capacitively, by strain gauges, or by variable reluctance: a magnetically permeable steel diaphragm sits between two inductive coils, and its displacement unbalances an AC bridge that is converted to a DC voltage. Sensors are usually kept at room temperature, joined to the cold process by a capillary running up through the cryostat. That tube must be leak-tight, its mean free path must be checked for low-pressure use, and, as the thermoacoustic lesson shows, a closed helium capillary can sing, so its size, length and routing matter.

For low and medium vacuum, direct gauges (capacitance manometers, diaphragms) measure force per area and do not care what gas is present. From high vacuum down, only indirect gauges work, which measure a gas property. A Pirani gauge uses the thermal conductivity of the gas (medium and high vacuum); hot-cathode and cold-cathode (Penning) gauges ionise the gas and measure the current (high to ultra-high). Indirect gauges read as if the gas were nitrogen, so other gases need a correction. Ionisation gauges do not like medium vacuum: electrodes burn out and leave soot. Wide-range gauges switch between methods at certain pressures, which can show up as a misleading step in the reading.

Flow meters

  • Orifice plate. A plate with a hole between two flanges makes a pressure drop that grows with the flow. Pressure taps upstream and at the vena contracta (the point of lowest pressure) feed a differential-pressure transmitter, often with a 4 to 20 mA output. Cheap and compact, but 2 to 5% accuracy and a high permanent pressure loss; discharge coefficient about 0.6.
  • Venturi. The same principle with a smoothly narrowing throat: much lower loss, accuracy about 0.5% and a discharge coefficient near 1, but dearer and harder to install.
  • Thermal (mass) meter. A small heater warms one of two resistance sensors; the flow cools it, and the electrical power needed to hold a fixed temperature difference gives mass flow. Fast, good at low flow, about ±1%, but for gas only.
  • Coriolis. A tube is vibrated at resonance; flowing fluid twists it, and the phase shift between inlet and outlet sensors is proportional to mass flow. Tests at CERN and in Italy covered 0.5 g/s to 1.2 kg/s and temperatures down to 1.7 K. Accuracy can be as good as 0.25%, repeatability 0.1%, independent of fluid properties, no individual cryogenic calibration, remote electronics (radiation tolerant), a leak-tight secondary containment. The cold elastic modulus (about 207.5 GPa below 20 K versus 194.6 GPa at 20 °C) needs a correction factor of 1.066. Downsides: size, pressure drop and price.
  • Vortex. A bluff body sheds vortices at a frequency f = St V / d (St is the Strouhal number, d the body width, V the speed). Independent of fluid properties, 1:10 turn-down, little loss, but needs turbulent flow, straight pipe, is sensitive to vibration, and has about 2% accuracy for gases. Used for warm low-pressure gas return.
  • Turbine. A freely spinning rotor whose pulse frequency is proportional to volume flow (f = kQ). High accuracy and a wide temperature range, suited to low flows, but gas readings need pressure and temperature corrections, moving parts must be lubricated, and it needs regular calibration.

For an incompressible fluid the standard relation is ṁ = Cd At √(2 ρ ΔP / (1 − β⁴)), where At is the throat area and β the throat-to-pipe diameter ratio. (The slide's equation did not extract cleanly; this is the standard form, with the lecture's Cd values.) Because flow goes as the square root of ΔP, a meter turned down to a tenth of its flow sees a hundredth of the signal: that is why orifice meters have limited range.

Liquid level

  • Superconducting wire. A heater drives the normal zone along a superconducting wire until it reaches the liquid surface, where better cooling stops it; the wire's resistance reads level. Calibrated in liquid helium at 4.2 K, it behaves differently in He II because heat transfer is so much better. High-temperature superconductor versions exist for liquid nitrogen.
  • Differential pressure. The pressure difference between the bottom and top of the vessel depends on the liquid and vapour columns: L = (ΔP/g − ρgas Ltotal) / (ρliquid − ρgas). Avoid liquid or gas traps in the sensing capillaries. For helium the sensitivity is about 1.06 Pa per mm of level.
  • Capacitive. The dielectric constant of the liquid changes the capacitance of a probe; mostly custom-made, needs in-situ calibration, works best with high-permittivity fluids such as liquid nitrogen.
  • Ultrasonic (sound speed and a resonance related to level) and weighing (mass of the vessel).

Rules of thumb

  • Do not buy more accuracy and precision than you need.
  • Use commercial sensors whenever possible; there are many.
  • Mount sensors outside the cryostat at 300 K when you can (pressure transducers, flow meters).
  • Use redundant sensors for critical devices inside the cryostat, and plan how to recalibrate them.
  • Avoid cold instrumentation feedthroughs if you possibly can.

Check your understanding

An orifice meter measures 10 L/min at 10 kPa. What differential pressure would it show at 1 L/min?
